## The 36-second budget

<Analogy title="Lap splits in a 2.4 km run">

In NAPFA's 2.4 km run you check your time at every lap. Sprint the first lap and you walk the last; dawdle and you cannot catch up. Nobody runs every metre at the same speed, but the lap times keep you honest.

</Analogy>

Ninety minutes for 150 questions works out to a simple average:

$$
t = \frac{T}{N}
$$

- $T$ is the total time in seconds.
- $N$ is the number of questions.
- $t$ is the average time you can afford per question.

<Steps title="The Prelim's time per question, without a calculator">
  <Step title="Minutes to seconds">
    $T = 90 \times 60 = 5400$ seconds.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Divide by the number of questions">
    $t = \dfrac{5400}{150}$. Cancel a zero from both: $\dfrac{540}{15}$. Then $15 \times 36 = 540$, so $t = 36$ seconds.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Turn it into checkpoints">
    Plan on about **30 seconds** for most questions. Then a quick rule tells you where you should be: **question number ÷ 2 = minutes gone**. Q30 at 15:00, Q60 at 30:00, Q90 at 45:00, Q120 at 60:00.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Save time for the slow ones">
    If the last 15 questions are long scenarios (as in 2026), give them about 60 s each: $135 \times 30 + 15 \times 60 = 4050 + 900 = 4950$ s $= 82.5$ minutes. That leaves **7.5 minutes** spare for flagged questions.
  </Step>
</Steps>

But 36 seconds is an **average**, not a rule for each question. Some questions take you 10 seconds; some you would not crack in 3 minutes. What matters is how you handle the slow ones. The simulator below sits one made-up Prelim twice with the same student: once working strictly in order, once with a time limit per question.

<KeyIdea>

36 seconds is an average you manage with checkpoints. **Cap the time** on any question that is not going anywhere: pick your best answer, flag it, move on, and come back if time is left. Capping protects the marks waiting for you at the end of the paper.

</KeyIdea>

Two rules turn that into a habit:

1. **The 10-second rule.** If after about 10 seconds you cannot see how to even start, cross out what you can, pick an answer, jot the question number on your scrap paper and move on.
2. **The 30-to-40-second cap.** If you can see the way but it is taking long, stop at around 30 to 40 seconds, pick your best answer, flag it and move on. Come back after you have reached the end.

<Mistake>

"I'm nearly there" is the most expensive thought in a timed paper. Two extra minutes on one question is four quick questions you never reach, and those were easier marks.

</Mistake>

## No negative marking: never leave a blank

<Analogy title="A free lucky-draw ticket">

Someone hands you a free lucky-draw ticket. It might win, it might not, but it costs nothing to drop it in the box. You would never throw it away unused. A blank answer is a lucky-draw ticket thrown in the bin.

</Analogy>

Every correct answer earns 1 mark and a wrong answer loses nothing. So what is a guess worth? Use the <Term def="The average result per try if you repeated the same situation many times: each outcome's value times its probability, added up.">expected value</Term>:

$$
E = 1 \times P(\text{right}) + 0 \times P(\text{wrong}) = \frac{1}{k}
$$

- $E$ is the average number of marks one guess earns.
- $k$ is the number of options still in play, so $P(\text{right}) = \frac{1}{k}$ for a pure guess among them.
- A wrong answer scores 0, so the second term vanishes.

With all four options in play, $E = \frac14$ of a mark. Cross out one option you are sure is wrong and $E = \frac13$; cross out two and $E = \frac12$. A blank is always worth exactly 0.

<Steps title="Twenty unanswered questions">
  <Step title="Blind guesses">
    $20 \times \frac14 = 5$ marks, on average, for 30 seconds of clicking.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Cross out one option in each first">
    $20 \times \frac13 \approx 6.7$ marks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="How much did eliminating one option help?">
    Each guess went from $\frac14$ to $\frac13$ of a mark: $\frac13 \div \frac14 = \frac43$, so every guess became **33% more valuable**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<GuessValue />

<Mistake>

Crossing out an option you are only *fairly* sure is wrong. If it was actually the right answer, that guess is now worth nothing. Eliminate only what you are certain about.

</Mistake>

## Reading a long question fast

The long scenario questions reward a reading trick: **read the question line first** (usually the last sentence), then hunt for the one detail that decides it, usually a number, a date or a comparison. Try it on this real question from the end of the 2026 paper. You have not learnt the words yet, so do not worry about that. Read the last sentence first, then look for the detail that is different between the data sets.

The question line asks for "the primary issue". The deciding detail is the **years**: training and validation data come from 2023, but the test data comes from 2025, and only the test score collapsed (92% down to 64%). Something changed **over time**, so the answer is the one about the data changing with time: *temporal distribution shift*. ## The Final: theory plus implementation

<Analogy title="A driving test">

The theory test gets you in, but you pass the driving test by actually driving. The Prelim checks what you know; the Final checks whether you can build it.

</Analogy>

The top 150 sit the Final on **{FINAL.dateLabel}**. {FINAL.format} {FINAL.lastYear} Results: {FINAL.results}

- **Section 1: 20 MCQs.** Harder than the Prelim. In 2026 most were scenarios with code: "you're debugging this function", "what does this training loop do wrong?". The paper suggested about 30 minutes for them.
- **Section 2: 3 programming tasks** in <Term def="A document that mixes text with cells of Python code you can run one at a time. Data scientists use Jupyter for experiments.">Jupyter notebooks</Term>. You implement a classic algorithm **from scratch** (writing the steps yourself instead of calling a library that does it all), using NumPy or PyTorch, inside a notebook that gives you the function names and some tests.

<FinalTaskMap />

<KeyIdea>

Every past Final task has been a well-known algorithm with a fixed recipe: gradient descent, K-means, retrieval, gradient boosting, IoU, attention. That makes the Final very preparable: practise implementing each one from scratch, without AI help, before February.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Budgeting a 3-hour Final (if 2027 matches 2026)">
  <Step title="MCQs first">
    Spend about 30 minutes on the 20 MCQs (1.5 minutes each) and bank those 20 marks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Minutes per mark">
    That leaves $180 - 30 = 150$ minutes for 80 marks: $\frac{150}{80} = \frac{15}{8} \approx 1.9$ minutes per mark.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Share it out">
    A 25-mark task gets about $25 \times 1.9 \approx 47$ minutes, a 20-mark task about 38, and a 35-mark task about 66. Check: $47 + 38 + 66 = 151 \approx 150$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Bank the easy parts first">
    Each task is split into sub-questions marked separately. A correct small function beats an ambitious broken one, so finish the core parts before any bonus part.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<ExamTip>

In 2026 there was no internet and no AI assistant during the Final, and you had to submit with the same email address you used for the Prelim. Keep every function name exactly as given (hidden tests call them by name), and run the provided test cells after each function.

</ExamTip>

<Mistake>

Practising your coding with an AI autocomplete switched on. It feels productive, but on the day it is gone. Practise the way you will be tested: plain Jupyter, your own memory of NumPy and PyTorch.

</Mistake>

<Takeaways>

- The Prelim is {PRELIM.questions} MCQs in {PRELIM.minutes} minutes, so $5400 \div 150 = 36$ s per question on average. Aim for about 30 s, check your pace with **question number ÷ 2 = minutes gone**, and keep spare time for long scenarios.
- **Cap the time** on stuck questions: guess, flag and come back. It reaches more questions and scores more than solving everything in order.
- No negative marking: a guess among $k$ options is worth $\frac1k$ of a mark on average and a blank is worth 0, so **never leave a blank**. Crossing out options you are sure about makes guesses worth more.
- The Final is 20 MCQs plus 3 Python programming tasks, implemented from scratch in Jupyter. Every past task is a classic algorithm you can practise in advance.

</Takeaways>
