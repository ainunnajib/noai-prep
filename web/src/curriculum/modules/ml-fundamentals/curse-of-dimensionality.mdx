More information should mean better predictions. If a model that knows a flat's floor area does well, surely a model that knows 50 facts about the flat does better? Often it does worse. Every feature you add makes the space your data lives in so much bigger that any dataset you could collect becomes a few lonely points in a vast emptiness. The mathematician Richard Bellman called this the **curse of dimensionality**. It explains why k-nearest neighbours breaks down, why models overfit when there are more features than examples, and why tools like PCA exist.

<Note title="What “dimension” means in this lesson">

Each **feature** is one **dimension**. A flat described by floor area and storey is a point in 2-dimensional space; a patient described by 100 blood measurements is a point in 100-dimensional space. We write $d$ for the number of features. Don't confuse this with NumPy's `ndim`: a table of 1,000 patients × 100 features is a 2-D array (`ndim` is 2) holding 1,000 points in 100-dimensional space.

</Note>

## Every feature multiplies the space

<Analogy title="Ten friends, one void deck">

Ten friends hanging out at a void deck bump into each other all the time. Scatter the same ten people across a 50-storey HDB block, each on a random floor in a random unit, and they almost never meet. Nobody left; there is simply far more room to spread out in. Adding features does the same thing to your data.

</Analogy>

Suppose you want at least one training example close to every possible input, so the model never has to guess blindly. Cut each feature's range into 10 equal slices and ask for one example in every cell:

- 1 feature: a line cut into 10 pieces, so **10** cells.
- 2 features: a square cut into a 10 × 10 grid, so **100** cells.
- 3 features: a cube cut into 10 × 10 × 10, so **1,000** cells.
- $d$ features: $10^d$ cells.

In general, with $k$ slices per feature and $d$ features,

$$
\text{number of cells} = \underbrace{k \times k \times \dots \times k}_{d \text{ times}} = k^d
$$

- $k$ is how finely you slice each feature (10 above).
- $d$ is the number of features.
- Each extra feature **multiplies** the count by $k$, so it grows <Term def="Growing by multiplying by the same factor at every step, like 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000. Much faster than adding the same amount each step.">exponentially</Term> in $d$.

<Steps title="Could all of Singapore fill a 10-feature space?">
  <Step title="Count the cells">
    10 features with 10 slices each give $10^{10}$ cells: ten billion.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count the data">
    Make every Singapore resident a data point: about 6 million people, or $6 \times 10^6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Best case">
    Even if no two people share a cell, at most $\dfrac{6 \times 10^6}{10^{10}} = 6 \times 10^{-4} = 0.0006$ of the cells contain anybody. That is 0.06%.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Conclusion">
    More than 99.9% of the cells are empty. With just 10 features, a whole country's worth of data is <Hl>sparse</Hl>: spread so thinly that most of the space has no examples at all.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## A neighbourhood that empties out

Now look from a single point. A new flat arrives and you want training examples similar to it: those inside a box around it that covers a share $s$ of each feature's range (for example $s = 0.5$, half the range). If the data is spread evenly,

$$
\text{share of the space inside the box} = s^d
$$

because a point must be inside the box in feature 1 **and** in feature 2 **and** … in feature $d$, and each "and" multiplies by another $s$. With $s = 0.5$: one feature gives $\tfrac12$, two give $\tfrac14$, three give $\tfrac18$, and ten give $\tfrac{1}{1024}$, about 0.1%. Out of 1,000 data points you would expect only about **one** neighbour.

<DimensionFill />

Set the box width to 0.5 and slide $d$ from 2 to 10. The scatter hardly changes, because a screen can only show two features at once, yet the neighbours disappear. The last readout shows how much data you would need to keep 10 neighbours on average: at $s = 0.5$ it **doubles** with every new feature, and with a smaller box it grows even faster.

<KeyIdea>

A neighbourhood covering a share $s$ of each feature holds only $s^d$ of the space. Each new feature multiplies the data you need to keep the same number of neighbours, so with a fixed dataset, **high-dimensional space is mostly empty**.

</KeyIdea>

The flip side is just as strange. To catch 10% of the data, the box must have side $0.1^{1/d}$ (the number which, multiplied by itself $d$ times, gives 0.1):

| features $d$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 10 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| side of the box | 0.10 | 0.32 | 0.46 | 0.79 | 0.98 |

With 10 features, the box holding your 10% "most similar" examples must stretch across 79% of every feature's range. Those examples are not really near the new point at all.

Why does sparsity hurt learning? With few examples near any new input, a model must fill the gaps by guessing, and a flexible model will happily fill them with patterns that are really just noise. That is **overfitting**, and you will watch it happen later in this lesson.

## Distances stop telling points apart

Many methods are built on distance. The simplest is <Term def="k-nearest neighbours: to predict for a new point, find the k training points closest to it and take a majority vote of their labels (or their average, for numbers).">k-nearest neighbours (k-NN)</Term>: find the $k$ training points closest to the new point and copy their most common label. Closeness is usually the <Term def="The straight-line distance between two points: square the difference in each feature, add the squares, take the square root.">Euclidean distance</Term>:

$$
\text{dist}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q}) = \sqrt{(x_1 - q_1)^2 + (x_2 - q_2)^2 + \dots + (x_d - q_d)^2}
$$

- $\mathbf{q}$ is the new (query) point and $\mathbf{x}$ is a training point; each is a list of $d$ feature values.
- $x_j - q_j$ is how much they differ in feature $j$; squaring makes every difference count as positive.
- Every feature adds one more squared term to the sum, and all terms count **equally**.

<Analogy title="Equally far hawker stalls">

If every stall in the hawker centre were exactly 50 m from your table, "go to the nearest stall" would be useless advice. In high dimensions, distances behave like that.

</Analogy>

Here is why. The squared distance is a sum of $d$ small random pieces, one per feature. For features spread evenly between 0 and 1, each piece is $\tfrac16$ on average, so the sum is about $\tfrac{d}{6}$ and the typical distance is about $\sqrt{d/6}$. But adding up many random pieces also **evens them out**, just as your average over 100 quizzes is steadier than your score on one quiz. However big $d$ gets, the distances wobble by only about $\pm 0.2$ around the typical value:

- $d = 6$: distances are about $1 \pm 0.2$, a big spread compared with the distance itself.
- $d = 600$: distances are about $10 \pm 0.2$, so almost every point is between 9.5 and 10.5 away.

The typical distance grows, but the spread does not. Relative to how far away things are, every point ends up about equally far.

<DistanceConcentration />

In symbols: if $D_{\min}$ and $D_{\max}$ are the distances from the query to its nearest and farthest points, then for many independent features

$$
\frac{D_{\min}}{D_{\max}} \to 1 \quad \text{as } d \to \infty,
$$

which reads "as the number of features grows, nearest divided by farthest gets closer and closer to 1". This is called <Term def="In high dimensions the distances from a point to all the other points become nearly equal, so 'near' and 'far' lose their meaning.">distance concentration</Term>.

<KeyIdea>

In high dimensions the nearest and farthest points are almost equally far away, so "nearest neighbour" carries little information. Methods built on distance, such as k-NN and k-means, suffer first.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="“Extra features can't hurt: the model will just ignore the useless ones”">

k-NN cannot ignore anything: every feature adds its own term to the distance, with equal weight, so 100 noise features drown out 2 useful ones. Models with weights *can* learn to ignore a feature, but only if there is enough data to tell signal from noise, and in high dimensions that is exactly what runs out.

</Mistake>

<Tip>

Distance-based methods also need features on the same scale. If one feature is monthly income in dollars (around 5,000) and another is age in years (around 40), income dominates every distance. <Term def="Rescaling a feature so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1: subtract the mean, then divide by the standard deviation.">Standardise</Term> the features first.

</Tip>

## More features than examples: p > n

Statisticians write $p$ for the number of features and $n$ for the number of training examples. A linear model

$$
\hat y = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_p x_p + b
$$

has one weight $w_j$ per feature plus a bias $b$: that is $p + 1$ <Term def="Numbers the model learns from the training data, such as the weights and bias of a linear model.">parameters</Term> to learn from $n$ examples. Watch what happens when there are as many parameters as examples.

<Steps title="Two examples, one feature: a perfect fit for anything">
  <Step title="The data">
    Two examples: $(x, y) = (1, 3)$ and $(2, 5)$. One feature, so the model is $\hat y = wx + b$: 2 parameters for 2 examples.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fit it exactly">
    The line must pass through both points: $w + b = 3$ and $2w + b = 5$. Subtracting the first from the second gives $w = 2$, then $b = 1$. The line $\hat y = 2x + 1$ has training error 0.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Now make the labels nonsense">
    Replace the labels with random numbers: $(1, 7)$ and $(2, -4)$. Now $w + b = 7$ and $2w + b = -4$, so $w = -11$ and $b = 18$. Training error is 0 again, although there was no pattern to learn.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The general rule">
    Two points can always be fitted by a line, three points by a flat plane using 2 features, and $n$ examples (almost always) by a linear model with $n - 1$ features plus a bias. Once parameters $\ge$ examples, a perfect training score is guaranteed, so it tells you nothing.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<FeaturesVsSamples />

In the visual, training error drops to exactly 0 once $p + 1$ reaches $n = 10$, while the error on new patients shoots up: the model has memorised the noise in its 10 training patients instead of learning the one real pattern. (Push $p$ further and the error on new patients can drift down again, which researchers call *double descent*, but it stays worse than the honest one-feature model.) This is the over-flexible, high-variance behaviour from the lesson on generalisation, and high dimensions make it very easy to fall into.

<KeyIdea>

When the number of features $p$ is close to or larger than the number of examples $n$, a model can fit the training data perfectly, noise and all. The signature is a near-perfect training score with a much worse validation score: overfitting.

</KeyIdea>

## Lifting the curse

- **Feature selection**: keep only features that matter, using domain knowledge or <Term def="A penalty on the sum of the absolute values of the weights. It pushes the weights of useless features to exactly 0 (also called Lasso).">L1 regularisation</Term>, which sets the weights of useless features to exactly 0.
- **Dimensionality reduction**: replace many related features with a few new ones that keep most of the information, for example with <Term def="Principal component analysis: finds the few directions along which the data varies most and describes each point by its position along them.">PCA</Term>.
- **Regularisation**: penalise large weights (L1 or L2) so the model cannot twist itself through every training point.
- **More data**: helps, but the amount needed grows exponentially with $d$, so it is rarely enough on its own.
- **Scale your features** before using any distance-based method.

<Note title="Why machine learning works at all">

Real data is not spread evenly over its space. A 28 × 28 greyscale image has 784 pixels, so it is a point in 784-dimensional space, but almost every random 784-pixel image looks like TV static. Real photos of handwritten digits occupy a thin, highly structured sliver of that space, so the *effective* number of dimensions is far smaller than 784. PCA and neural networks work by finding and exploiting that structure.

</Note>

<ExamTip>

NOAI asks about the curse in two ways, and the arithmetic behind it is quick.

- **Definition**: look for "data becomes sparse", "distances become less meaningful", "need exponentially more data" and "risk of overfitting". The classic trap option is "more features always improve accuracy".
- **Scenario**: few samples, many features, near-perfect training score, much lower validation score. Diagnose overfitting caused by high dimensionality ($p$ close to or above $n$); fix it by cutting features (selection, PCA), adding L1/L2 regularisation or collecting more data.
- **Quick sums**: $s^d$ is mental maths for $s = 0.5$ or $0.1$. Remember $2^{10} = 1024 \approx 1000$, so $0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Each feature is a dimension. The cells needed to cover the space grow exponentially: $k^d$.
- A neighbourhood covering a share $s$ of every feature holds only $s^d$ of the space, so high-dimensional data is sparse.
- Distances concentrate: the nearest point is almost as far as the farthest, so k-NN and other distance-based methods degrade.
- With $p + 1 \ge n$ a linear model can fit even random labels perfectly: perfect training score, poor validation score.
- Fight back with feature selection, dimensionality reduction (PCA), regularisation, more data and feature scaling.

</Takeaways>
