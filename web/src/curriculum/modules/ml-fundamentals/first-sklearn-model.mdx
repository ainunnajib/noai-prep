A straight line, a k-nearest-neighbours vote and a decision tree work in very different ways, yet in **scikit-learn**, Python's standard library for classical machine learning, you use all three with the same few lines of code. This lesson gives you the words every ML question uses (features, labels, parameters, hyperparameters) and that recipe. Once you know it, you can try any classical model in a minute.

<Analogy title="A new property agent learning to price HDB flats">

Before she has seen any sales, the agent's guesses are wild. Then she studies hundreds of past sales. For each flat she looks at the **floor area, storey and town** (the inputs), and then at the **price it actually sold for** (the answer). Slowly she builds rules of thumb such as "every extra square metre adds about \$5,000". Nobody gave her that number: she **learned** it from the data. Machine learning has a name for each of these ideas. Let's meet them one at a time.

## Features and labels: the data

Here are six HDB resale flats, written as a table:

| floor_area (m²) | storey | town | resale_price (\$k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 67 | 4 | Bishan | 420 |
| 73 | 9 | Jurong West | 450 |
| 92 | 11 | Tampines | 530 |
| 95 | 8 | Ang Mo Kio | 560 |
| 110 | 15 | Sengkang | 610 |
| 122 | 7 | Punggol | 680 |

- Each row is one <Term def="One example in the dataset, such as one flat, one student or one email. Also called a sample, data point or observation.">sample</Term>: one flat.
- The columns we use as inputs are the <Term def="An input variable the model uses to make a prediction, such as floor area. Features are stored together in a table called X.">features</Term>: floor area, storey and town.
- The column we want to predict is the <Term def="The correct answer for a sample, which the model learns to predict, such as the resale price. Also called the target. Stored as y.">label</Term> (also called the **target**): the resale price.

By convention the features are stored in a table called $X$ (capital, because it is a whole table) and the labels in a list called $y$ (small, because it is a single column). With $n$ samples and $d$ features, $X$ has $n$ rows and $d$ columns, which we write as its **shape** $(n, d)$, and $y$ has $n$ entries. For the table above, $X$ has shape $(6, 3)$ and $y$ has 6 entries.

Learning from examples that come with their labels is called <Term def="Learning from examples that include the correct answer (the label), so the model can compare its predictions with the truth.">supervised learning</Term>. It comes in two flavours, depending on the label:

- **Regression**: the label is a number on a scale, such as a price, a temperature or a test score.
- **Classification**: the label is a category, such as pass or fail, spam or not spam, or the flat type (3-room, 4-room, 5-room).

## Parameters: what the model learns

A **model** is a function that turns the features of a sample into a prediction. The simplest model for price uses one feature:

$$
\hat{y} = w \times \text{area} + b
$$

- $\hat{y}$ ("y-hat") is the model's **predicted** price. The hat means "estimate", to keep it apart from $y$, the real price.
- $w$ is the **weight**: how many thousand dollars each extra square metre adds.
- $b$ is the **intercept** (or bias): a fixed amount added to every prediction.

The numbers $w$ and $b$ are the model's <Term def="A number inside the model that training learns from the data, such as a weight or an intercept. You never type its value yourself.">parameters</Term>. Different values give different lines, so different predictions. **Training** (scikit-learn calls it **fitting**) means choosing the parameters that make the predictions as close as possible to the real labels. "Close" is measured by a <Term def="A single number that measures how wrong the model's predictions are on the training data. Training tries to make it as small as possible.">loss</Term>. For regression the usual loss is the **mean squared error**:

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2
$$

Read it from the inside out. For each flat $i$, take the error $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ (real price minus predicted price). Square it, so that misses above and below the line both count as positive and big misses count a lot. Then add up ($\sum$) the squares for all $n$ flats and divide by $n$ to get the average. A perfect line has MSE 0.

Now try to do the training yourself.

<WhatFitDoes />

You can get close, but `fit()` finds the line with the lowest possible MSE, $w \approx 4.62$ and $b \approx 111$. Most models train this way: pick a loss, then search for the parameters that make it smallest. What the parameters look like depends on the model:

- **Linear models**: one weight per feature, plus an intercept.
- **Decision trees**: the yes/no questions and their split points, such as "is area ≤ 95?". A tree picks its splits greedily, one question at a time, each time choosing the split that best separates the labels.
- **Neural networks**: millions of weights.

One model you will meet below is the exception: **k-nearest neighbours** learns no parameters at all. Its `fit` just stores the training rows, and all the work happens when it predicts.

<KeyIdea>

**Parameters are learned.** For most models, training (fitting) means searching for the parameter values that make the loss on the training data as small as possible.

</KeyIdea>

## Hyperparameters: what you choose

Some settings cannot be learned this way, because they decide *how* the learning happens or *how flexible* the model may be. These are <Term def="A setting you choose before training, such as the learning rate, the number of neighbours k or a tree's maximum depth. Training does not change it.">hyperparameters</Term>. You meet many of them in this course:

- the **learning rate** and the number of **epochs** (passes over the data) in gradient descent;
- **k**, the number of neighbours that vote in k-nearest neighbours (`n_neighbors` in scikit-learn);
- the **maximum depth** of a decision tree (`max_depth`).

| | Parameter | Hyperparameter |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it? | the training algorithm, from the data | you, before training |
| When? | during `fit()` | when you create the model |
| Examples | weights $w$, intercept $b$, a tree's split points | learning rate, epochs, $k$, `max_depth` |
| In scikit-learn | read afterwards as `model.coef_`, `model.intercept_` | written in the brackets: `KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)` |

How do you choose a good hyperparameter, then? You try several values and compare how well each trained model does on data it has not seen. That is the subject of the next lesson, [Generalisation, overfitting and bias-variance](#/learn/ml-fundamentals/generalisation-and-overfitting).

## The scikit-learn recipe

<Analogy title="Same routine at every food court stall">

Chicken rice or mala, every food court stall runs the same routine. You order (and add your options, such as "less spicy" or "no ice"), the stall cooks, and you collect your food. Once you know the routine, any stall is easy. scikit-learn works the same way: **create** the model (with your options, the hyperparameters), **fit** it (the model does the work of learning) and **predict** (collect the answer).

</Analogy>

Here is the whole recipe for the HDB flats:

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

model = LinearRegression()          # 1. create (settings go in the brackets)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)         # 2. fit: learn the parameters
y_pred = model.predict(X_new)       # 3. predict labels for new rows
print(model.score(X_test, y_test))  # 4. score the predictions
```

`X_test` and `y_test` are labelled flats you kept aside: `fit` never sees them, so they show how the model does on new data ([Generalisation, overfitting and bias-variance](#/learn/ml-fundamentals/generalisation-and-overfitting) explains why this matters).

- `X_train` must be **two-dimensional**, like a table: a list of rows, one row per sample, one column per feature. Even with a single feature, each row is a list: `[[67], [73], [92]]`.
- `y_train` is one-dimensional: one label per row, `[420, 450, 530]`.
- `fit` returns the model itself. Learned parameters are stored on the model with names that **end in an underscore**, such as `coef_` and `intercept_`. The underscore is scikit-learn's way of saying "this exists only after fitting".
- `score` predicts the rows you give it and compares the predictions with the true labels. Regressors return <Term def="The coefficient of determination: 1 means perfect predictions, 0 means no better than always predicting the average label, and it can even be negative when the predictions are worse than always guessing the average.">R²</Term>, where 1 is perfect. Classifiers return **accuracy**, the fraction of correct predictions.

Step through the same four lines for four different tools. Switch the model and watch how little changes.

<SklearnRecipe />

The last tab shows the other kind of object in scikit-learn. A **transformer** such as `StandardScaler` does not predict anything; it rewrites the features. It follows the matching recipe:

- `fit(X)` learns what it needs from the training data (here the mean and standard deviation of each feature).
- `transform(X)` applies it to any data.
- `fit_transform(X)` does both in one call, and is meant for the training data only.

<KeyIdea>

Every scikit-learn model follows **create → fit → predict → score**. Hyperparameters go in when you **create** the model; parameters come out of **fit** (with names ending in `_`). Transformers use **fit → transform** instead.

</KeyIdea>

## Worked example: predict the output

NOAI questions often show a short program and ask what it prints, with no computer to check. Try this one before reading the steps.

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

model = LinearRegression()
model.fit([[1], [2], [3]], [5, 7, 9])
print(model.predict([[10]]))
```

<Steps title="Reading the code by hand">
  <Step title="Look at the data">
    The feature values are 1, 2, 3 and the labels are 5, 7, 9. Each step of 1 in $x$ adds 2 to $y$, and $5 = 2 \times 1 + 3$. So every point lies exactly on the line $y = 2x + 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What does fit learn?">
    `fit` finds the line with the smallest MSE. A line through every point has MSE 0, which cannot be beaten, so it learns `coef_` $= [2]$ and `intercept_` $= 3$. (Python may print the intercept as `3.000000000000001`: computers store decimals with tiny rounding errors.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="What does predict return?">
    $\hat{y} = 2 \times 10 + 3 = 23$. `predict` returns a NumPy array of floats, one per row, so it prints `[23.]`.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Trap: one pair of brackets">
    `model.predict([10])` crashes with `ValueError: Expected 2D array, got 1D array instead`. A single sample still needs to be a table with one row: `[[10]]`.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Mistake title="Three classic slips">

- **One pair of brackets.** `X` must be 2-D even for one feature or one sample: `[[100]]`, not `[100]`. With a NumPy array `x` of shape `(n,)`, write `x.reshape(-1, 1)`.
- **Predicting before fitting.** A freshly created model has no parameters, so `predict` raises `NotFittedError`.
- **Fitting on the test data.** Call `fit` (and `fit_transform`) on training data only. For test data use `predict`, `score` or `transform`, so that the test data stays unseen.

</Mistake>

## Where the recipe sits in a project

`fit` and `predict` are the middle of a longer journey. A real project first **collects** data, then **preprocesses** it (cleans missing values, encodes categories such as town into numbers, scales features), then **trains** models, **evaluates** them on data they have not seen and finally **deploys** the best one so people can use it. After deployment, monitoring often feeds new data back into collection.

<ExamTip>

How NOAI asks about this lesson:

- **"Which is a hyperparameter?"** Learned from data means parameter (weights, biases, split points, cluster centres). Chosen before training means hyperparameter (learning rate, epochs, batch size, $k$, `max_depth`, number of trees, regularisation strength).
- **"What does `fit` do?"** It learns the parameters from the training data: for most models by minimising a loss, and for k-NN simply by storing the data. It does not choose hyperparameters and does not evaluate the model.
- **Code reading.** Look for 1-D versus 2-D `X`, `fit_transform` on test data, and what `score` returns (R² for regressors, accuracy for classifiers).
- **Regression or classification?** Look at the label: a number on a scale means regression; a category means classification.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- **Features** ($X$, one row per sample) are the inputs; the **label** ($y$) is the answer to predict. A numeric label means regression; a category means classification.
- **Parameters** (weights, intercepts, split points) are learned by `fit`, usually by minimising a loss such as the MSE on the training data (k-NN's `fit` just stores the data).
- **Hyperparameters** (learning rate, $k$, `max_depth`, epochs) are chosen by you when you create the model.
- Every scikit-learn model follows **create → fit → predict → score**; transformers follow **fit → transform**. Learned attributes end in `_`.
- `X` is always 2-D: `[[100]]`, not `[100]`. Fit on training data only.

</Takeaways>
