A table of ten thousand numbers tells you almost nothing at a glance; one good picture of it tells you a lot. Data scientists plot **before** they model, to catch odd values, skewed columns and hidden patterns, and **while** they train, to watch a model learn. This lesson is about choosing the right plot for your question and drawing it with matplotlib and seaborn, the two plotting libraries named in the IOAI syllabus.

<Analogy title="Three weather pictures">

The weather service shows a rain map (where is it raining now?), a temperature line through the day (how is it changing?) and a bar chart of rainfall per month (which month was wettest?). Same weather, three questions, three different pictures. Choosing a plot works the same way: **the question decides the picture**.

</Analogy>

## Why look at the data at all?

Summary numbers such as the mean feel precise, but they can hide almost everything. In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe made four tiny datasets, now called <Term def="Four small datasets made by F. J. Anscombe in 1973 that share the same means, variances, correlation and best-fit line but look completely different when plotted.">Anscombe's quartet</Term>, that share the same mean, the same <Term def="A number from −1 to 1 that measures how closely two columns follow a straight-line trend. 1 means a perfect rising line, 0 means no straight-line trend.">correlation</Term> and the same best-fit line. Only a plot tells them apart.

<KeyIdea>

Always look at a plot before you trust a summary number or fit a model. A curve, an <Term def="A value far away from the rest of the data, such as one flat costing three times as much as the others.">outlier</Term> or one far-away point can change the whole story while the averages stay the same.

</KeyIdea>

## Pick the plot for the question

Five plots answer most questions you will ask about a table:

- A <Term def="A plot of how one numeric column is spread out. The range is cut into equal-width intervals called bins, and each bar's height counts the values in that bin.">histogram</Term> shows how **one numeric column** is spread out. Its range is cut into equal intervals called *bins*, and each bar counts the values in one bin.
- A <Term def="A plot with one dot per row, placing one numeric column across and another up, to show whether they are related.">scatter plot</Term> shows the **relationship between two numeric columns**: one dot per row.
- A **line plot** joins points in order, so it shows **change over time** (or over training steps).
- A **bar chart** compares **one number per category**, such as the average price in each town.
- A <Term def="A summary of a distribution: the box spans the middle half of the values (from the first to the third quartile), a line marks the median, whiskers reach the furthest normal values and dots mark outliers.">box plot</Term> compares the **spread** of a numeric column across categories and marks outliers.

All five thumbnails below are drawn from one new, bigger `df`: 144 HDB resale flats sold from 2017 to 2024, with `price` in thousands of dollars as in the last lesson. Try each question.

<PlotChooser />

Here is each plot as a matplotlib call and the seaborn call that draws the same picture:

- **Histogram**. matplotlib: `ax.hist(df['price'], bins=12)`. seaborn: `sns.histplot(data=df, x='price', bins=12)`.
- **Scatter**. matplotlib: `ax.scatter(df['area'], df['price'])`. seaborn: `sns.scatterplot(data=df, x='area', y='price')`.
- **Line**. matplotlib: `med = df.groupby('year')['price'].median()`, then `ax.plot(med.index, med.values)`. seaborn: `sns.lineplot(data=df, x='year', y='price', estimator='median')`.
- **Bar**. matplotlib: `avg = df.groupby('town')['price'].mean()`, then `ax.bar(avg.index, avg.values)`. seaborn: `sns.barplot(data=df, x='town', y='price')`.
- **Box**. matplotlib: `ax.boxplot(groups)`, where `groups` holds one list of prices per category. seaborn: `sns.boxplot(data=df, x='flat_type', y='price')`.

Notice the extra `groupby` line on the matplotlib side for lines and bars. With 18 flats per year, `ax.plot(df['year'], df['price'])` would zig-zag through every single flat, so you work out one value per year first. seaborn's `lineplot` and `barplot` do that averaging for you (the mean, unless you pass `estimator=`), and they also add a shaded band or error bars to show the uncertainty; pass `errorbar=None` to remove them.

<KeyIdea>

Relationship → scatter. Distribution → histogram (or box plot to compare groups). Change over time → line. Comparing categories → bar.

</KeyIdea>

### Reading a histogram

The shape of a histogram is the story. A single hump that is symmetric means most values are near the middle. A long tail on the right, like the HDB prices above, is called <Term def="A distribution with a long tail on the right: most values are small or medium and a few are very large. The large values pull the mean above the median.">right skew</Term>: a few very large values pull the **mean** above the **median**. The number of bins matters too. With too few bins every shape looks like a block; with too many, random noise looks like a pattern. You will try this with the bins slider further down.

### Reading a box plot

A box plot squeezes a whole column into five numbers. The <Term def="The values that cut sorted data into four equal parts. Q1 has a quarter of the values below it, Q2 is the median and Q3 has three quarters below it.">quartiles</Term> $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ mark where a quarter and three quarters of the sorted values lie, and

$$
\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1
$$

is the <Term def="Q3 − Q1: the width of the middle half of the data. It ignores the extreme quarter at each end, so outliers barely change it.">interquartile range</Term>, the width of the middle half of the data. A value counts as an outlier when it lies more than $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ beyond the box, that is below $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$. These two limits are called the *fences*.

<Steps title="Draw a box plot by hand: 9 bus waiting times (minutes)">
  <Step title="Sort, then median">
    The times are 2, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 20. With 9 values the median is the 5th: **6**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The quartiles">
    $Q_1 = 5$ (the 3rd value) and $Q_3 = 8$ (the 7th value), so the box runs from 5 to 8 with a line at 6. The IQR is $8 - 5 = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The fences">
    Lower fence: $5 - 1.5 \times 3 = 0.5$. Upper fence: $8 + 1.5 \times 3 = 12.5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Draw the whiskers">
    The whiskers stretch to the furthest values inside the fences: down to **2** and up to **9**. The 20 is beyond 12.5, so it is drawn as a separate dot: an outlier. Notice that the mean, $66 / 9 \approx 7.33$, is dragged above the median by that one bus.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Now move the buses yourself. The box, the fences and the outlier dots are recomputed from the quartiles every time a value changes.

wait = pd.Series([2, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 20])   # minutes
q1, med, q3 = wait.quantile([0.25, 0.5, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
low, high = q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr

print('Q1 =', q1, ' median =', med, ' Q3 =', q3, ' IQR =', iqr)
print('fences:', low, 'and', high)
print('outliers:', wait[(wait < low) | (wait > high)].tolist())
print('whiskers end at', wait[wait >= low].min(), 'and', wait[wait <= high].max())
print('mean =', round(wait.mean(), 2), ' median =', wait.median())`} />

</Note>

## Figures, axes and the plotting recipe

matplotlib draws on a <Term def="In matplotlib, the whole picture or window: the canvas that holds one or more Axes.">Figure</Term>: the whole canvas. Inside it sit one or more <Term def="In matplotlib, one individual plot inside a figure, with its own x-axis, y-axis, title and data. Not the same as an axis line: one Axes has two axis lines.">Axes</Term>, and each Axes is **one plot** with its own x-axis, y-axis, title and data. (The name is confusing: one *Axes* contains two *axis* lines.) Every matplotlib plot follows the same four-step recipe:

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, ax = plt.subplots()                  # 1. a figure holding one axes
ax.scatter(df['area'], df['price'])       # 2. ONE plotting call on the axes
ax.set_xlabel('floor area (m²)')          # 3. label the axes and add a title
ax.set_ylabel('price ($k)')
ax.set_title('Price against floor area')
plt.show()                                # 4. display it
```

Only step 2 changes between plot types: `ax.plot` for lines, `ax.scatter` for points, `ax.hist` for distributions, `ax.bar` for categories and `ax.boxplot` for box plots. If the plotting calls pass `label='…'`, then `ax.legend()` adds a key.

<Analogy title="Easel, paper, brush">

`plt.subplots()` sets up the easel (the figure) and clips sheets of paper onto it (the axes). The plotting call is the brush you choose for this drawing. The labels are the caption you write underneath so other people know what they are looking at.

</Analogy>

<PlotRecipeBuilder />

Several plots side by side are a grid of axes. `plt.subplots(nrows, ncols)` returns the figure and the axes together:

- `fig, ax = plt.subplots()` gives a single Axes, `ax`.
- `fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2)` gives two Axes side by side, unpacked into two names.
- `fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3)` gives a NumPy array of Axes with shape `(2, 3)`: 2 rows and 3 columns. You pick one by row and column, so `axes[0, 2]` is the top-right plot.
- With a single row or a single column the array is squeezed to 1-D: `fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3)` gives shape `(3,)`, so you write `axes[1]` for the middle plot, and `axes[0, 1]` raises an `IndexError`.

You will also see a shorter "pyplot" style in quick notebooks: `plt.plot(x, y)`, `plt.xlabel('…')`, `plt.title('…')`. These draw on whichever axes is current, which is fine for one plot but confusing for several. The two styles map one-to-one: `plt.xlabel` ↔ `ax.set_xlabel`, `plt.title` ↔ `ax.set_title`.

## seaborn: statistical plots in one line

<Term def="A Python library built on matplotlib that draws common statistical plots straight from a DataFrame and its column names, with labels and legends added for you.">seaborn</Term> (imported as `sns`) is built on top of matplotlib. You hand it the whole DataFrame and the **names** of the columns, and it does the grouping, colouring, axis labels and legend for you:

```python
import seaborn as sns

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
sns.boxplot(data=df, x='flat_type', y='price', hue='town', ax=ax)
ax.set_title('Price by flat type and town')
plt.show()
```

The `hue` argument splits the data by a category column and gives each group its own colour, with a legend. Here it draws three boxes (Bishan, Tampines, Woodlands) side by side for each flat type. In plain matplotlib you would loop over the groups yourself. Other seaborn calls you will meet:

- `sns.histplot(data=df, x='price', hue='flat_type')`: overlapping histograms, one colour per flat type.
- `sns.scatterplot(data=df, x='area', y='price', hue='town')`: dots coloured by town.
- `sns.countplot(data=df, x='town')`: a bar chart of how many rows each category has.
- `sns.heatmap(df.corr(numeric_only=True), annot=True)`: a grid coloured by the correlation between every pair of numeric columns, a favourite first look before modelling.

Because seaborn returns the matplotlib Axes it drew on (or draws on the one you pass with `ax=`), the usual `ax.set_title` and `ax.set_xlabel` calls still work afterwards. That is true of the "axes-level" functions used here. Figure-level ones that you will meet in tutorials, such as `sns.displot`, `sns.catplot` and `sns.pairplot`, make their own figure, do not take `ax=`, and return a grid object instead.

<Mistake title="The plot is right, the message is wrong">

- **No labels or units.** "price" could be dollars or thousands of dollars. Always label both axes, with units.
- **A line through categories.** Joining Bishan, Tampines and Woodlands with a line suggests an order and a trend that do not exist. Use bars.
- **A bar chart that does not start at zero.** Bar heights are compared by eye, so cutting off the bottom makes small differences look huge.
- **Histogram vs bar chart.** A histogram's x-axis is a numeric scale cut into bins, so its bars touch. A bar chart's x-axis is a list of separate categories.

</Mistake>

## The line plot you will draw most: training curves

When a model trains, it repeatedly measures its <Term def="A single number that measures how wrong a model's predictions are. Training tries to make it as small as possible.">loss</Term>, a number for how wrong its predictions are, and nudges itself to lower it. One full pass through the training data is an <Term def="One complete pass through all of the training data during training.">epoch</Term>. People plot two losses against epoch: the **training loss**, on the data the model learns from, and the **validation loss**, on a <Term def="Data held back from training and used only to check how well the model does on examples it has not learned from.">validation set</Term> that the model never trains on. The shape of these two lines is one of the most common things NOAI asks you to diagnose.

<TrainingCurves />

When validation loss rises while training loss keeps falling, the model is <Term def="Fitting the training data so closely, including its noise, that performance on new data gets worse.">overfitting</Term>. A simple cure is <Term def="Stopping training at the epoch where the validation loss is lowest (or after it stops improving for a few epochs) and keeping that version of the model.">early stopping</Term>: keep the model from the epoch with the lowest validation loss.

Prelim questions on plotting are quick recall, so know the KeyIdea mapping cold, plus one extra: **correlations between many columns → heatmap**. Know the recipe names too: `plt.subplots` returns `(fig, ax)`, an **Axes** is one plot (not an axis line), and `plt.subplots(2, 3)` gives an axes array of shape `(2, 3)`, while `plt.subplots(1, 3)` gives shape `(3,)`. For box plots, the outlier fences are $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$: easy mental maths once you know them. For training curves: wild oscillation means the learning rate is too high, and a rising validation loss means overfitting.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Plot first: summary numbers can hide curves, outliers and single points that drive a trend (Anscombe's quartet).
- The question decides the plot: scatter for relationships, histogram or box plot for distributions, line for change over time, bar for categories.
- A box spans $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ with the median inside; values beyond $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ from the box are outliers.
- The matplotlib recipe is always `fig, ax = plt.subplots()`, one plotting call on `ax`, then labels and a title; `plt.subplots(r, c)` gives a grid of axes.
- seaborn draws statistical plots straight from a DataFrame and column names, and `hue` splits them by a category with a legend.

</Takeaways>
