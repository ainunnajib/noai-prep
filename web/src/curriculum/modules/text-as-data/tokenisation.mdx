Every language model, from a spam filter to ChatGPT, starts the same way: it chops the text into pieces and replaces each piece with a number. That first step, **tokenisation**, decides which words the model can read at all, how long its inputs are, and even why a chatbot can struggle to count the letters in "strawberry".

In [Encoding categories](#/learn/data-and-features/encoding-categories) you split sentences into words, gave every word an id and used `[UNK]` and `[PAD]`. Here we look at that pipeline properly.

<Analogy title="Packing a sentence into boxes">

You are shipping a sentence in boxes. **One box per word** means few boxes, but you need a box shape for every possible word, and an odd item like "shiok" has no box at all. **One box per letter**: 26 shapes cover everything, but you need a mountain of boxes. Or use **standard sizes** chosen for what you ship most often, and pack rare items in several smaller boxes. Modern tokenisers do exactly this.

</Analogy>

## Three ways to cut text

A <Term def="One piece of text that the model reads as a single unit, such as a word, part of a word or a single character. Each token is mapped to an integer id.">token</Term> is one piece the model reads as a unit. The <Term def="The fixed list of all tokens a tokeniser knows, each with its own integer id. It is built once from training text.">vocabulary</Term> is the fixed list of every token the tokeniser knows, built once from training text. A word the vocabulary does not contain is <Term def="Out of vocabulary: a word that never appeared in the text the vocabulary was built from, so it has no id of its own.">out of vocabulary (OOV)</Term>.

- **Word-level**: split on spaces and punctuation. Sequences are short and each token means something, but the vocabulary is huge (run, runs, running, ran are four entries) and every new word, typo, name or Singlish word becomes `[UNK]`. The model cannot tell "shiok" from "durian" if both are `[UNK]`.
- **Character-level**: one token per character. The vocabulary is tiny (about 100 symbols) and no *word* is ever unknown (only a symbol outside that alphabet, such as an emoji, can be), but sequences become about five times longer and a single letter carries almost no meaning.
- **Subword**: keep common words whole and split rare words into frequent pieces, such as "unbelievably" → `un` + `believ` + `ably`. Algorithms such as **BPE** and **WordPiece** learn the pieces from data.

To build a word-level vocabulary, reserve the first ids for special tokens (`<PAD>` = 0, `<UNK>` = 1, `<BOS>` = 2, `<EOS>` = 3; see below), then count the training words and keep the most frequent: `Counter(words).most_common(vocab_size - 4)`. Every other word maps to `<UNK>`. (BERT-style tokenisers write these in square brackets, `[PAD]` and `[UNK]`; others use angle brackets. They are the same tokens.)

<TokeniserCompare />

| | word-level | character-level | subword (BPE, WordPiece) |
|---|---|---|---|
| vocabulary size | very large (100k+) | tiny (~100) | chosen, typically 30k–100k |
| sequence length | shortest | longest | in between |
| unknown words | many `[UNK]` | none (only symbols outside the alphabet) | almost none |
| meaning per token | high | very low | medium |

<KeyIdea>

Subword tokenisation gives an **open vocabulary** (any word can be spelled from known pieces) with **manageable sequence lengths**, and related words share pieces (`run`, `run` + `ning`). That trade-off is why BERT (a model that reads text to understand it), GPT (a model that writes text) and almost every other modern model use it.

</KeyIdea>

Real sizes: BERT's WordPiece vocabulary has 30,522 tokens, and GPT-2's BPE vocabulary has 50,257. A useful rule of thumb for English with GPT-style tokenisers is that one token is about three-quarters of a word.

## How BPE learns its merges

<Term def="Byte-pair encoding: a subword tokeniser that starts from single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols in the training text into a new token.">Byte-pair encoding (BPE)</Term> is surprisingly simple. Think of how you invent shorthand while taking notes: the letter combinations you write most often get their own symbol first.

<Steps title="The BPE training algorithm">
  <Step title="Start from characters">
    Split every word of the training corpus into single characters. The starting vocabulary is the set of all characters.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count adjacent pairs">
    For every word, look at each pair of neighbouring symbols, and add the word's count to that pair's total.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Merge the winner">
    The most frequent pair becomes **one new token**. Add it to the vocabulary, write down the merge rule, and replace the pair everywhere in the corpus.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Repeat">
    Recount and merge again until the vocabulary reaches the size you chose. Vocabulary size $=$ number of starting characters $+$ number of merges.
  </Step>
</Steps>

To tokenise a **new** word, split it into characters and apply the saved merge rules **in the order they were learned**. Training counts, encoding just replays the rules.

<Steps title="Worked example: hug ×10, pug ×5, pun ×12, bun ×4, hugs ×5">
  <Step title="Count the pairs">

- $(u, g)$ is in hug, pug and hugs: $10 + 5 + 5 = 20$
- $(p, u)$ is in pug and pun: $5 + 12 = 17$
- $(u, n)$ is in pun and bun: $12 + 4 = 16$
- $(h, u)$ is in hug and hugs: $10 + 5 = 15$
- $(g, s) = 5$ and $(b, u) = 4$

</Step>
  <Step title="Merge 1: u + g → ug">
    20 is the largest count. The corpus becomes h·ug, p·ug, p·u·n, b·u·n, h·ug·s.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Merge 2: u + n → un">
    Recount: $(u, n) = 16$ now beats $(h, ug) = 15$ and $(p, u) = 12$ (the $p, u$ in pug was used up by merge 1).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Merge 3: h + ug → hug">
    $(h, ug) = 10 + 5 = 15$ is the top pair. The vocabulary is now b, g, h, n, p, s, u, ug, un, hug: 7 letters + 3 merges $=$ 10 tokens.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Tokenise new words">
    bugs → b · ug · s. hugs → hug · s. mug → m · ug, and since "m" was never in the training text it becomes `[UNK]` · ug.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The whole algorithm fits in a few lines of plain Python. Run it, then change the corpus or the number of merges.

<PyRunner code={`from collections import Counter

corpus = {"hug": 10, "pug": 5, "pun": 12, "bun": 4, "hugs": 5}
base = {c for w in corpus for c in w}   # the starting vocabulary
# start: every word split into characters
splits = {w: list(w) for w in corpus}
merges = []

def count_pairs():
    pairs = Counter()
    for w, freq in corpus.items():
        s = splits[w]
        for a, b in zip(s, s[1:]):   # adjacent pairs
            pairs[(a, b)] += freq
    return pairs

def merge(symbols, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(symbols):
        if i < len(symbols) - 1 and (symbols[i], symbols[i + 1]) == pair:
            out.append(symbols[i] + symbols[i + 1]); i += 2
        else:
            out.append(symbols[i]); i += 1
    return out

for step in range(3):
    pairs = count_pairs()
    best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)   # most frequent
    merges.append(best)
    for w in splits:
        splits[w] = merge(splits[w], best)
    print(f"merge {step + 1}: {best} x{pairs[best]} ->", splits)

def tokenise(word):
    # a character never seen in training is unknown
    symbols = [c if c in base else "[UNK]" for c in word]
    for pair in merges:   # replay rules in learned order
        symbols = merge(symbols, pair)
    return symbols

print(tokenise("bugs"), tokenise("hugs"), tokenise("mug"))`} />

Two relatives you will meet in exam options:

- **WordPiece** (BERT) also merges bottom-up, but picks the pair that is frequent *compared with how common its two parts are on their own* (a likelihood score), not the raw count. A piece that continues a word starts with `##`: "tokenisation" → `token` `##isation`.
- **Byte-level BPE** (GPT-2 onwards) starts from the 256 possible bytes instead of characters (a byte is a number from 0 to 255; every character is stored as 1 to 4 bytes). Every text, emoji and Chinese included, is made of bytes, so nothing is ever `[UNK]`. GPT-2's 50,257 tokens are 256 bytes, 50,000 merges and one special token.

<Mistake title="Thinking BPE understands meaning">

BPE knows nothing about grammar or meaning. It is a **greedy frequency count**: `ing` and `un` appear because they are common, not because anyone taught it prefixes and suffixes. Because those *are* frequent, the pieces often line up with them, which is why exam answers say subword tokenisation "captures morphology" (the parts words are built from). And a model struggles to count the r's in "strawberry" because it sees a couple of subword tokens, not ten letters.

</Mistake>

## Special tokens

Tokenisers reserve a few ids for **special tokens** that are not words but signals to the model:

| token | meaning | used by |
|---|---|---|
| <Tok>[PAD]</Tok> | filler to make sequences equal length | all |
| <Tok>[UNK]</Tok> | any token not in the vocabulary | word-level, WordPiece, character-level BPE (not byte-level BPE) |
| <Tok>[CLS]</Tok> | added at the **start**; its final vector summarises the whole sequence and feeds a classifier | BERT |
| <Tok>[SEP]</Tok> | **separates** two segments and marks the end | BERT |
| <Tok>[MASK]</Tok> | hides a word that BERT must predict during pre-training (its first, general training on huge amounts of text) | BERT |
| <Tok>&lt;BOS&gt;</Tok> | **beginning** of sequence | generative models |
| <Tok>&lt;EOS&gt;</Tok> | **end** of sequence; generation stops when the model outputs it | generative models |

A BERT input for a pair of sentences looks like `[CLS] the laksa was shiok [SEP] i want more [SEP]`. GPT-2 uses a single special token, `<|endoftext|>`, as its end (and start) marker.

## Padding, truncation and the attention mask

Models train on **batches**: several sequences processed together as one rectangular table (a tensor). Sentences have different lengths, so:

- <Term def="Adding filler tokens ([PAD]) to a sequence so that it reaches the required length.">Padding</Term> fills short sequences with `[PAD]` up to a common length: either a fixed `max_length` or the longest sequence in the batch.
- <Term def="Cutting a sequence that is longer than max_length down to max_length tokens.">Truncation</Term> cuts sequences longer than `max_length`.
- The <Term def="A 0/1 array with the same shape as the token ids: 1 marks a real token, 0 marks padding that the model must ignore.">attention mask</Term> has a 1 for every real token and a 0 for every pad, so the model ignores the padding.

<Analogy title="Reserved desks in the exam hall">

The exam hall is set out in full rows of desks, a neat rectangle. A row with fewer students gets a "RESERVED" card on each empty desk, and the invigilator's list says to skip those desks when collecting scripts. The cards are the `[PAD]` tokens; the list is the attention mask.

</Analogy>

<PaddingMaskGrid />

For a batch of $B$ sequences padded to length $T$, `input_ids` and `attention_mask` both have shape $(B, T)$. If sequence $i$ has $n_i$ real tokens, the number of pads is

$$
\text{pads} = B \times T - \sum_{i=1}^{B} n_i ,
$$

that is, the size of the rectangle minus the real tokens. The attention mask's row $i$ adds up to $n_i$. Padding usually goes on the **right**; text generators are often padded on the **left** when generating, so every row's last real token sits in the final column, where the next word is produced.

## End markers and labels: two classic bugs

Generative models also wrap every training sequence as `<BOS> … <EOS>`; the end marker is how the model learns to **stop**. For long sentences the order of operations matters. The length limit is called `max_len` here, as in the 2026 Final code (the tokenisers of Hugging Face, a popular library of ready-made models that you will use in [Using and fine-tuning models with Hugging Face](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/hugging-face-in-practice), call it `max_length`).

<EndMarkerTruncation />

<Mistake title="Truncating after adding the end marker">

`([BOS_ID] + ids + [EOS_ID])[:max_len]` cuts off `<EOS>` whenever the sentence is too long, and the model is trained on sentences that never end. Cut the **content** to `max_len − 2` first, then add `<BOS>` and `<EOS>`, so the markers always survive. Remember that special tokens count towards `max_len`: with `max_len = 10` and two markers, only 8 content tokens fit.

</Mistake>

The 2026 Final tested exactly this bug. Count carefully: with `max_len = 10`, an 8-word sentence fits exactly ($1 + 8 + 1 = 10$), so its `<EOS>` survives; the bug only shows with 9 or more words.

### Labels, and why padding gets −100

Training compares the model's guesses with the right answers. The right answer at a position is its **label**, and the **loss** is one number measuring how wrong the guesses are; training makes it smaller. In a text generator each position predicts the **next** token: for `<BOS> the bus came <EOS>` the pairs are `<BOS>` → the, the → bus, bus → came and came → `<EOS>`.

So the labels are the ids moved along one place. Hugging Face causal language models (GPT-style models in the `transformers` library) take labels = a copy of `input_ids` and do the shift inside. Computing the loss yourself in PyTorch, shift explicitly so position $t$ is scored against token $t + 1$: `F.cross_entropy(logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, V), ids[:, 1:].reshape(-1))`. Here `ids` has shape $(B, T)$ and `logits` has shape $(B, T, V)$: a score for each of the $V$ vocabulary tokens at every position. Without the shift the model just learns to copy its input.

<Steps title="Labels for the short row “ok lah” (max_len = 8)">
  <Step title="The ids">
    `[2, 11, 12, 3, 0, 0, 0, 0]`: `<BOS>`, ok, lah, `<EOS>` and four pads.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The labels">
    The same ids with every pad position set to −100: `[2, 11, 12, 3, −100, −100, −100, −100]`.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The loss">
    PyTorch's `cross_entropy` skips every −100 label (its default `ignore_index`), so the loss is averaged over the real predictions only: `<BOS>` → ok, ok → lah, lah → `<EOS>`.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Only the labels get −100. The **input** keeps pad id 0, because the model looks each input id up in its table of word vectors ([Word embeddings](#/learn/text-as-data/word-embeddings)) and there is no row −100.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

PAD, UNK, BOS, EOS = 0, 1, 2, 3
vocab = {"the": 4, "bus": 5, "came": 6, "late": 7, "so": 8,
         "i": 9, "ran": 10, "ok": 11, "lah": 12}

def encode(text, max_len):
    ids = [vocab.get(w, UNK) for w in text.lower().split()]
    ids = ids[: max_len - 2]      # 1. cut the content first
    ids = [BOS] + ids + [EOS]     # 2. then add the markers
    mask = [1] * len(ids)
    n_pad = max_len - len(ids)
    return ids + [PAD] * n_pad, mask + [0] * n_pad

batch = ["the bus came late so i ran", "ok lah", "the bus came"]
pairs = [encode(t, max_len=8) for t in batch]
input_ids = np.array([p[0] for p in pairs])
attention_mask = np.array([p[1] for p in pairs])
# labels (Hugging Face style): copy of the ids, -100 on padding
labels = np.where(attention_mask == 1, input_ids, -100)

print(input_ids)
print(attention_mask)
print(labels)
print("shape:", input_ids.shape, "| real tokens:", attention_mask.sum())`} />

The first sentence has 7 words, so with two markers it needs 9 slots: the content is cut to 6 words and `<EOS>` (id 3) still ends the row.

<Reveal label="Check it in PyTorch: −100 labels are ignored">

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
logits = torch.randn(5, 4)     # 5 positions, 4-token vocabulary
# the last 2 positions are padding, labelled -100
labels = torch.tensor([2, 1, 3, -100, -100])

# ignore_index=-100 is the default
print(F.cross_entropy(logits, labels))
# only the 3 real positions: the same loss
print(F.cross_entropy(logits[:3], labels[:3]))
# padding labelled with the pad id 0 instead
wrong = torch.tensor([2, 1, 3, 0, 0])
print(F.cross_entropy(logits, wrong))   # padding pollutes the loss
```

```text
tensor(2.1849)
tensor(2.1849)
tensor(1.8775)
```

The first two losses are equal, so the −100 positions were skipped. Labelling padding 0 instead changes the loss: the model is graded on padding.

</Reveal>

<ExamTip>

Five facts cover most NOAI text questions: subword tokenisation **handles unknown words with a moderate vocabulary**; BPE **merges the most frequent adjacent pair**; `[CLS]` is a **summary for classification**, `[SEP]` **separates/ends** segments; the attention mask is **1 for real tokens, 0 for padding**; −100 is a **label** value, not an input id. In Final code, count the special tokens in `max_len` and check whether truncation comes before or after `<EOS>`.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Word-level tokens are meaningful but hit unknown words; characters almost never do but make sequences long; **subwords** balance the two.
- **BPE** starts from characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair; new words are encoded by replaying the merges in order.
- `[CLS]` summarises (BERT classification), `[SEP]` separates, `[PAD]` fills, `[UNK]` replaces unknowns, `<BOS>`/`<EOS>` mark start and end.
- Batches are rectangles: pad short sequences, truncate long ones, and use the **attention mask** (1 real, 0 pad) so padding is ignored.
- Truncate the content **before** adding `<EOS>`, and label padding positions **−100** so the loss skips them.

</Takeaways>
