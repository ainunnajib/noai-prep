Is this email spam? Is this food review happy or angry? Should this customer message go to the refunds team or the kitchen? Each question takes a piece of text and gives back one label from a fixed list. This is <Term def="Giving each piece of text one label from a fixed set of classes (spam/not spam, positive/negative, topic A/B/C), learned from labelled examples.">text classification</Term>, one of the most common jobs in AI. Every text classifier, from a 1990s spam filter to a fine-tuned BERT, follows the same three-stage recipe, and you already know all the parts.

<Analogy title="Sorting letters at a post office">

A sorting centre does not read your letter. It reads the address (**tokenise**), turns it into a postcode (**vectorise**) and drops the letter into the bin for that postcode (**classify**). If the postcode is good, sorting is easy. A text classifier works the same way: the classifier at the end only sees a vector of numbers, so everything depends on how well that vector captures what the text means.

</Analogy>

## Three stages: tokenise, vectorise, classify

Our running example is <Term def="Deciding whether a piece of text expresses a positive or a negative opinion. It is a classic text-classification task.">sentiment analysis</Term> of hawker-centre reviews, with two classes: positive and negative.

1. **Tokenise.** Split the text into tokens, usually after lower-casing. Simple pipelines also drop <Term def="Very common words such as 'the', 'was' and 'and' that carry little meaning on their own. Some pipelines remove them before vectorising.">stop words</Term>. Tokens that are not in the vocabulary are ignored (or mapped to `[UNK]`).
2. **Vectorise.** Turn the tokens into **one vector of fixed length**. This is needed because a classifier such as logistic regression expects the same number of inputs every time, but reviews have different numbers of words.
3. **Classify.** Feed the vector to a classifier. Here it is logistic regression: a score $z = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$, squashed by the sigmoid into a probability $p = \sigma(z)$. Predict "positive" when $p \ge 0.5$.

The visual runs a real (tiny) model. Switch between two ways of doing stage 2: **TF-IDF**, from the bag-of-words lesson, and **averaged word embeddings**, from the embeddings lesson.

Look at the two vectors. The TF-IDF vector has one slot per vocabulary word, and almost all of them are zero: it is a <Term def="A vector in which most entries are zero. Bag-of-words and TF-IDF vectors are sparse because a review uses only a few of the thousands of words in the vocabulary.">sparse</Term> vector. The averaged embedding is <Term def="A vector in which (almost) every entry is a meaningful non-zero number, such as a 300-number word embedding.">dense</Term>: just 2 numbers here (300 or so in real models), all of them used. The average of the word vectors is

$$
\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \mathbf{e}_t
$$

where $n$ is the number of known tokens in the review and $\mathbf{e}_t$ is the embedding (vector) of token number $t$. In words: add up the word vectors and divide by how many there are.

<KeyIdea>

Every text classifier is a **vectoriser plus a classifier**: tokenise, turn the tokens into one fixed-length vector, then classify the vector. Most of the progress in NLP has come from better vectorisers; the classifier on top is often just logistic regression or a single linear layer.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="Throwing away the word 'not'">

Ready-made stop-word lists often contain "not", "no" and "never". scikit-learn's built-in English list does. For sentiment that deletes the most important word in "not good"! Check the list before you use it, or skip stop-word removal altogether: TF-IDF already gives very common words a low weight.

</Mistake>

## Inside a linear text classifier: one weight per word

Use the simplest vector of all: the **bag-of-words count vector** $\mathbf{x}$, where $x_j$ is how many times vocabulary word $j$ appears in the review. Logistic regression's score is then

$$
z = b + \sum_{j=1}^{V} w_j \, x_j
$$

- $V$ is the vocabulary size (64 words in our toy model, 10,000 or more in real ones).
- $x_j$ is the count of word $j$ in this review. Almost all counts are 0.
- $w_j$ is the learned **weight** of word $j$. A positive weight pushes towards the positive class; a negative weight pushes towards negative.
- $b$ is the **bias**: the score of a review with no known words at all.

Because every $x_j$ for a missing word is 0, the sum is simply **the bias plus the weights of the words that appear** (counted as many times as they appear). Each word casts a vote of fixed strength, wherever it sits in the sentence. That is why a linear model is <Term def="Easy for a person to inspect: you can see exactly why the model made a prediction. In a linear text classifier, each word's weight shows how it pushes the decision.">interpretable</Term>: you can read off exactly why it decided what it did.

### Where a bag of words breaks

The weights also show the model's blind spots:

- **Word order is thrown away.** *The food was good, not bad* and *the food was bad, not good* contain exactly the same words, so they get exactly the same vector and the same prediction (0.28 in our model). The **Shuffle** button proves it.
- **Negation and sarcasm** depend on how words combine, which a sum of separate votes cannot express. It is like judging a movie review by counting happy and sad words without ever noticing the word "not".
- **Small data learns accidents.** `mee` has a large positive weight only because 3 of our 4 training reviews containing it were positive.

A partial fix is to add <Term def="Sequences of n neighbouring tokens. Bigrams are word pairs such as 'not bad'; adding them as extra features lets a bag-of-words model see a little local order.">n-grams</Term>: word pairs such as "not bad" become extra vocabulary entries. That helps, but the vocabulary explodes and each pair needs its own training examples. The real fix is a model that reads words *in order*: a recurrent network (next lesson) or a Transformer.

<ExamTip>

Expect options that test whether you know **bag of words (and TF-IDF) ignores word order**. A quick test: if two sentences contain the same words in a different order, bag-of-words, TF-IDF and averaged embeddings all give them **identical** vectors. Be suspicious of options claiming that TF-IDF "understands negation" or "captures context".

</ExamTip>

## Three ways to build the vector

**1. TF-IDF + logistic regression**

- **Vector**: one slot per vocabulary word (thousands), mostly zeros.
- **Word order**: ignored.
- **"tasty" ≈ "delicious"?** No: they are unrelated slots.
- **Training**: seconds on a CPU.
- **Easy to inspect?** Yes, one weight per word.

**2. Averaged word embeddings + classifier**

- **Vector**: the mean of the word vectors (e.g. 300 numbers).
- **Word order**: ignored.
- **"tasty" ≈ "delicious"?** Yes: similar words have similar vectors.
- **Training**: seconds to minutes.
- **Easy to inspect?** Partly.

</div>
<div style={{ border: '2px solid var(--accent-line)', borderRadius: 12, padding: '0.1rem 0.9rem', background: 'var(--accent-soft)' }}>

**3. Fine-tuned pretrained encoder (e.g. BERT)**

- **Vector**: the encoder's output for the `[CLS]` token (768 numbers in BERT-base).
- **Word order**: used: every word is read in context.
- **"tasty" ≈ "delicious"?** Yes.
- **Training**: minutes to hours; a GPU helps.
- **Easy to inspect?** Hard.

The third approach needs a little more explanation. A pretrained encoder such as <Term def="Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers: a Transformer encoder pretrained on huge amounts of text by filling in masked-out words. It is fine-tuned for tasks such as classification.">BERT</Term> has already read billions of words. It puts a special `[CLS]` token at the start of every input, and its final vector for `[CLS]` summarises the whole sentence, word order included. To classify, you add a small **classification head** (one linear layer and a softmax) on top of that vector. Then you <Term def="Continuing to train a pretrained model on your own labelled data, usually with a small learning rate, so it adapts to your task without forgetting what it learned in pretraining.">fine-tune</Term>: train the head *and* all of BERT's weights on your labelled examples for a few epochs, with a small learning rate (around $2 \times 10^{-5}$) so the pretrained knowledge is nudged rather than wiped out. Reusing knowledge from one task for another like this is called <Term def="Reusing a model trained on one big task (such as predicting missing words in billions of sentences) as the starting point for a different, smaller task.">transfer learning</Term>.

The averaged-embedding model is short enough to write out in PyTorch. Notice the one line where word order disappears:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
vocab = {"the": 0, "food": 1, "was": 2, "good": 3, "not": 4, "bad": 5}

class AvgEmbeddingClassifier(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, dim, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, dim)   # one learnable vector per word
        self.fc = nn.Linear(dim, n_classes)        # the classifier

    def forward(self, ids):          # ids: (batch, seq_len)
        vecs = self.emb(ids)         # (batch, seq_len, dim)
        avg = vecs.mean(dim=1)       # (batch, dim)  <- word order is lost here
        return self.fc(avg)          # (batch, n_classes) logits

model = AvgEmbeddingClassifier(len(vocab), dim=8, n_classes=2)
a = torch.tensor([[0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]])   # the food was good not bad
b = torch.tensor([[0, 1, 2, 5, 4, 3]])   # the food was bad not good
print(model(a).shape)
print(model(a))
print(model(b))
print(torch.allclose(model(a), model(b)))
```

### Which approach should you choose?

The deciding factor is usually **how many labelled examples you have**:

- **Tens to a few hundred:** start with TF-IDF + logistic regression. It trains in seconds, it is hard to beat at this size, and it gives you a baseline that every fancier model must beat. Frozen pretrained embeddings plus logistic regression are another good choice here.
- **Thousands or more:** fine-tune a pretrained encoder. It usually gives the best accuracy because it starts out already knowing the language, and it reads word order.
- **Never** train a big network from scratch on a small labelled set. It has far more parameters than examples, so it memorises the training set (overfits) instead of learning sentiment.

## Evaluating: when accuracy lies

Real text datasets are usually <Term def="A dataset in which some classes have many more examples than others, for example 150 praise messages but only 10 food-safety reports.">imbalanced</Term>. Imagine a food-delivery app that sorts 200 messages into *praise* (150), *late delivery* (40) and *food safety* (10). The rare class is the one that matters most. Accuracy, the fraction of all messages labelled correctly, hardly notices the 10 food-safety messages, so we measure each class separately. For one class $c$, count the true positives (TP: correctly labelled $c$), false positives (FP: wrongly labelled $c$) and false negatives (FN: really $c$, but missed):

$$
\text{precision}_c = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall}_c = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad F1_c = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}
$$

<Term def="Of all the messages the model labelled as class c, the fraction that really are class c.">Precision</Term> asks "when the model says food safety, is it right?". <Term def="Of all the messages that really are class c, the fraction the model caught.">Recall</Term> asks "of the real food-safety messages, how many did it catch?". <Term def="The harmonic mean of precision and recall, 2PR/(P + R). It is high only when both are high.">F1</Term> combines them; the form $\frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}$ equals the harmonic mean $\frac{2PR}{P + R}$ and is faster by hand. Finally,

$$
\text{macro-F1} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{c=1}^{K} F1_c
$$

the plain average of the $K$ per-class F1 scores. Every class gets an equal vote, however small it is. (**Weighted-F1** averages by class size instead, so big classes dominate it again; for single-label problems **micro-F1** equals accuracy.)

<ExamTip>

For "which metric is misleading on imbalanced data?" the answer is **accuracy**: always predicting the majority class scores high while catching nothing rare. F1 is the **harmonic** mean of precision and recall (not the arithmetic mean). For many classes of different sizes, report **macro-F1**. With no calculator, use $F1 = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}$ straight from the confusion matrix.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Text classification = **tokenise → vectorise → classify**. The vector must have a fixed length, whatever the length of the text.
- In a linear bag-of-words model, $z = b + \sum_j w_j x_j$: each word adds its own fixed weight. That makes it easy to inspect, but blind to word order, negation and sarcasm.
- Bag of words, TF-IDF and averaged embeddings all ignore word order. Fine-tuning a pretrained encoder (BERT's `[CLS]` vector plus a linear head, small learning rate) reads words in context and is usually the most accurate when you have enough labelled data.
- With little labelled data, start with a TF-IDF + logistic regression baseline; never train a huge model from scratch on a small dataset.
- On imbalanced classes, accuracy can look great while the rare class is ignored. Use per-class precision, recall and F1, and **macro-F1** to give every class an equal vote.

</Takeaways>
