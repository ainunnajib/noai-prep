Each question takes a piece of text and returns one label from a fixed list: that is <Term def="Giving each piece of text one label from a fixed set of classes (spam/not spam, positive/negative, topic A/B/C), learned from labelled examples.">text classification</Term>, and every text classifier, from a 1990s spam filter to a fine-tuned BERT, follows the same three-stage recipe built from parts you already know.

<Analogy title="Sorting letters at a post office">

A sorting centre does not read your letter. It reads the address (**tokenise**), turns it into a postcode (**vectorise**) and drops the letter into the bin for that postcode (**classify**). A text classifier works the same way: the classifier at the end only sees a vector of numbers, so everything depends on how well that vector captures what the text means.

## Three stages: tokenise, vectorise, classify

Our running example is <Term def="Deciding whether a piece of text expresses a positive or a negative opinion. It is a classic text-classification task.">sentiment analysis</Term> of hawker-centre reviews, with two classes: positive and negative.

1. **Tokenise.** Split the text into tokens, usually after lower-casing. Simple pipelines also drop <Term def="Very common words such as 'the', 'was' and 'and' that carry little meaning on their own. Some pipelines remove them before vectorising.">stop words</Term>, and some **normalise** words: <Term def="Chopping word endings off by fixed rules, e.g. running → run, studies → studi. Fast, but the result is not always a real word.">stemming</Term> chops endings by rule (running → run, studies → studi), while <Term def="Mapping a word to its dictionary form using a vocabulary and grammar, e.g. ran → run, better → good.">lemmatisation</Term> maps a word to its dictionary form (ran → run, better → good). Tokens not in the vocabulary are ignored (or mapped to `[UNK]`).
2. **Vectorise.** Turn the tokens into **one vector of fixed length**, because a classifier such as logistic regression expects the same number of inputs every time, but reviews have different numbers of words.
3. **Classify.** Feed the vector to a classifier. Here it is logistic regression: a score $z = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b$, squashed by the sigmoid into a probability $p = \sigma(z)$. Predict "positive" when $p \ge 0.5$.

The visual runs a real (tiny) model. Switch between two ways of doing stage 2: **TF-IDF**, from the bag-of-words lesson, and **averaged word embeddings**, from the embeddings lesson.

Each figure in this lesson trains its own toy model, so exact probabilities differ between figures and code; the pattern is what matters. Look at the two vectors. The TF-IDF vector has one slot per vocabulary word, and almost all of them are zero: it is a <Term def="A vector in which most entries are zero. Bag-of-words and TF-IDF vectors are sparse because a review uses only a few of the thousands of words in the vocabulary.">sparse</Term> vector. The averaged embedding is <Term def="A vector in which (almost) every entry is a meaningful non-zero number, such as a 300-number word embedding.">dense</Term>: just 2 numbers here (300 or so in real models), all of them used. The average of the word vectors is

$$
\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \mathbf{e}_t
$$

where $n$ is the number of known tokens in the review and $\mathbf{e}_t$ is the embedding (vector) of token number $t$. In words: add up the word vectors and divide by how many there are.

<KeyIdea>

Every text classifier is a **vectoriser plus a classifier**: tokenise, turn the tokens into one fixed-length vector, then classify the vector. Most of the progress in NLP has come from better vectorisers; the classifier on top is often just logistic regression or a single linear layer.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="Throwing away the word 'not'">

Ready-made stop-word lists often contain "not", "no" and "never" (scikit-learn's English list does). For sentiment that deletes the most important word in "not good"! Check the list first, or skip stop-word removal: TF-IDF already gives very common words a low weight. (This lesson's toy models use a short hand-made list that keeps "not".)

</Mistake>

## Inside a linear text classifier: one weight per word

Use the simplest vector of all: the **bag-of-words count vector** $\mathbf{x}$, where $x_j$ is how many times vocabulary word $j$ appears in the review. Logistic regression's score is then

$$
z = b + \sum_{j=1}^{V} w_j \, x_j
$$

- $V$ is the vocabulary size (64 words in our toy model, 10,000 or more in real ones).
- $x_j$ is the count of word $j$ in this review. Almost all counts are 0.
- $w_j$ is the learned **weight** of word $j$. A positive weight pushes towards the positive class; a negative weight pushes towards negative.
- $b$ is the **bias**: the score of a review with no known words at all.

Because every $x_j$ for a missing word is 0, the sum is simply **the bias plus the weights of the words that appear** (counted as many times as they appear). Each word casts a vote of fixed strength, wherever it sits in the sentence. That is why a linear model is <Term def="Easy for a person to inspect: you can see exactly why the model made a prediction. In a linear text classifier, each word's weight shows how it pushes the decision.">interpretable</Term>: you can read off exactly why it decided what it did.

  </Step>

### Where a bag of words breaks

The weights also show the model's blind spots:

- **Word order is thrown away.** *The food was good, not bad* and *the food was bad, not good* contain exactly the same words, so they get exactly the same vector and the same prediction (0.28 in our model). The **Shuffle** button proves it.
- **Negation and sarcasm** depend on how words combine, which a sum of separate votes cannot express. It is like judging a movie review by counting happy and sad words without ever noticing the word "not".
- **Small data learns accidents.** `mee` has a large positive weight only because 3 of our 4 training reviews containing it were positive.

A partial fix is to add <Term def="Sequences of n neighbouring tokens. Bigrams are word pairs such as 'not bad'; adding them as extra features lets a bag-of-words model see a little local order.">n-grams</Term>: word pairs such as "not bad" become extra vocabulary entries. That helps, but the vocabulary explodes and each pair needs its own training examples. The real fix is a model that reads words *in order*: a recurrent network (next lesson) or a Transformer.

<ExamTip>

Expect options that test whether you know **bag of words (and TF-IDF) ignores word order**. A quick test: if two sentences contain the same words in a different order, bag-of-words, TF-IDF and averaged embeddings all give them **identical** vectors. Be suspicious of options claiming that TF-IDF "understands negation" or "captures context".

</ExamTip>

## Three ways to build the vector

| | TF-IDF + logistic regression | Averaged embeddings + classifier | Fine-tuned encoder (BERT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| vector | one slot per word (thousands), mostly zeros | mean of the word vectors (e.g. 300 numbers) | encoder output at `[CLS]` (768 numbers in BERT-base) |
| word order | ignored | ignored | used: every word read in context |
| "tasty" ≈ "delicious"? | no: unrelated slots | yes | yes |
| training | seconds on a CPU | seconds to minutes | minutes to hours; a GPU helps |
| easy to inspect? | yes: one weight per word | partly | hard |

A pretrained encoder such as <Term def="Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers: a Transformer encoder pretrained on huge amounts of text by filling in masked-out words. It is fine-tuned for tasks such as classification.">BERT</Term> has already read billions of words. (You will open up Transformers in [Attention and Transformers](#/learn/attention-and-transformers/attention-intuition) and BERT itself in [BERT, GPT and language modelling](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/bert-and-gpt); for now treat it as a black box that turns text into a vector.) To <Term def="Continuing to train a pretrained model on your own labelled data, usually with a small learning rate, so it adapts to your task without forgetting what it learned in pretraining.">fine-tune</Term> it for sentiment:

<Steps title="Fine-tuning BERT for sentiment">
  <Step title="Read the review">
    BERT reads `[CLS] the laksa was cold [SEP]`, each word in the context of all the others.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the [CLS] vector">
    Its final vector at `[CLS]` (768 numbers) summarises the whole review, word order included.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add a classification head">
    One linear layer and a softmax turn that vector into P(positive) and P(negative).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Train gently">
    Train the head *and* all of BERT on your labelled reviews for 2 to 4 passes (epochs) with a tiny learning rate ($2 \times 10^{-5}$ to $5 \times 10^{-5}$), so the pretrained knowledge is nudged, not wiped out.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Reusing what a model learned on one big task as the starting point for another is called <Term def="Reusing a model trained on one big task (such as predicting missing words in billions of sentences) as the starting point for a different, smaller task.">transfer learning</Term>.

The averaged-embedding model is short enough to write out in PyTorch; open it to see the one line where word order disappears.

<Reveal label="PyTorch version of the averaged-embedding classifier">

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
vocab = {"the": 0, "food": 1, "was": 2, "good": 3, "not": 4, "bad": 5}

class AvgEmbeddingClassifier(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab_size, dim, n_classes):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, dim)   # one learnable vector per word
        self.fc = nn.Linear(dim, n_classes)        # the classifier

    def forward(self, ids):          # ids: (batch, seq_len)
        vecs = self.emb(ids)         # (batch, seq_len, dim)
        avg = vecs.mean(dim=1)       # (batch, dim)  <- word order is lost here
        return self.fc(avg)          # (batch, n_classes) logits

model = AvgEmbeddingClassifier(len(vocab), dim=8, n_classes=2)
a = torch.tensor([[0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]])   # the food was good not bad
b = torch.tensor([[0, 1, 2, 5, 4, 3]])   # the food was bad not good
print(model(a).shape)
print(model(a))
print(model(b))
print(torch.allclose(model(a), model(b)))
```

### Which approach should you choose?

The deciding factor is usually **how many labelled examples you have**:

- **Always** start with a TF-IDF + logistic regression **baseline**. It trains in seconds and tells you what any fancier model must beat.
- **Small labelled set (tens to a few hundred):** reuse a pretrained model ([transfer learning](#/learn/vision-tasks/transfer-learning-and-augmentation) again): freeze it and train logistic regression on its embeddings, or fine-tune it gently. Pretrained knowledge is what makes small datasets workable, and it usually beats TF-IDF even here.
- **Thousands or more:** fully fine-tune a pretrained encoder (BERT) for the best accuracy.
- **Never** train a big network from scratch on a small labelled set: with far more parameters than examples, it memorises the training set (overfits).

## Evaluating: when accuracy lies

Real text datasets are usually <Term def="A dataset in which some classes have many more examples than others, for example 150 praise messages but only 10 food-safety reports.">imbalanced</Term>. Imagine a food-delivery app that sorts 200 messages into *praise* (150), *late delivery* (40) and *food safety* (10). The rare class matters most, yet accuracy hardly notices it, as you saw in [Imbalanced classes](#/learn/model-evaluation/imbalanced-data).

The fix is the one from [Confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1](#/learn/model-evaluation/confusion-matrix-and-metrics) and [macro averaging](#/learn/model-evaluation/regression-and-overlap-metrics): score each class $c$ with $F1_c = 2\,TP / (2\,TP + FP + FN)$, then take **macro-F1**, the plain average over the classes, so every class gets an equal vote however small it is.

<MacroF1Explorer />

<Steps title="The lazy model on 20 messages (14 praise, 4 late, 2 food safety)">
  <Step title="It predicts 'praise' for everything">
    It gets all 14 praise messages right and the other 6 wrong, so accuracy $= 14/20 = 70\%$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="F1 for praise">
    $TP = 14$, $FP = 6$ (the 6 messages wrongly called praise), $FN = 0$. So $F1 = \frac{28}{28 + 6 + 0} = \frac{28}{34} \approx 0.82$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="F1 for the other two classes">
    It never predicts *late* or *food safety*, so $TP = 0$ and both F1 scores are 0.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Macro-F1">
    $\frac{0.82 + 0 + 0}{3} \approx 0.27$. Accuracy said 70%; macro-F1 shows the model is useless for two of the three classes.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<ExamTip>

For "which metric is misleading on imbalanced data?" the answer is **accuracy**: predicting the majority class scores high while catching nothing rare. F1 is the **harmonic** mean of precision and recall. For classes of different sizes, report **macro-F1**. Without a calculator, use $F1 = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}$ straight from the confusion matrix.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Text classification = **tokenise → vectorise → classify**. The vector must have a fixed length, whatever the length of the text.
- In a linear bag-of-words model, $z = b + \sum_j w_j x_j$: each word adds its own fixed weight. That makes it easy to inspect, but blind to word order, negation and sarcasm.
- Bag of words, TF-IDF and averaged embeddings all ignore word order. A fine-tuned encoder (BERT's `[CLS]` vector plus a linear head) reads words in context and is usually the most accurate.
- Start with a TF-IDF + logistic regression baseline. With little labelled data, reuse a pretrained model; never train a huge model from scratch on a small dataset.
- On imbalanced classes, accuracy can look great while the rare class is ignored. Use per-class precision, recall and F1, and **macro-F1** to give every class an equal vote.

</Takeaways>
