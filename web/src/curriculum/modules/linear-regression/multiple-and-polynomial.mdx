A flat's price depends on more than its size: age, storey and distance to the MRT all matter. So far our line used one input. This lesson lets it use as many as you like, writes the whole model as **one matrix product**, solves it in **one formula** (no gradient descent needed), and then uses the very same maths to fit **curves**.

<Analogy title="A taxi fare is a multiple regression">

A taxi fare is roughly *base fare + (rate per km) × distance + (rate per minute) × time*. Each input has its own rate, and there is a fixed starting amount. If nobody told you the rates, you could work them out from a pile of old receipts. That is exactly what multiple regression does: it learns one weight per input, plus a bias, from examples.

</Analogy>

## From one feature to many

With one input, the model was $\hat{y} = wx + b$. With $d$ inputs (features) $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d$ it becomes

$$
\hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_d x_d + b
$$

- $\hat{y}$ ("y-hat") is the prediction, for example the fare.
- $x_j$ is feature number $j$ of one example, for example $x_1$ = distance in km, $x_2$ = time in minutes.
- $w_j$ is the <Term def="A number the model learns that says how strongly one feature pushes the prediction up or down.">weight</Term> of feature $j$: its "rate".
- $b$ is the <Term def="A constant added to every prediction, also called the intercept. It is the prediction when every feature is 0.">bias</Term> (intercept): the base fare.

**How to read a weight:** $w_j$ is how much $\hat{y}$ changes when $x_j$ goes up by 1 *while every other feature stays the same*. If $w_1 = 0.85$, one extra kilometre adds about 85 cents for a trip of the same duration.

With two features the model is a flat, tilted **plane** floating above the $(x_1, x_2)$ floor; with more it is a "hyperplane" you cannot draw, but the maths does not care.

## Matrix form: every prediction in one line

Writing $w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots$ for every example gets tiring. Instead, stack the data in a table:

- The <Term def="A table of numbers with rows and columns. A matrix with n rows and d columns has shape n×d.">matrix</Term> $X$ has **one row per example** and **one column per feature**.
- **Fold the bias in:** give $X$ an extra first column of 1s and give $w$ a matching first entry $w_0 = b$. Then $w_0 \times 1 = b$ is added to every prediction automatically, and the separate $+\,b$ disappears.

For five taxi rides with features $[1, \text{km}, \text{min}]$:

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 10 \\ 1 & 5 & 15 \\ 1 & 8 & 30 \\ 1 & 3 & 20 \\ 1 & 10 & 25 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
w = \begin{bmatrix} w_0 \\ w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and the model is simply $\hat{y} = Xw$. Multiplying a matrix by a column means: for each row, multiply it entry by entry with the column and add up (a <Term def="Multiply two lists entry by entry and add the results: [1, 2]·[3, 4] = 1×3 + 2×4 = 11.">dot product</Term>). Row 1 gives $\hat{y}_1 = 1\cdot w_0 + 2\cdot w_1 + 10\cdot w_2$, which is exactly the fare formula for ride 1. One product, all five predictions.

<PredictionsAsXw />

From here on, to keep the notation short, **$d$ counts every column of $X$, including the column of 1s**. Shapes then follow one rule:

$$
\underbrace{X}_{n \times d}\;\underbrace{w}_{d \times 1} = \underbrace{\hat{y}}_{n \times 1}
$$

A product $(a \times k)(k \times c)$ only exists when the **inner** sizes match (both $k$), and the answer has the **outer** sizes $a \times c$. Here the inner size $d$ says "one weight per column of $X$", and the answer has one prediction per row.

<KeyIdea>

Put each example in a row of $X$ (with a column of 1s for the bias). Then **all** predictions are $\hat{y} = Xw$, with shapes $(n \times d)(d \times 1) = (n \times 1)$.

</KeyIdea>

## The normal equation: jump straight to the bottom

In earlier lessons you met the loss as a bowl and walked down it with gradient descent. For linear regression we can do better and **solve for the bottom directly**. The loss is the mean squared error

$$
L(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \frac{1}{n}\lVert y - Xw \rVert^2 ,
$$

where $\lVert \cdot \rVert^2$ means "square every entry and add them up". Its gradient, which you met in the gradient descent lesson, is

$$
\nabla L = \frac{2}{n} X^\top (Xw - y).
$$

Here $Xw - y$ is the column of errors, and $X^\top$ is the <Term def="The matrix flipped over its diagonal: row i becomes column i. An n×d matrix becomes d×n.">transpose</Term> of $X$. At the bottom of the bowl the gradient is zero, so

$$
X^\top X\, w = X^\top y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y .
$$

This is the <Hl>normal equation</Hl>. Symbol by symbol:

- $X^\top X$ is a small $d \times d$ square: entry $(j, k)$ adds up feature $j$ × feature $k$ over all examples.
- $X^\top y$ is a $d \times 1$ column: entry $j$ adds up feature $j$ × target.
- $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is the <Term def="For a square matrix A, the matrix A⁻¹ with A⁻¹A = I (the identity). Multiplying by it undoes multiplying by A, like dividing by a number. It only exists when det(A) ≠ 0.">inverse</Term> of $X^\top X$: multiplying by it undoes multiplying by $X^\top X$, just as dividing by 3 undoes multiplying by 3.

Why "normal"? Setting the gradient to zero says $X^\top(y - Xw) = 0$: the errors are at right angles (*normal*) to every feature column, so no feature can explain any of the leftover error.

<NormalEquationShapes />

<Steps title="Normal equation by hand: points (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 6)">
  <Step title="Build $X$ and $y$">
    One feature plus the column of 1s: $X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $y = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Shapes: $X$ is $3 \times 2$, so $w$ will be $2 \times 1$ (bias, slope).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compute $X^\top X$">
    Each entry is a column of $X$ dotted with a column of $X$. Ones with ones: $1+1+1 = 3$. Ones with $x$: $0+1+2 = 3$. $x$ with $x$: $0+1+4 = 5$. So $X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compute $X^\top y$">
    Ones with $y$: $1+2+6 = 9$. $x$ with $y$: $0\cdot1 + 1\cdot2 + 2\cdot6 = 14$. So $X^\top y = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 14 \end{bmatrix}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Invert the 2×2 matrix">
    For $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ the inverse is $\frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$, where $ad - bc$ is the <Term def="A single number computed from a square matrix. For a 2×2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] it is ad − bc. The matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant is not 0.">determinant</Term>. Here $ad - bc = 3\cdot5 - 3\cdot3 = 6$, so $(X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -3 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply to get $w$">
    $w = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 5\cdot9 - 3\cdot14 \\ -3\cdot9 + 3\cdot14 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 2.5 \end{bmatrix}$. The best line is $\hat{y} = 0.5 + 2.5x$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check">
    Predictions $0.5, 3, 5.5$; errors $y - \hat{y} = 0.5, -1, 0.5$. They add up to $0$, and $x$ times error adds up to $0\cdot0.5 + 1\cdot(-1) + 2\cdot0.5 = 0$. That is $X^\top(y - Xw) = 0$, the "normal" condition.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## When the formula breaks

$(X^\top X)^{-1}$ only exists when $\det(X^\top X) \neq 0$. A square matrix with determinant 0 is called <Term def="A square matrix whose determinant is 0. It has no inverse, because it squashes some directions flat and that cannot be undone.">singular</Term>, and $X^\top X$ is singular exactly when some column of $X$ can be built from the other columns. Two common causes:

- **Redundant features.** Height in cm *and* height in m; temperature in °C *and* °F (since $F = 1.8C + 32$ is built from the °C column and the column of 1s); a feature copied twice.
- **More columns than examples** ($d > n$). Three points cannot pin down five weights.

Then infinitely many $w$ fit the data equally well. If columns 1 and 2 are identical, only $w_1 + w_2$ matters: $(1, 1)$, $(2, 0)$ and $(5, -3)$ all give the same predictions, so there is no single answer to "invert" towards.

Three ways out:

1. **Remove the redundant column.** Usually the best fix.
2. **Use the <Term def="A generalised inverse X⁺ that exists for every matrix. When X has independent columns it equals (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ; otherwise X⁺y is the best-fitting w with the smallest length.">pseudoinverse</Term>** $X^+$, written `np.linalg.pinv(X)`. When the columns of $X$ are independent (it has *full column rank*), $X^+ = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ and $w = X^+y$ is just the normal equation. When they are not, $X^+y$ still exists: among all the best-fitting $w$ it picks the **shortest** one. For two identical columns that means splitting the weight equally, $(1, 1)$ rather than $(2, 0)$.
3. **Add a small penalty on the weights** (ridge regression, next lesson), which makes the matrix invertible again.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={23} />

**Normal equation or gradient descent?** Both reach the same $w$ (the visual above counts the gradient descent steps). The normal equation needs no learning rate and no iterations, but inverting a $d \times d$ matrix costs roughly $d^3$ operations: fine for $d = 100$, hopeless for a million features. Gradient descent takes many cheap steps, scales to huge $d$ and $n$, and also works for models that have no formula at all (logistic regression, neural networks).

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

# taxi rides: [1, km, minutes] and fares
X = np.array([[1, 2, 10],
              [1, 5, 15],
              [1, 8, 30],
              [1, 3, 20],
              [1, 10, 25]], dtype=float)
y = np.array([6.3, 9.4, 15.0, 8.6, 15.6])

w = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y        # the normal equation, literally
print("w     =", w.round(3))
print("solve =", np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y).round(3))  # better: no explicit inverse
print("lstsq =", np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0].round(3))
print("pinv  =", (np.linalg.pinv(X) @ y).round(3))
print("predictions:", (X @ w).round(2))`} />

All four lines print `[2.559 0.849 0.183]`: a base fare of about 2.56 dollars, 85 cents per km and 18 cents per minute. In real code prefer `np.linalg.lstsq` or `np.linalg.solve`; computing an inverse explicitly is slower and less accurate.

<Mistake title="Element-wise * is not matrix multiplication">

In NumPy, `X * w` multiplies entry by entry (and broadcasts), which is **not** $Xw$. Use `X @ w` (or `np.dot(X, w)`). And if you fold the bias into $X$, do not forget the column of 1s: without it every prediction is forced through 0 when all features are 0.

</Mistake>

## Curves from straight-line maths

What if the data bends? Keep the model, change the features. From one input $x$, **make new columns** $x^2$, $x^3$, … and run the same regression:

$$
\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 + w_3 x^3
$$

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & x_1^3 \\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & x_2^3 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n & x_n^2 & x_n^3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each row is one example: the input $x_i$ and its powers.

This is <Term def="Linear regression on the features 1, x, x², …, x^k made from a single input x. The fitted curve is a polynomial of degree k.">polynomial regression</Term>, and the highest power is its **degree**. The curve is not straight in $x$, but look at the weights: each $w_k$ simply multiplies a fixed column of numbers, and the prediction is their sum. The model is **linear in the weights**, so $\hat{y} = Xw$, the normal equation and gradient descent all work unchanged.

<Analogy title="Lego arches">

Every Lego brick is straight, yet enough of them, placed well, build a curved arch. Polynomial regression builds a curve out of fixed pieces ($1$, $x$, $x^2$, $x^3$), and the only thing it learns is how much of each piece to use.

</Analogy>

<PolyBasisMixer />

<KeyIdea>

"Linear" regression means **linear in the weights**, not "fits a straight line". Any model of the form $\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 f_1(x) + w_2 f_2(x) + \dots$, with fixed functions $f_j$, is linear regression on new features.

</KeyIdea>

For example, with features $[1, x, x^2, x^3]$ and weights $w = [1, -1, 0.5, 0.25]$, the input $x = 2$ becomes the row $[1, 2, 4, 8]$, and the prediction is $1 - 2 + 0.5\cdot4 + 0.25\cdot8 = 1 - 2 + 2 + 2 = 3$.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy', 'scikit-learn']} code={`import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

x = np.array([-2, -1.5, -1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2])
y = np.array([0.8, 1.36, 1.85, 1.14, 0.85, -0.04, 0.4, 1.59, 3.65])

X = np.column_stack([x**0, x, x**2, x**3])   # features 1, x, x², x³
w = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
print("NumPy weights  :", w.round(3))

poly = PolynomialFeatures(degree=3, include_bias=False)  # makes x, x², x³
model = LinearRegression().fit(poly.fit_transform(x.reshape(-1, 1)), y)
print("sklearn weights:", round(model.intercept_, 3), model.coef_.round(3))`} />

Both give $w \approx [0.628, -1.079, 0.396, 0.456]$, the same weights the **Fit** button found above. scikit-learn's `PolynomialFeatures` just builds the extra columns for you.

<Tip title="Scale before you power up">

Powers blow up quickly: $x = 100$ gives $x^3 = 1{,}000{,}000$. Columns of wildly different sizes make gradient descent crawl and the normal equation inaccurate. Rescale $x$ to a small range (for example standardise it) **before** making powers.

</Tip>

## How many powers? Too few and too many

More powers always let the curve bend more, so the error on the training data can only go down (or stay the same). But the point of a model is to predict **new** data. That is why we hold back a <Term def="Data kept aside during training and used only to check how well the model predicts examples it has not seen.">validation set</Term> and watch its error too.

<DegreeSweep />

- Degree 0 to 2: the curve is too stiff and misses the pattern even on the training points. This is <Term def="The model is too simple to capture the pattern, so both training and validation error are high.">underfitting</Term>.
- Degree 3 to 7: both errors are low. Degree 3 has the lowest validation error.
- Degree 8 and 9: the curve swings wildly between the points. At degree 9 its 10 weights can thread all 10 training points exactly (just as 2 points fix a line), so the training error drops to 0 while the validation error explodes. This is <Term def="The model learns the noise in the training data, so training error is low but error on new data is high.">overfitting</Term>, and notice the giant weights it needs.

Statisticians name these two failure modes. A model with high <Term def="Error that comes from the model being too simple to represent the pattern (underfitting). Not the same thing as the bias term b.">bias</Term> is too simple: it makes the same systematic mistakes whatever data you train it on. A model with high <Term def="Error that comes from the model being so flexible that it changes a lot when the training data changes slightly (overfitting).">variance</Term> is so flexible that a slightly different training sample would give a completely different curve. Raising the degree lowers the bias but raises the variance: that is the **bias-variance trade-off**, and the best model sits in between. (This "bias" is a kind of error, not the bias term $b$.)

You can pick the degree with the lowest validation error, or keep a flexible model and **punish large weights** so it cannot swing wildly. That second idea, regularisation, is the next lesson.

<ExamTip>

Prelim questions on this topic are fast recall, so have these ready:
- **Shapes:** $(a \times k)(k \times c) = a \times c$. $X$ is $n \times d$, $w$ is $d \times 1$, $X^\top X$ is $d \times d$.
- **Normal equation:** $w = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. It fails when $X^\top X$ is singular (duplicate or perfectly related features, or $d > n$); then use the pseudoinverse, drop a feature, or regularise.
- **$(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is the pseudoinverse** only when $X$ has full column rank.
- **Polynomial regression is still linear regression**: linear in the weights.
- **Closed form vs gradient descent:** closed form costs about $d^3$; gradient descent scales to huge $d$.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Stack examples as rows of $X$ with a column of 1s: all predictions are $\hat{y} = Xw$, shapes $(n \times d)(d \times 1)$.
- Setting the MSE gradient to zero gives the normal equation $w = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$: the exact least-squares answer in one step.
- It needs $X^\top X$ to be invertible. Redundant columns or $d > n$ make it singular; the pseudoinverse `np.linalg.pinv` still returns the shortest best-fitting $w$.
- Polynomial features $x, x^2, x^3, \dots$ fit curves with the same maths, because the model stays linear in $w$.
- Too high a degree overfits: training error falls to 0 while validation error explodes.

</Takeaways>
