At the end of the last lesson a degree-9 curve threaded all 10 training points and failed badly on new data, using weights in the hundreds. Throwing away flexibility (a lower degree) is one cure. **Regularisation** is a better one: keep the flexible model, but make big weights *cost* something, so the model only uses them when they really pay off. Questions on it appeared in both the 2026 Prelim and the 2026 Final. The lesson ends with a closely related question: how much should one big mistake cost?

<Analogy title="Packing under an airline weight limit">

With a 20 kg limit you cannot bring everything at full size. An **L2** traveller packs a lighter version of everything: smaller bottles, fewer shirts, but a bit of each. An **L1** traveller leaves whole items at home and takes the important ones at full size. Ridge (L2) and lasso (L1) regularisation make exactly these two choices about a model's weights.

</Analogy>

## Charging the model for big weights

Regularisation adds a **penalty** on the size of the weights to the loss. The two classic versions are

$$
L_{\text{ridge}}(w) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{\text{MSE: fit the data}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j^2}_{\text{L2 penalty}}
$$

$$
L_{\text{lasso}}(w) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}_{\text{MSE: fit the data}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{d} |w_j|}_{\text{L1 penalty}}
$$

- The first part is the usual mean squared error: it rewards fitting the training data.
- $\sum_j w_j^2$ is the **sum of squared weights**, also written $\lVert w \rVert_2^2$ (the squared L2 norm). Using it gives <Term def="Linear regression trained with MSE plus λ times the sum of squared weights (an L2 penalty). Also called Tikhonov regularisation; in neural networks the same idea is weight decay.">ridge regression</Term>.
- $\sum_j |w_j|$ is the **sum of absolute weights**, written $\lVert w \rVert_1$ (the L1 norm). Using it gives the <Term def="Linear regression trained with MSE plus λ times the sum of absolute weights (an L1 penalty). LASSO stands for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator.">lasso</Term>.
- $\lambda \geq 0$ (lambda) is the <Term def="How much the penalty counts compared with the data-fitting error. λ = 0 means no regularisation; a huge λ forces all weights towards 0.">regularisation strength</Term>, a hyperparameter you choose. $\lambda = 0$ is plain least squares.
- The sums run over the feature weights $w_1, \dots, w_d$ only. The **bias $w_0$ is not penalised**: it only shifts every prediction up or down and cannot make the curve wiggle, so shrinking it would just drag all predictions towards 0 for no benefit (imagine forcing every flat-price prediction towards zero dollars).

Now a weight is only worth making big if it lowers the MSE by more than it adds to the penalty. The wild degree-9 curve needed weights like 400 and −678, whose squares cost a fortune, so it is no longer the cheapest option.

<Steps title="Computing a penalised loss">
  <Step title="The setting">
    A model has bias $w_0 = 5$, weights $w_1 = 3$ and $w_2 = -1$, and its MSE on the training data is $2$. Take $\lambda = 0.1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ridge (L2)">
    Square and add the feature weights: $3^2 + (-1)^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$. Penalty $= 0.1 \times 10 = 1$. Ridge loss $= 2 + 1 = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Lasso (L1)">
    Add the absolute values: $|3| + |-1| = 4$. Penalty $= 0.1 \times 4 = 0.4$. Lasso loss $= 2 + 0.4 = 2.4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What about the bias?">
    $w_0 = 5$ appears in neither penalty. Notice also that L2 punishes the big weight much more (9 versus 3 for $w_1$), while L1 charges every unit of weight the same.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>

Regularised loss = data error + $\lambda \times$ (size of the weights). **L2/ridge** uses $\sum w_j^2$, **L1/lasso** uses $\sum |w_j|$. Bigger $\lambda$ means smaller weights and a simpler model; the bias is left out of the penalty.

</KeyIdea>

## Why L1 snaps weights to exactly zero

The two penalties look similar, but they behave very differently near zero. To see why, shrink the problem to **one weight**. Suppose that without a penalty the best value is $w = a$, so the data part of the loss is $(w - a)^2$.

**With L2:** minimise $(w - a)^2 + \lambda w^2$. Setting the derivative to zero, $2(w - a) + 2\lambda w = 0$, gives

$$
w^* = \frac{a}{1 + \lambda}.
$$

L2 **divides** by $1 + \lambda$. The weight gets smaller but never reaches 0 (unless $a$ was already 0).

**With L1:** minimise $(w - a)^2 + \lambda |w|$. For $w > 0$ the derivative is $2(w - a) + \lambda = 0$, so $w = a - \frac{\lambda}{2}$. But if $a \le \frac{\lambda}{2}$, that would be negative, and the best you can do is stop at exactly $0$. In one line:

$$
w^* = \operatorname{sign}(a)\cdot\max\!\left(|a| - \tfrac{\lambda}{2},\; 0\right).
$$

L1 **subtracts** a fixed amount and stops at zero, so every small weight becomes exactly 0.

The deep reason is the **pull** of each penalty, its slope. The slope of $\lambda w^2$ is $2\lambda w$, which fades to nothing as $w$ approaches 0, like a spring that goes slack. The slope of $\lambda |w|$ is $\pm\lambda$ however small $w$ is: a constant tug that can drag a weight all the way to 0 and hold it there.

<ShrinkOrSnap />

<Steps title="Shrink or snap, with λ = 2">
  <Step title="A big weight: a = 3">
    L2: $3 \div (1 + 2) = 1$. L1: $3 - 2/2 = 2$. Both shrink it; L1 keeps more of a big weight.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A small weight: a = 0.5">
    L2: $0.5 \div 3 \approx 0.17$, small but still there. L1: $0.5 - 1 < 0$, so the answer is exactly $0$: the feature is switched off.
  </Step>
</Steps>

You can watch the two pulls in PyTorch, which is how you would add a penalty in a Final task: add it to the loss and let autograd find its gradient.

```python
import torch

w = torch.tensor([3.0, -1.0, 0.2], requires_grad=True)
lam = 0.1

l2 = lam * (w ** 2).sum()      # ridge penalty
l2.backward()
print("L2 pull:", w.grad)       # 2 * lam * w

w.grad = None
l1 = lam * w.abs().sum()       # lasso penalty
l1.backward()
print("L1 pull:", w.grad)       # lam * sign(w)
```

```text
L2 pull: tensor([ 0.6000, -0.2000,  0.0400])
L1 pull: tensor([ 0.1000, -0.1000,  0.1000])
```

The small weight 0.2 feels a pull of only 0.04 from L2, but the full 0.1 from L1.

## The picture: diamond versus circle

There is a second, geometric way to see the same thing. A penalty with strength $\lambda$ behaves like a **budget** on the weights: keep $|w_1| + |w_2| \le t$ for L1, or $w_1^2 + w_2^2 \le t^2$ for L2, where a bigger $\lambda$ means a smaller budget $t$. With two weights the L1 budget is a **diamond** and the L2 budget is a **circle**.

The MSE, drawn over $(w_1, w_2)$, is a bowl whose contour lines are ovals around the unpenalised best fit $\hat{w}$. The regularised answer is the point inside the budget with the lowest MSE: the place where the ovals, growing outwards from $\hat{w}$, **first touch** the shape.

<L1L2Constraint />

The diamond's corners stick out along the axes, so a growing oval very often touches a corner first, and at a corner one weight is exactly 0. With many weights the L1 region has many corners and edges, so many weights land on zero together. The circle has no corners, so the touching point is usually somewhere with both weights non-zero.

<KeyIdea>

**L1 (lasso) gives sparse models**: many weights exactly 0, so it also performs feature selection. **L2 (ridge) gives small but dense weights**: everything shrinks, nothing is switched off.

</KeyIdea>

## Turning the knob: λ, and scikit-learn's C

As $\lambda$ grows from 0, the weights trace out a **regularisation path**. Explore it on a small, made-up dataset with two useless features:

<RegularisationPath />

- **$\lambda$ too small:** almost plain least squares. With few examples and many features, it overfits.
- **$\lambda$ too large:** every weight is squashed towards 0 and the model predicts nearly the average of $y$ every time. That is <Hl>underfitting</Hl>.
- **In between:** the validation error is lowest. Choose $\lambda$ with a validation set or cross-validation, never with the training error (which always prefers $\lambda = 0$).

Three practical rules:

1. **Standardise the features first.** The penalty treats all weights alike, but a feature measured in millimetres needs a much bigger weight than the same feature in metres, so it would be punished unfairly.
2. **Do not penalise the bias.** Libraries handle this for you; in your own code, leave $w_0$ out of the penalty (and out of its gradient).
3. **Know which way the knob turns.** scikit-learn's `Ridge(alpha=…)` and `Lasso(alpha=…)` use `alpha` like $\lambda$: bigger means stronger. But `LogisticRegression(C=…)` and `SVC(C=…)` use <Hl>C, the inverse of the regularisation strength</Hl>, playing the role of $1/\lambda$: a **smaller C means stronger regularisation**.

<Mistake title="C is not λ">

"The model overfits, so increase C" is backwards. In `LogisticRegression` and `SVC`, increasing C *weakens* the penalty and makes overfitting worse. To regularise more, **decrease** C (for example from 100 to 0.1).

</Mistake>

Two more facts connect regularisation to things you already know.

**Ridge fixes the singular matrix.** Writing the ridge loss with the total (not mean) squared error, $\lVert y - Xw \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert w \rVert^2$, and setting its gradient to zero gives $w = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y$, where $I$ is the identity matrix (1s on the diagonal; use 0 in the bias position to leave it unpenalised). Adding $\lambda > 0$ to the diagonal makes the matrix invertible even when columns are duplicated, the problem from the last lesson.

**L2 is "weight decay".** The gradient of $\lambda \sum w_j^2$ is $2\lambda w$, so a gradient descent step becomes $w \leftarrow w - \eta(\nabla \text{MSE} + 2\lambda w) = (1 - 2\eta\lambda)\,w - \eta\, \nabla\text{MSE}$. Every step first shrinks ("decays") each weight by a factor slightly below 1. In PyTorch, `torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, weight_decay=1e-4)` does this for you: it adds `weight_decay * w` to each gradient, which is the same as a penalty of $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 10^{-4} \lVert w \rVert^2$.

## MSE versus MAE: how much do you fear big mistakes?

Regularisation changes the *penalty* part of the loss. The *data* part is a choice too. Besides the MSE there is the <Term def="Mean absolute error: the average of |y − ŷ| over all examples. Every unit of error costs the same, however big the error is.">mean absolute error</Term>:

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
$$

Squaring makes big errors enormous. With errors $1, 1, 1, 1, 10$:

- MSE $= (1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 100) \div 5 = 20.8$, and the single error of 10 supplies $100/104 \approx 96\%$ of it.
- MAE $= (1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 10) \div 5 = 2.8$.

So a model trained with MSE will bend over backwards to reduce one huge error, even if that makes all the others worse. That is great when big errors are genuinely disastrous, and terrible when the big error is an <Term def="A data point far from the pattern of the rest, often caused by a typo, a sensor glitch or a rare event.">outlier</Term> caused by a typo.

<Analogy title="Two teachers">

The MSE teacher is strict about disasters: one answer that is 10 marks off hurts you more than ten answers that are 1 mark off. The MAE teacher simply counts marks lost, so one disaster costs exactly as much as ten small slips.

</Analogy>

<RobustLossCompare />

The simplest model of all, predicting one constant $c$ for everyone, shows the difference clearly. For the data $1, 2, 3, 4, 100$:

- The constant with the smallest **MSE** is the **mean**, $110 \div 5 = 22$: dragged far upwards by the 100.
- The constant with the smallest **MAE** is the **median**, $3$: moving $c$ up by a little adds to the error of every point below $c$ and removes it from every point above, so the best spot has as many points on each side, however far away they are.

That is why MAE is called <Term def="Not thrown off much by a few extreme data points.">robust</Term> to outliers. A popular compromise is the **Huber loss**. For a residual $r = y - \hat{y}$ and a threshold $\delta$ that you choose,

$$
\ell_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2} r^2 & \text{if } |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\left(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right) & \text{if } |r| > \delta \end{cases}
$$

It acts like the squared error for small residuals (smooth, easy to optimise) and grows only linearly, like the absolute error, beyond $\delta$ (so outliers cannot dominate). The two pieces meet smoothly at $|r| = \delta$. Switch it on in the visual above to see a third line.

<ExamTip>

Regularisation questions in the Prelim are quick recall. Know these cold:
- **L1 = lasso = $\lambda\sum|w_j|$ = sparse, many exact zeros, feature selection.** **L2 = ridge = $\lambda\sum w_j^2$ = small, non-zero weights = weight decay.**
- Why L1 gives zeros: its slope stays at $\pm\lambda$ near 0 (diamond corners); L2's slope $2\lambda w$ fades to 0.
- **Bigger $\lambda$ → simpler model → underfitting; $\lambda = 0$ → no regularisation.** scikit-learn's **C is the inverse**: smaller C = stronger regularisation.
- The **bias is not regularised**; **standardise** features before regularising.
- **MSE ↔ mean, sensitive to outliers; MAE ↔ median, robust; Huber in between.**
- $\nabla_w\, \lambda\lVert w \rVert^2 = 2\lambda w$.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Regularisation adds $\lambda \times$ (weight size) to the loss so a flexible model cannot use huge weights to chase noise; the bias is not penalised.
- L2/ridge ($\sum w_j^2$) divides weights down smoothly; L1/lasso ($\sum |w_j|$) subtracts a fixed amount and snaps small weights to exactly 0 (diamond corners), selecting features.
- Too little $\lambda$ overfits, too much underfits: pick it on validation data. In scikit-learn, C = inverse strength, so small C = strong regularisation.
- MSE punishes big errors quadratically (best constant: the mean); MAE punishes them linearly and is robust to outliers (best constant: the median); Huber mixes the two.

</Takeaways>
