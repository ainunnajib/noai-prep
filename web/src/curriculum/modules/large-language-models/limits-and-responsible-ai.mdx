LLMs write fluent, confident text about almost anything. That is exactly what makes them risky: they can be fluently *wrong*, unfair in ways that are hard to see, and hijacked by text hidden in what they read. This lesson explains **why** each problem happens, so you can predict it instead of memorising a list, and then how people measure, reduce and govern these risks, including Singapore's approach. NOAI lists responsible AI in its Generative AI domain, and the same reasoning answers many "which metric?" and "which fix?" questions.

<Analogy title="The student who never leaves a blank">

In the NOAI prelim a wrong answer costs nothing, so on a question you cannot do, the smart move is to guess: a blank scores 0 for certain, while a guess scores 1 a quarter of the time. Now picture a student trained for years under that rule on millions of questions. They become superb at writing a confident-looking answer to anything, and they almost never write "I don't know". That student is an LLM.

</Analogy>

## Why LLMs make things up

A <Term def="Fluent, confident output from an AI model that is false or not supported by any source, such as an invented fact, quote or reference.">hallucination</Term> is fluent, confident output that is false. In 2023 two New York lawyers were fined US$5,000 after filing a court brief that cited six court cases ChatGPT had invented, complete with fake quotations.

Recall how an LLM writes (from [How LLMs are built](#/learn/large-language-models/how-llms-are-built)): it turns everything so far into probabilities for the next token, picks one, appends it and repeats. Nothing in this loop looks anything up or checks it. A year in an answer is just another token, chosen because it is **probable**.

<HallucinationConfidence />

<KeyIdea>

An LLM produces text that is **probable**, not text that is **verified**. When a fact was learnt well, probable and true coincide. When it was not, the most probable continuation is still a fluent sentence, with a guess in the gap.

</KeyIdea>

Four things push a model towards guessing:

1. **Rare or missing knowledge.** Facts seen thousands of times are learnt well; facts seen a few times blur with similar ones (like the MRT years above). Anything after the model's <Term def="The date at which a model's training data ends. The model knows nothing about later events unless they are given to it in the prompt.">knowledge cut-off</Term> is simply unknown.
2. **Training rewards guessing.** Web text rarely answers a question with "I don't know", and most tests used to score models give no credit for admitting uncertainty, so, like a prelim candidate, the model learns that a guess beats a blank. OpenAI researchers made exactly this argument in a 2025 paper, *Why Language Models Hallucinate*.
3. **Sampling, with no going back.** Above temperature 0 the model sometimes picks a less likely token (see [Decoding](#/learn/large-language-models/decoding-and-sampling)). It writes left to right and never revises, so once a wrong token is out, the next tokens are chosen to *agree* with it: one wrong year grows into a whole invented story.
4. **Rewarded for sounding helpful.** Preference training rewards answers people like, and people like confident answers.

<Steps title="Small error rates add up in long answers">
  <Step title="The set-up">
    A model writes a summary containing 5 separate facts. Each is right with probability $0.9$, independently of the others.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply">
    All five right: $0.9^5$. Square twice, then multiply once more: $0.9^2 = 0.81$, $0.81^2 = 0.6561$, and $0.6561 \times 0.9 \approx 0.59$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Read it">
    Only about **59%** of such summaries are fully correct, so roughly 4 in 10 contain at least one error, even though each fact is "usually right". The longer the answer, the more it needs checking.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Reducing hallucination

- **Ground the answer.** Retrieve trustworthy documents, put them in the prompt and tell the model to answer only from them. This is called <Term def="Retrieval-augmented generation: search a document store for passages relevant to the question and add them to the prompt, so the model answers from evidence instead of memory.">retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)</Term>, and you will build it in [the RAG pipeline](#/learn/prompting-and-rag/rag-pipeline). Switch on the source in the visual to see why it works.
- **Ask for sources, and check them.** Citations turn claims into something a person can verify.
- **Make "I don't know" allowed**, explicitly, in the prompt.
- **Use tools** for what token prediction is bad at: a calculator or code for arithmetic, a search engine for recent events.
- **Keep a human in the loop** for anything that matters: medicine, law, homework you will hand in.

<Mistake title="Myth: temperature 0 stops hallucination">

Temperature 0 always picks the single most likely token. That removes *random* slips, but if the model's top guess is wrong it repeats that wrong answer every time: ask it about the made-up prize and it says "2016" with total consistency. Low temperature makes a model **predictable**, not **truthful**.

</Mistake>

## Bias in, bias out

<Analogy title="One family's recipe book">

A recipe book written entirely by one family makes every dish taste the way that family likes it. Nobody meant to leave other tastes out; the book only knows what it was given. A model is the same: it learns whatever patterns are in its data, including unfair ones.

</Analogy>

In this lesson <Term def="Systematic errors that treat some group of people worse than others, for example lower approval rates or higher error rates for one group. Not the same as the bias term b of a neuron.">bias</Term> means unfair, systematic differences in how a model treats groups of people. It enters in a few ways:

- **Historical bias**: the labels record past human decisions, unfair ones included. In 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had scrapped an experimental CV-screening tool. Trained on ten years of CVs that came mostly from men, it learnt to downgrade CVs containing the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain".
- **Representation bias**: some groups are rare in the data, so the model works worse for them. The 2018 *Gender Shades* study found commercial face-analysis systems misclassified the gender of darker-skinned women up to 34.7% of the time, against at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.
- **Proxies**: deleting a sensitive column does not delete the information. Postcode, school, hobbies or word choice (that word "women's") can stand in for it.

LLMs inherit all three from web text: they can repeat stereotypes (such as linking certain jobs to one gender) and tend to work best in the languages and cultures most common online.

<BiasAmplifier />

Why does the model make things *worse* than the records? At the same score, past reviewers approved B applicants less often, so the quickest way for the model to lower its loss is to raise B's bar. It then applies that rule to every applicant, every time: a tendency in the data becomes a hard rule.

<KeyIdea>

A model learns whatever predicts its labels, unfair patterns included. **Fairness must be measured per group**: one overall accuracy number can hide a large gap.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="How 90% accuracy can hide a 70% group">
  <Step title="Group A: 80 applicants">
    40 qualified, 40 not. The model approves 38 of the 40 qualified and correctly rejects 38 of the 40 unqualified, so it gets $38 + 38 = 76$ right: $76/80 = 95\%$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Group B: 20 applicants">
    10 qualified, 10 not. The model rejects all 10 unqualified ones but approves only 4 of the qualified ones, so it gets $10 + 4 = 14$ right: $14/20 = 70\%$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Overall">
    $\dfrac{76 + 14}{100} = 90\%$. The headline looks good because B is only a fifth of the data.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Look per group">
    Qualified applicants rejected: A $2/40 = 5\%$, B $6/10 = 60\%$. Approval rate: A $40/80 = 50\%$ (38 qualified plus 2 unqualified), B $4/20 = 20\%$. Same ability, very different treatment.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Two common fairness checks come from this table. <Term def="A fairness check: each group is approved (or receives the positive prediction) at a similar rate.">Demographic parity</Term> compares approval rates (50% against 20%). <Term def="A fairness check: people who truly qualify are approved at a similar rate in each group, i.e. similar recall per group.">Equal opportunity</Term> compares how often *qualified* people are approved (95% against 40%). They can disagree, and choosing between them is a human decision about values, which is why governance matters.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={263} />

That is the same trap in another form: one big number dominated by the common case. To reduce bias, **audit the data** (who is in it, how were the labels made?), **report metrics per group**, collect better data, **reweight** or resample, look for proxies, and keep people reviewing high-stakes decisions. Metrics like recall and precision per class come from [confusion matrices](#/learn/model-evaluation/confusion-matrix-and-metrics).

<Mistake title="Two traps">

- *"We deleted the gender column, so the model can't be biased."* Proxies still carry the information, and the labels still carry the past decisions.
- *"Bias" means three different things in this course*: the bias term $b$ in $wx + b$, the statistical bias of the bias–variance trade-off, and the unfair bias of this lesson. Read the question to see which one is meant.

</Mistake>

## Prompt injection: when data becomes orders

<Analogy title="The forged memo">

A forged memo slipped into a manager's inbox says "Urgent: transfer the money to this account now. Signed, the CEO." An assistant who obeys every memo they read is easy to exploit, however clever they are.

</Analogy>

<Term def="An attack that hides instructions inside content a model reads (a web page, email, document or tool result) so that the model follows them instead of its developer's or user's instructions.">Prompt injection</Term> hides instructions in content an LLM reads. When a user types the attack directly ("ignore your rules and…") to get forbidden output, it is often called a <Term def="A prompt written by the user to trick a model into breaking the rules its developer set, such as its safety rules.">jailbreak</Term>. The more dangerous kind is **indirect**: the instruction sits in a web page, email or document that the model reads *for* you, so you never see it.

Why is it hard to stop? In an ordinary program, instructions (the code) and data are kept apart. An LLM receives everything as **one stream of tokens**, and it was trained to follow instructions found in text. There is no reliable "this part is only data" switch, and attackers can phrase an instruction in endless ways. Prompt injection is ranked the number-one risk in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications, a widely used security checklist.

<PromptInjectionDemo />

Since no filter catches everything, the defence is **layers**, designed so that a fooled model cannot do much harm:

- **Least privilege**: give the assistant only the tools and data it needs. A summariser does not need a "send" button.
- **Human confirmation** before anything with side effects: sending, paying, deleting, posting.
- **Mark untrusted text** as data and tell the model never to obey it (helps, but is not enough alone).
- **Detect and monitor**: scan inputs and outputs for suspicious instructions, log what the assistant does, and attack your own system before others do.

<KeyIdea>

Anything an LLM reads can act like an instruction. Treat retrieved and third-party text as untrusted, and limit what the model is allowed to do.

</KeyIdea>

## Privacy and misuse

**Privacy.** Web-scraped training data contains personal information, and models can memorise rare strings: in 2021 researchers extracted names, phone numbers and email addresses that GPT-2 had memorised from its training data. What you type into a chatbot may also be stored and, depending on the service, used for training. So do not paste personal data, passwords or confidential work into public chatbots. In Singapore the Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) governs how organisations collect and use personal data.

**Misuse.** Generative AI makes convincing fakes cheap: <Term def="Realistic fake video, images or audio of a real person, generated or edited with AI.">deepfake</Term> videos and cloned voices for scams, fake news at scale, phishing emails in perfect English. Responses include safety training (models refuse clearly harmful requests), labelling or watermarking AI-made content, and habits such as checking an unexpected "urgent" request through a second channel.

## Responsible AI and how it is governed

Organisations turn these lessons into principles and practices:

| Principle | The question it asks | Example practice |
|---|---|---|
| Transparency and explainability | Do people know AI is involved, and can a decision be explained? | Say when users talk to a bot; give reasons for a rejection |
| Fairness | Does it work equally well for different groups? | Per-group metrics, bias audits |
| Accountability | Who answers for it when it goes wrong? | A named owner, records of how it was built, a way to appeal |
| Safety and robustness | Does it behave reliably, even under attack? | Testing, <Term def="Deliberately attacking your own AI system (with tricky, harmful or adversarial inputs) to find failures before real attackers or users do.">red-teaming</Term>, monitoring after launch |
| Privacy and data governance | Is personal data handled lawfully and carefully? | Collect only what is needed; follow the PDPA |
| Human oversight | Can a person step in? | Human review of high-stakes decisions |

**Singapore's approach** is practical guidance plus testing tools, mostly voluntary:

- The **Model AI Governance Framework** (IMDA and PDPC; first edition 2019, second 2020) rests on two principles: decisions made with AI should be **explainable, transparent and fair**, and AI systems should be **human-centric**. It asks organisations to match the level of human involvement to the risk: the more severe or likely the harm to people, the more a person should be in the loop.
- The **Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI** (2024, IMDA and the AI Verify Foundation) covers nine dimensions: accountability, data, trusted development and deployment, incident reporting, testing and assurance, security, content provenance, safety and alignment research, and AI for the public good.
- **AI Verify** (launched 2022, open-sourced through the AI Verify Foundation in 2023) is a testing framework and software toolkit that lets companies check their AI systems against internationally recognised principles such as transparency, fairness and robustness, and report the results.

Other places take other routes. The **EU AI Act**, in force from August 2024, is binding law that sorts AI uses by risk: a few are banned outright (such as social scoring), and high-risk uses such as hiring and credit scoring carry strict duties.

<Takeaways>

- LLMs **hallucinate** because they generate probable text, not verified text; rare facts, training that rewards guessing and left-to-right sampling make confident errors likely. Ground answers in sources and check them.
- Models learn **bias** from historical labels, unrepresentative data and proxies, and can amplify it. Measure accuracy, approval and error rates **per group**; one overall number can hide a large gap.
- **Prompt injection** works because instructions and data reach the model as one token stream. Defend in layers: least privilege, human confirmation, marking untrusted text, monitoring.
- Also guard **privacy** (memorised data, what you paste in) and watch for **misuse** such as deepfakes.
- Responsible AI rests on transparency, fairness, accountability, safety, privacy and human oversight; Singapore's **Model AI Governance Framework**, its 2024 generative-AI edition and **AI Verify** put them into practice.

</Takeaways>
