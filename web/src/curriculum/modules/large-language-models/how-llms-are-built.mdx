ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Llama can seem like magic, but they are all built with a recipe that fits on one page: read an enormous amount of text, learn to follow instructions, then learn which answers people prefer. Knowing the recipe explains what these models are good at and why they fail in strange ways. NOAI asks about every step, from "what does GPT stand for?" to "what are LLMs trained on?".

<Analogy title="From library reader to exam pro">

Imagine a student who first reads the entire National Library (every book, newspaper and website) without anyone testing them. That is **pretraining**. They know a lot but ramble. Next they attend a short course on answering exam questions by copying model answers written by teachers: **supervised fine-tuning**. Finally, teachers look at pairs of their answers, say which one is better, and the student adjusts to earn more praise: **preference alignment**. Almost all the knowledge came from the reading; the course and the feedback shaped how it is used.

</Analogy>

## Three stages, one model

A <Term def="Large language model: a very large Transformer network (billions of weights) trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Llama are LLMs.">large language model (LLM)</Term> is a decoder-only Transformer (the GPT design from [BERT, GPT and language modelling](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/bert-and-gpt)) with billions of <Term def="The numbers inside a network that training adjusts. 'Parameters' and 'weights' mean the same thing here. A '7B model' has about 7 billion of them.">parameters</Term>. The same network is trained three times in a row, each time with different data and a different goal. Step through the stages below and watch how the answers change.

<LLMTrainingStages />

<KeyIdea>

**Pretraining → supervised fine-tuning (SFT) → preference alignment (such as RLHF).** Pretraining gives the model its knowledge and language. The two later, much smaller stages teach it to follow instructions and to give the answers people prefer.

</KeyIdea>

## Stage 1: pretraining, or "guess the next token"

Text is first cut into <Term def="The pieces a language model reads and writes: whole words, parts of words or punctuation. A rough rule for English: 1 token ≈ 3/4 of a word.">tokens</Term> (see [tokenisation](#/learn/text-as-data/tokenisation)). Pretraining then asks one question at every position of every document: *given the tokens so far, what comes next?* The model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary, and it is scored on the probability it gave to the token that really came next. The training loss is the average cross-entropy over all positions:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln p_\theta\left(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\right)
$$

- $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$ are the $T$ tokens of a piece of text.
- $p_\theta(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$ is the probability that the model, with weights $\theta$ ("theta"), gives to the real token $x_t$ after seeing only the tokens before it. The bar $\mid$ reads "given".
- $-\ln p$ is near 0 when $p$ is near 1 (a confident, correct guess) and very large when $p$ is near 0. Averaging over the $T$ positions gives one number for gradient descent to push down.

In words: **make the real next token as likely as possible, everywhere.**

<NextTokenPairs />

Notice that nobody labelled anything. The "right answer" for each example is simply the next token, which is already in the text. Training where the labels come from the data itself is called <Term def="Training where the labels are made automatically from the raw data (here, the next token of the text), so no human labelling is needed. That is what makes training on trillions of tokens possible.">self-supervised learning</Term>. It is why pretraining can use most of the public web: around 10 trillion tokens or more for recent models (Meta's Llama 3 was pretrained on over 15 trillion).

<Steps title="Scoring a model on three tokens by hand">
  <Step title="Read off the probabilities">
    Suppose the model gave the three real next tokens probabilities $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac18$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take −ln of each">
    $-\ln\tfrac12 = \ln 2 \approx 0.69$, then $-\ln\tfrac14 = 2\ln 2 \approx 1.39$, then $-\ln\tfrac18 = 3\ln 2 \approx 2.08$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average them">
    $\mathcal{L} = \frac{\ln 2 + 2\ln 2 + 3\ln 2}{3} = 2\ln 2 \approx 1.39$. Each training step nudges the weights so that these probabilities rise and the average falls.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Why does such a simple game teach so much? Because predicting well *requires* knowledge. To guess the token after "The capital of Japan is", the model must know the fact. After "Two plus three equals" it needs arithmetic, and to continue a Python function it must follow the code. Over trillions of examples, the only way to keep the loss low is to absorb grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning.

What comes out is a <Term def="An LLM straight after pretraining, before any instruction tuning. It continues text in the style of its training data rather than answering like an assistant.">base model</Term>: a brilliant autocomplete. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions, because on the web questions often come in lists.

This stage also gives GPT its name. **GPT** stands for **Generative Pre-trained Transformer**: *generative* because it produces text token by token, *pre-trained* because of this first stage, and *Transformer* because of the network design.

## Stage 2: supervised fine-tuning teaches the format

<Term def="Continuing to train an already-trained model on a smaller, more specific dataset so that it adapts to a new task or behaviour.">Fine-tuning</Term> means continuing to train an already-trained model on a smaller, focused dataset. In <Term def="Supervised fine-tuning: training a pretrained LLM on example prompts paired with ideal responses written by people, using the same next-token loss. Also called instruction tuning.">supervised fine-tuning (SFT)</Term>, also called instruction tuning, the data is tens of thousands of example conversations, each a prompt plus an ideal reply written by a person. Special tokens mark who is speaking:

```text
<|user|>
Summarise this email briefly.
<|assistant|>
Meeting moved to Friday, 3 pm.
<|end|>
```

The loss is the **same next-token loss** as before, usually counted only on the reply tokens. After training on these examples the model has learned the *format*: when a request follows `<|user|>`, an answer follows `<|assistant|>`. It learns few new facts here; SFT reshapes how existing knowledge is used. ([Fine-tuning and LoRA](#/learn/large-language-models/fine-tuning-and-lora) shows how to fine-tune cheaply.)

<Mistake title="Myth: fine-tuning is where the model gets its knowledge">

SFT data is tiny: tens of thousands of conversations against trillions of pretraining tokens. Knowledge comes from pretraining; SFT teaches behaviour. If the base model never saw a fact, a small SFT set will not reliably add it, and trying can teach the model to make things up confidently.

</Mistake>

## Stage 3: learning from preferences (RLHF)

SFT copies human-written answers, but writing a perfect answer is slow, and most prompts have no single correct reply, only better and worse ones. *Comparing* two answers is much easier than writing one, just as judging a cooking contest is easier than cooking. So the last stage learns from comparisons. The classic recipe is <Term def="Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare pairs of model answers, a reward model learns to predict their preferences, and the LLM is then trained with reinforcement learning to earn high reward.">RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)</Term>:

1. **Sample**: for many prompts, the SFT model writes several answers.
2. **Compare**: people pick the better answer in each pair.
3. **Train a reward model**: a separate network learns to give each answer a score $r$ so that preferred answers score higher. It models the chance that a person prefers answer A to answer B as $\sigma(r_A - r_B)$, where $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ is the [sigmoid](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression). Equal scores give $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, a coin flip; the bigger the gap, the more confident the preference.
4. **Optimise the LLM**: with <Term def="Learning by trial and error to maximise a reward signal, instead of copying correct answers. Here, the LLM writes answers and is updated to make high-reward answers more likely.">reinforcement learning</Term> (usually an algorithm called PPO), the LLM writes answers, the reward model scores them, and the LLM is updated to make high-scoring answers more likely. A penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model stops it from producing odd text that merely fools the reward model.

<PreferenceToReward />

Look at what the people in the loop did: they never wrote an answer, they only compared. The model learns what humans prefer from a <Hl>reward signal, not from correct labels</Hl>. That is the defining feature of reinforcement learning, and it is exactly what this past-paper question tests.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="noai2025" n={3} />

The effect is large. In OpenAI's InstructGPT study (2022), people preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained this way to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model over 100 times bigger. The goals are often summed up as **helpful, honest and harmless**. Newer methods do the same job more simply: **DPO** (direct preference optimisation) learns from the comparisons directly, without a separate reward model, and some labs replace some human judges with AI feedback guided by a written list of principles. In exam questions, "RLHF" or "alignment" names this stage.

<Mistake title="RLHF is not where truth comes from">

The reward model learns what labellers *like*, which is not always what is *true*. If confident, fluent answers get rewarded, the model learns to sound confident even when it is wrong. Try preferring the myth (answer C) in the visual above. This is one reason aligned LLMs still hallucinate (see [Limits, risks and responsible use of AI](#/learn/large-language-models/limits-and-responsible-ai)).

</Mistake>

## Scaling laws: why labs keep going bigger

<Analogy title="A well-planned training season">

A runner on a good training plan finds that each doubling of training effort brings a steady, predictable improvement in their timing. They cannot know exactly what race day holds, but they can plan the season. LLM labs plan their training runs the same way.

</Analogy>

In 2020, researchers at OpenAI trained many language models of different sizes and found that the pretraining loss falls **smoothly and predictably** as you scale up three things: the number of parameters $N$, the number of training tokens $D$, and the <Term def="The total amount of arithmetic used for training, counted in FLOPs (floating-point operations: single multiplications or additions of decimal numbers).">compute</Term> $C$. Each relationship is a <Term def="A relationship of the form y = a·x^(−k). Multiplying x by 10 always multiplies y by the same factor, 10^(−k). On log–log axes it is a straight line.">power law</Term>:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(C) &\approx a \cdot C^{-k} \\[4pt]
\log L &\approx \log a - k \log C
\end{aligned}
$$

- $L$ is the pretraining loss (lower is better) and $C$ is the compute used.
- $a$ and $k$ are constants measured from smaller experiments. The exponent $k$ is small (about 0.05 in that study).
- The second line is the first with logs taken of both sides: a straight line with slope $-k$ when you plot $\log L$ against $\log C$. So every 10× more compute multiplies the loss by the same factor, $10^{-k}$.

For a Transformer, compute is easy to estimate. Each parameter does about 6 floating-point operations per training token (about 2 in the forward pass and 4 in the backward pass), so

$$
C \approx 6\,N\,D \ \text{FLOPs}.
$$

<ScalingLawPlot />

In 2022, DeepMind's **Chinchilla** paper sharpened the picture: for a fixed compute budget, the best results come from growing the model and the data **together**, at about **20 training tokens per parameter**. Many earlier models were too big for their data. GPT-3 had 175 billion parameters but saw only 300 billion tokens, under 2 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla, with 70 billion parameters trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, used about the same compute as DeepMind's 280-billion-parameter Gopher and beat it.

<Steps title="Plan a training run with a budget of 1.2 × 10²² FLOPs">
  <Step title="Write D in terms of N">
    The rule of thumb says $D = 20N$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Substitute into C = 6ND">
    $1.2 \times 10^{22} = 6 \cdot N \cdot 20N = 120\,N^2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Solve for N">
    $N^2 = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{22}}{120} = 10^{20}$, so $N = 10^{10}$: a **10-billion-parameter** model.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Then find D">
    $D = 20 \times 10^{10} = 2 \times 10^{11}$, which is **200 billion tokens**. Check: $6 \times 10^{10} \times 2 \times 10^{11} = 1.2 \times 10^{22}$. ✓
  </Step>
</Steps>

Two warnings keep this honest. Real loss curves eventually flatten towards a floor, because some text is genuinely unpredictable. And "compute-optimal" only minimises the cost of *training*. A model that will answer millions of questions is cheaper to *run* if it is smaller, so labs now often train smaller models on far more than 20 tokens per parameter: the 8-billion-parameter Llama 3 saw about 15 trillion tokens, nearly 2,000 per parameter. Running costs are the subject of [Context windows, the KV cache and cost](#/learn/large-language-models/context-and-cost).

<Mistake title="Myth: a bigger model is always better">

Only if the budget grows with it. With the compute fixed, a model that is too big sees too few tokens and ends up *worse* than a smaller model trained on more data. Drag the budget in the plot and compare the bars: the biggest model is rarely the winner.

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

Facts NOAI-style questions lean on:

- **GPT = Generative Pre-trained Transformer**. LLMs are pretrained on massive text corpora from the internet, books and code.
- Pretraining is **self-supervised** (the label is the next token); SFT uses **human-written prompt–response pairs**; RLHF uses **human comparisons → reward model → reinforcement learning**.
- The order is pretraining → SFT → RLHF. A distractor that swaps two stages is wrong.
- Scaling laws: loss falls **smoothly, as a power law**, with parameters, data and compute: a straight line on log–log axes. Use $C \approx 6ND$ and Chinchilla's ≈ 20 tokens per parameter for quick estimates.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- An LLM is built in stages: **pretraining** (next-token prediction on trillions of tokens) → **supervised fine-tuning** (human-written example conversations) → **preference alignment** such as **RLHF** (human comparisons → reward model → reinforcement learning).
- Pretraining is **self-supervised**: the next token is a free label, so no human labelling is needed and the whole web can be used. Its loss is the average of $-\ln p(\text{real next token})$.
- Knowledge comes almost entirely from pretraining (about 98% of the compute); the later stages teach format, helpfulness and safety.
- RLHF learns from what people *prefer*. That makes models more helpful, but it rewards sounding right, not being right.
- **Scaling laws**: loss falls predictably (a power law, straight on log–log axes) as parameters $N$, tokens $D$ and compute $C \approx 6ND$ grow. For a fixed budget, grow model and data together (about 20 tokens per parameter).

</Takeaways>
