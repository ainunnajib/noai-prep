ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Llama are all built with the same short recipe: read an enormous amount of text, learn to follow instructions, then learn which answers people prefer. The recipe explains what these models are good at and why they fail in strange ways, and NOAI asks about every step, from "what does GPT stand for?" to "what are LLMs trained on?".

<Analogy title="From library reader to exam pro">

A student first reads the entire National Library without anyone testing them: **pretraining**. They know a lot but ramble. Next, in a short exam-skills course, they copy model answers written by teachers: **supervised fine-tuning**. Finally, teachers compare pairs of their answers, say which is better, and the student adjusts to earn more praise: **preference alignment**. Almost all the knowledge came from the reading; the course and the feedback shaped how it is used.

</Analogy>

## Three stages, one model

A <Term def="Large language model: a very large Transformer network (billions of weights) trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. GPT-4, Gemini, Claude and Llama are LLMs; ChatGPT is a chatbot built on GPT models.">large language model (LLM)</Term> is a decoder-only Transformer (the GPT design from [BERT, GPT and language modelling](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/bert-and-gpt)) with billions of <Term def="The numbers inside a network that training adjusts. 'Parameters' and 'weights' mean the same thing here. A '7B model' has about 7 billion of them.">parameters</Term>. The same network is trained in three stages, each with different data and a different goal. Step through the stages below and watch how the answers change.

<LLMTrainingStages />

<KeyIdea>

**Pretraining → supervised fine-tuning (SFT) → preference alignment (such as RLHF).** Pretraining gives the model its knowledge and language. The two later, much smaller stages teach it to follow instructions and give answers people prefer.

</KeyIdea>

## Stage 1: pretraining, or "guess the next token"

Text is first cut into <Term def="The pieces a language model reads and writes: whole words, parts of words or punctuation. A rough rule for English: 1 token ≈ 3/4 of a word.">tokens</Term> (see [tokenisation](#/learn/text-as-data/tokenisation)). Pretraining then asks, at every position of every document: *given the tokens so far, what comes next?* The model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary and is scored on the probability it gave the token that really came next. The training loss is the average of $-\ln p$ over all positions (the cross-entropy loss from [likelihood and entropy](#/learn/classification/likelihood-and-entropy)):

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \ln p_\theta\left(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}\right)
$$

- $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T$ are the $T$ tokens of a piece of text.
- $p_\theta(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$ is the probability that the model, with weights $\theta$ ("theta"), gives to the real token $x_t$ after seeing only the tokens before it. The bar $\mid$ reads "given".
- $-\ln p$ is near 0 when $p$ is near 1 (a confident, correct guess) and very large when $p$ is near 0. Averaging over the $T$ positions gives one number for gradient descent to push down.

In words: **make the real next token as likely as possible, everywhere.**

<NextTokenPairs />

Nobody labelled anything: the "right answer" for each example is simply the next token, already in the text. Training where the labels come from the data itself is called <Term def="Training where the labels are made automatically from the raw data (here, the next token of the text), so no human labelling is needed. That is what makes training on trillions of tokens possible.">self-supervised learning</Term>, and it is why pretraining can use most of the public web: Meta's Llama 3 was pretrained on over 15 trillion tokens.

You scored exactly this game by hand in [BERT, GPT and language modelling](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/bert-and-gpt) (where tokens were written $w_t$; here they are $x_t$): if the model gives the three real next tokens probabilities $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac18$, then $\mathcal{L} = \frac{\ln 2 + 2\ln 2 + 3\ln 2}{3} = 2\ln 2 \approx 1.39$, a perplexity of $e^{\mathcal{L}} = 4$. Each training step nudges the weights so that these probabilities rise and the average falls.

Why does such a simple game teach so much? Because predicting well *requires* knowledge: to continue "The capital of Japan is" the model must know the fact, and to continue a Python function it must follow the code. Over trillions of examples, the only way to keep the loss low is to absorb grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning.

What comes out is a <Term def="An LLM straight after pretraining, before any instruction tuning. It continues text in the style of its training data rather than answering like an assistant.">base model</Term>: a brilliant autocomplete. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions, because on the web questions often come in lists.

This stage gives GPT its name, which you met in the BERT and GPT lesson: **Generative Pre-trained Transformer**. *Generative* because it produces text token by token, *pre-trained* because of this first stage, *Transformer* because of the network design.

## Stage 2: supervised fine-tuning teaches the format

<Term def="Continuing to train an already-trained model on a smaller, more specific dataset so that it adapts to a new task or behaviour.">Fine-tuning</Term> means continuing to train an already-trained model on a smaller, focused dataset. In <Term def="Supervised fine-tuning: training a pretrained LLM on example prompts paired with ideal responses, using the same next-token loss. Also called instruction tuning.">supervised fine-tuning (SFT)</Term>, also called instruction tuning, the data is tens of thousands to a few million example conversations, each a prompt plus an ideal reply, written by people or generated by a stronger model and checked. Some examples are refusals, so an SFT model already declines many clearly harmful requests. Special tokens mark who is speaking:

<CodeBlock language="text" title="chat template" showLineNumbers={false} code={`<|user|>
Summarise this email briefly.
<|assistant|>
Meeting moved to Friday, 3 pm.
<|end|>`} />

The loss is the **same next-token loss**, usually counted only on the reply tokens. The model learns the *format* (a request after `<|user|>`, an answer after `<|assistant|>`) but few new facts. ([Fine-tuning and LoRA](#/learn/large-language-models/fine-tuning-and-lora) shows how to fine-tune cheaply.)

<Mistake title="Myth: fine-tuning is where the model gets its knowledge">

SFT data is tiny: a few million conversations at most, against trillions of pretraining tokens. If the base model never saw a fact, a small SFT set will not reliably add it, and trying can teach the model to make things up confidently.

</Mistake>

## Stage 3: learning from preferences (RLHF)

Writing a perfect answer is slow, and most prompts have no single correct reply, only better and worse ones. *Comparing* two answers is much easier, just as judging a cooking contest is easier than cooking, so the last stage learns from comparisons. The classic recipe is <Term def="Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare pairs of model answers, a reward model learns to predict their preferences, and the LLM is then trained with reinforcement learning to earn high reward.">RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)</Term>:

1. **Sample**: for many prompts, the SFT model writes several answers.
2. **Compare**: people pick the better answer in each pair.
3. **Train a reward model**: a separate network learns to give each answer a score $r$ so that preferred answers score higher. It models the chance that a person prefers answer A to answer B as $\sigma(r_A - r_B)$, where $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ is the [sigmoid](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression). Equal scores give $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, a coin flip; the bigger the gap, the more confident the preference.
4. **Optimise the LLM**: with <Term def="Learning by trial and error to maximise a reward signal, instead of copying correct answers. Here, the LLM writes answers and is updated to make high-reward answers more likely.">reinforcement learning</Term> (usually an algorithm called PPO; the idea is introduced in [Reinforcement learning](#/learn/ml-fundamentals/reinforcement-learning)), the LLM writes answers, the reward model scores them, and the LLM is updated to make high-scoring answers more likely. A penalty for drifting too far from the SFT model stops it producing odd text that merely fools the reward model.

<PreferenceToReward />

The people in the loop never wrote an answer; they only compared. The model learns from a <Hl>reward signal, not from correct labels</Hl>: the defining feature of reinforcement learning, and exactly what this past-paper question tests.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="noai2025" n={3} />

The effect is large: in OpenAI's InstructGPT study (2022), people preferred a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained this way to the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, and SFT plus RLHF cost under 2% of GPT-3's pretraining compute. The goals are often summed up as **helpful, honest and harmless**. A newer method, **DPO** (direct preference optimisation), learns from the same comparisons with an ordinary loss: no separate reward model and no reinforcement-learning loop.

Since 2024, **reasoning models** (such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1) add another reinforcement-learning stage. The model writes a long chain of working before its answer, and the reward comes from *checking the final answer automatically* (the maths answer matches, the code passes its tests), not from human preferences: reinforcement learning with **verifiable rewards**. Even so, the knowledge it reasons with still comes mainly from pretraining.

<Mistake title="RLHF is not where truth comes from">

The reward model learns what labellers *like*, which is not always what is *true*. If confident, fluent answers get rewarded, the model learns to sound confident even when it is wrong. Try preferring the myth (answer C) in the visual above. This is one reason aligned LLMs still hallucinate (see [Limits, risks and responsible use of AI](#/learn/large-language-models/limits-and-responsible-ai)).

</Mistake>

## Scaling laws: why labs keep going bigger

In 2020, OpenAI researchers trained language models of many sizes and found that the pretraining loss falls **smoothly and predictably** as three things grow: the number of parameters $N$, the number of training tokens $D$, and the <Term def="The total amount of arithmetic used for training, counted in FLOPs (floating-point operations: single multiplications or additions of decimal numbers).">compute</Term> $C$. Each relationship is a <Term def="A relationship of the form y = a·x^(−k). Multiplying x by 10 always multiplies y by the same factor, 10^(−k). On log–log axes it is a straight line.">power law</Term>:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(C) &\approx a \cdot C^{-k} \\[4pt]
\log L &\approx \log a - k \log C
\end{aligned}
$$

- $L$ is the pretraining loss (lower is better) and $C$ is the compute used.
- $a$ and $k$ are constants measured from smaller experiments. The exponent $k$ is small (about 0.05 in that study).
- Taking logs of both sides gives the second line: a straight line with slope $-k$ when you plot $\log L$ against $\log C$. So every 10× more compute multiplies the loss by the same factor, $10^{-k}$.

For a Transformer, compute is easy to estimate. Each parameter does about 6 floating-point operations per training token (about 2 in the forward pass and 4 in the backward pass), so

$$
C \approx 6\,N\,D \ \text{FLOPs}.
$$

Below, each coloured curve is one model size trained on more and more tokens: its loss falls, then levels off when the model is too small to learn more. The dashed line is the lowest loss *any* size reaches with each budget: it touches each curve where that size is the best choice, and every 10× more compute multiplies it by the same factor (about 0.84 here).

<ScalingLawPlot />

In 2022, DeepMind's **Chinchilla** paper sharpened the picture: for a fixed compute budget, the best results come from growing the model and the data **together**, at about **20 training tokens per parameter**.

<Reveal label="Going further: why Chinchilla changed how labs train">

Many earlier models were too big for their data: GPT-3 had 175 billion parameters but saw only 300 billion tokens, under 2 per parameter. Chinchilla (70 billion parameters, 1.4 trillion tokens) used about the same compute as DeepMind's 280-billion-parameter Gopher and beat it.

</Reveal>

<Steps title="Plan a training run with a budget of 1.2 × 10²² FLOPs">
  <Step title="Write D in terms of N">
    The rule of thumb says $D = 20N$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Substitute into C = 6ND">
    $1.2 \times 10^{22} = 6 \cdot N \cdot 20N = 120\,N^2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Solve for N">
    $N^2 = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{22}}{120} = 10^{20}$, so $N = 10^{10}$: a **10-billion-parameter** model.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Then find D">
    $D = 20 \times 10^{10} = 2 \times 10^{11}$, which is **200 billion tokens**. Check: $6 \times 10^{10} \times 2 \times 10^{11} = 1.2 \times 10^{22}$. ✓
  </Step>
</Steps>

Two warnings. Real loss curves eventually flatten towards a floor, because some text is genuinely unpredictable. And "compute-optimal" only minimises the cost of *training*: a model that will answer millions of questions is cheaper to *run* if it is smaller, so labs now often train small models on far more data (the 8-billion-parameter Llama 3 saw about 15 trillion tokens, nearly 2,000 per parameter). Running costs are the subject of [Context windows, the KV cache and cost](#/learn/large-language-models/context-and-cost).

<Mistake title="Myth: a bigger model is always better">

Only if the budget grows with it. With compute fixed, a model that is too big sees too few tokens and ends up *worse* than a smaller one trained on more data. Drag the budget in the plot and compare the bars: the biggest model is rarely the winner.

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

Facts NOAI-style questions lean on:

- **GPT = Generative Pre-trained Transformer**, pretrained on massive text corpora from the internet, books and code.
- The training signal at each stage: pretraining, the **next token** (self-supervised); SFT, **example prompt–response pairs**; RLHF, **human comparisons → reward model → reinforcement learning**.
- The order is pretraining → SFT → RLHF. A distractor that swaps two stages is wrong.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- An LLM is built in stages: **pretraining** on trillions of tokens → **supervised fine-tuning** on example conversations → **preference alignment** such as **RLHF**.
- Pretraining is **self-supervised**: the next token is a free label, so the whole web can be used. Its loss is the average of $-\ln p(\text{real next token})$.
- Knowledge comes mainly from pretraining; SFT and RLHF (or DPO) teach format, helpfulness and safety. **Reasoning models** add a further reinforcement-learning stage rewarded by automatically checked answers.
- RLHF learns from what people *prefer*. That makes models more helpful, but it rewards sounding right, not being right.
- **Scaling laws**: loss falls predictably (a power law, straight on log–log axes) as parameters $N$, tokens $D$ and compute $C \approx 6ND$ grow. For a fixed budget, grow model and data together (about 20 tokens per parameter).

</Takeaways>
