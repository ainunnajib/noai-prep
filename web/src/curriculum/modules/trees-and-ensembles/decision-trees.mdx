## Measuring impurity: Gini and entropy

Think of each node as a bag of sweets in two flavours. A bag of all-orange sweets is **pure**: you know what you will draw. A 50-50 bag is the most uncertain. Write $p_k$ for the **fraction** of the node's examples in class $k$ (so the fractions add up to 1). For the 32 flats, $p_\text{under} = 18/32$ and $p_\text{over} = 14/32$.

The **Gini impurity** of a node is

$$
G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2
$$

- $K$ is the number of classes (2 here);
- $p_k^2$ squares each class fraction, and $\sum$ adds the squares up;
- $\sum p_k^2$ is the chance that two examples drawn at random (with replacement) have the **same** class, so $G$ is the chance that they **differ**.

A pure node has one $p_k = 1$, so $G = 1 - 1 = 0$. A 50-50 node has $G = 1 - (0.5^2 + 0.5^2) = 0.5$, the largest possible with two classes.

You met **entropy** in [Likelihood and entropy](#/learn/classification/likelihood-and-entropy) as the average surprise of a distribution, in bits. For a node, the outcomes are its classes:

$$
H = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k
$$

- $\log_2 p_k$ is negative for $0 < p_k < 1$, so the minus sign in front makes $H$ positive;
- each class contributes $p_k \times (-\log_2 p_k)$, and a class with $p_k = 0$ contributes 0;
- roughly, $H$ is the smallest possible average number of yes/no questions needed to pin down the class (exactly so when the fractions are $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots$).

A pure node has $H = -(1 \times \log_2 1) = 0$. A 50-50 node is a fair coin, $H = 1$ bit, the largest entropy possible with two classes.

<KeyIdea>

Gini $= 1 - \sum p_k^2$ and entropy $= -\sum p_k \log_2 p_k$ both measure how mixed a node is. Both are **0 for a pure node** and **largest for an even mix** (0.5 and 1 bit with two classes).

</KeyIdea>

## Scoring a question: information gain

A question splits a node of $n$ examples into a **yes** side with $n_\text{yes}$ examples and a **no** side with $n_\text{no}$. The impurity after the split is the **weighted average** of the two sides:

$$
I_\text{after} = \frac{n_\text{yes}}{n}\, I(\text{yes}) + \frac{n_\text{no}}{n}\, I(\text{no})
$$

Here $I$ is whichever impurity you use (Gini or entropy), and each side is weighted by its share of the examples, so a big group counts more than a small one. The <Term def="How much a split lowers the impurity: impurity before the split minus the weighted impurity after it. The tree picks the question with the largest gain.">information gain</Term> is the drop:

$$
\text{gain} = I(\text{parent}) - I_\text{after}
$$

The tree tries every candidate question and keeps the one with the **largest gain**. (Strictly, "information gain" is the entropy name; with Gini it is also called the "Gini decrease", and scikit-learn calls both the *impurity decrease*.)

  </Step>

    The parent has $H = 1$ bit. Split A: both sides have $H(0.2) \approx 0.722$, so the information gain is $1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits. Split B: $1 - \tfrac{8}{10} \times H(\tfrac58) \approx 1 - 0.8 \times 0.954 = 0.237$ bits. Entropy also prefers A.

<Mistake>

Forgetting the weights. The plain average for split B is $(0 + 0.469)/2 \approx 0.234$, which looks *better* than split A's 0.32 and picks the wrong question. Always weight each side by its number of examples. Another trap: the gain is **parent minus after**, never the other way round; a gain is never negative.

</Mistake>

<Reveal label="Going further: why not just count mistakes?">

Scoring a split by how many examples its majority votes get wrong often cannot tell splits apart. Take 4 orange and 4 blue (Gini 0.5). Split X gives (3 orange, 1 blue) and (1 orange, 3 blue); split Y gives (2 orange, 4 blue) and (2 orange, 0 blue). Both make 2 mistakes, but Gini prefers Y: its gain is $0.5 - \tfrac68 \times 0.444 \approx 0.167$, against $0.5 - 0.375 = 0.125$ for X, because Y creates a pure group that later splits can build on.

</Reveal>

## Growing the whole tree

The full training algorithm (called <Term def="Classification And Regression Trees: the standard algorithm for growing binary decision trees by greedy best splits. scikit-learn's trees use an optimised version of it.">CART</Term>) just repeats the scoring you did by hand:

1. Put all the training examples in the root.
2. For **every feature** and **every threshold** (the midpoints between neighbouring sorted values, so $m$ distinct values give $m - 1$ thresholds), compute the gain. Keep the best question.
3. Split the examples into the yes and no children.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 inside each child, until a node is pure, reaches the maximum depth, or has too few examples to split.

This is <Term def="Making the choice that looks best right now, without looking ahead to how it affects later choices. Fast, but not guaranteed to find the best overall answer.">greedy</Term>: each question is the best one *right now*, with no look-ahead. Finding the best possible tree overall is far too slow, and greedy trees work well in practice.

A few consequences that exam questions love:

- **No feature scaling needed.** A split only uses the *order* of one feature's values, so changing units moves the threshold but sends the same flats each way.
- **Boundaries are axis-aligned.** A diagonal boundary, such as "price depends on area minus lease", needs a staircase of many small cuts.
- **Missing values can be handled.** CART can send a row with a missing value down a *surrogate split* (a backup question on another feature); XGBoost, LightGBM and scikit-learn's newer trees learn a default direction for missing values at each split instead.
- **Trees also do regression.** A <Term def="A decision tree that predicts a number. Each leaf predicts the average target of its training examples, and splits are chosen to reduce the mean squared error.">regression tree</Term> predicts a number: each leaf predicts the **average** of its training targets, and splits are chosen to reduce the **mean squared error** instead of Gini. This is scikit-learn's `DecisionTreeRegressor`, the building block of gradient boosting in the last lesson of this module, [Gradient boosting](#/learn/trees-and-ensembles/gradient-boosting).

## Depth and overfitting

If nothing stops it, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure. On real data that means building tiny boxes around single unusual examples, which is <Term def="When a model learns the noise and quirks of its training data instead of the general pattern, so it scores well on training data but worse on new data.">overfitting</Term>. It is like a rulebook with a special rule for every past case: perfect on the cases it has seen, useless for a new one. Slide the depth below and watch the two accuracy curves.

<TreeDepthOverfit />

What you saw is the bias-variance trade-off in one picture:

- A **shallow** tree (depth 1 or 2) cannot even trace the circle. Its errors are systematic: high <Term def="Error from a model being too simple to capture the real pattern. It shows up as poor accuracy on both training and validation data (underfitting).">bias</Term>.
- A **deep** tree fits every training point, noise included. Change the training sample and it changes completely: high <Term def="Error from a model being too sensitive to the particular training sample, so it changes a lot when the data changes. It shows up as a big gap between training and validation scores (overfitting).">variance</Term>.

You control a tree's complexity with <Term def="A setting chosen before training rather than learned from the data, such as max_depth for a tree.">hyperparameters</Term> (the scikit-learn names are in brackets):

- **maximum depth** (`max_depth`): at most this many questions on any path;
- **minimum leaf size** (`min_samples_leaf`): every leaf must keep at least this many training examples, so the tree cannot isolate single points;
- **minimum split size** (`min_samples_split`): a node needs at least this many examples to be split;
- **pruning** (`ccp_alpha`): grow a big tree, then cut back branches whose gain is too small to be worth the extra complexity.

Choose their values by checking accuracy on validation data (or with cross-validation), never on the training data, which always prefers the deepest tree.

<KeyIdea>

Deep trees have **low bias but high variance**: they memorise noise. Keep a single tree simple with `max_depth` or `min_samples_leaf`, or keep the deep trees and **average many of them**, which is what random forests do in the next lesson, [Bagging and random forests](#/learn/trees-and-ensembles/bagging-and-random-forests).

</KeyIdea>

<ExamTip>

Traps in NOAI tree questions: an option that averages the two children **without weights**; "train longer" as the fix for 100% training and much lower validation accuracy (limit `max_depth` or `min_samples_leaf` instead); "standardise the features first" (trees never need it); and a tree that "splits at random" (it picks the best threshold).

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- A decision tree asks yes/no questions about one feature at a time, so it splits the space into **rectangles**; each leaf predicts its majority class (or the average, for regression).
- **Gini** $= 1 - \sum p_k^2$ and **entropy** $= -\sum p_k \log_2 p_k$ are 0 for a pure node and largest for an even mix (0.5 and 1 bit with two classes).
- Each split maximises the **information gain**: parent impurity minus the **weighted** average impurity of the children, found by a greedy search over every feature and threshold.
- Trees need no feature scaling, but a full-depth tree **overfits** (low bias, high variance). Limit it with `max_depth`, `min_samples_leaf` or pruning, or average many trees.

</Takeaways>
