At the end of the last lesson, a fully grown tree memorised the noise: perfect on its training data, shaky on new data, and completely different whenever the training data changed a little. The fix sounds too simple to work: grow **lots** of deep trees, each on a slightly different version of the data, and let them vote. That is **bagging**. Add one extra twist and you get a **random forest**, one of the most reliable models for tables of data, and a favourite NOAI topic.

<Analogy title="Asking 100 friends where to eat">

Ask one friend for a dinner recommendation and you inherit her quirks: maybe she rates every place by its chilli level. Ask 100 friends and go with the most popular answer, and the quirks cancel out: what is left is food that most people genuinely like. There is one catch. If all 100 friends got their picks from the same viral food video, asking more of them adds nothing, because they all make the **same** mistake. Averaging works best when the opinions are **independent**.

</Analogy>

## Step 1: a different dataset for every tree

We only have one training set, so where do "slightly different versions" come from? We make them by resampling. A <Term def="A random sample of n examples drawn from a dataset of n examples WITH replacement, so some examples appear several times and others not at all.">bootstrap sample</Term> draws $n$ examples from the $n$ training examples **with replacement**: after each draw, the example goes back, so it can be drawn again. It is like drawing names from a hat and putting each name back before the next draw. Each bootstrap sample has the same size as the original data, but it is a different mix: some rows appear twice or more, and some rows are missing.

<BootstrapSampler />

How many rows get left out? You can work it out exactly.

<Steps title="The chance that a row is never drawn">
  <Step title="One draw">
    Each draw picks one of the $n$ rows, so the chance it misses a particular row (say row 7) is $1 - \frac{1}{n}$. With $n = 10$ that is $0.9$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="n independent draws">
    To be left out, row 7 must be missed by **every** draw, so multiply: $\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$. With $n = 10$: $0.9^{10} \approx 0.349$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Larger datasets">
    $n = 100$: $0.99^{100} \approx 0.366$. As $n$ grows, $\left(1 - \frac1n\right)^n$ gets closer and closer to $\frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368$, where $e \approx 2.718$ is <Term def="The mathematical constant e ≈ 2.718, the base of natural logarithms. One of its properties: (1 − 1/n)ⁿ approaches 1/e as n grows.">Euler's number</Term>.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The 63.2% rule">
    So about **36.8%** of the rows are left out of each bootstrap sample, and about $1 - 0.368 = $ **63.2%** of the distinct rows appear at least once. The left-out rows are called <Term def="The training rows that were not drawn into a particular tree's bootstrap sample (about 36.8% of them). That tree never saw them, so they can test it.">out-of-bag (OOB)</Term> rows for that tree.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>

A bootstrap sample draws $n$ rows from $n$ **with replacement**. It contains about **63.2%** of the distinct rows (some repeated) and leaves about **36.8%** out of bag.

</KeyIdea>

## Step 2: grow a tree on each sample and average

<Term def="Bootstrap AGGregatING: train the same kind of model on many bootstrap samples and combine their predictions by voting (classification) or averaging (regression).">Bagging</Term>, short for **b**ootstrap **agg**regat**ing** (Leo Breiman, 1996), is:

1. Draw $B$ bootstrap samples from the training data.
2. Grow one deep, unpruned tree on each sample. The trees are independent of each other, so they can be trained **in parallel**.
3. To predict, ask every tree. For classification take a **majority vote** (scikit-learn averages the trees' class probabilities instead, which almost always agrees); for regression take the **average**.

Why does this help? Each deep tree has low bias but high variance: its prediction wobbles depending on which noisy rows it happened to see. Averaging cancels the wobbles that differ from tree to tree and keeps what they agree on. Precisely, suppose every tree's prediction has <Term def="A measure of spread: the average squared distance of a quantity from its mean. Here, how much a tree's prediction changes from one training sample to another.">variance</Term> $\sigma^2$, and any two trees' errors have correlation $\rho$ (rho; 0 means unrelated, 1 means they always make the same error). Then the average of $B$ trees has variance

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\text{average of } B \text{ trees}) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2
$$

- If the trees were **independent** ($\rho = 0$), only the second term remains: $\frac{\sigma^2}{B}$. A hundred trees would cut the variance a hundredfold.
- If the trees are **correlated** ($\rho > 0$), the second term still shrinks as $B$ grows, but the first term $\rho\,\sigma^2$ does not depend on $B$ at all. It is a **floor** that more trees can never break through.

With $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $B = 100$: for $\rho = 0$ the variance is $0.01$, but for $\rho = 0.5$ it is $0.5 + \frac{0.5}{100} = 0.505$, barely half of one tree's.

<AveragingVariance />

<KeyIdea>

Bagging **reduces variance**, not bias: each tree is already flexible enough, and averaging stops it from chasing noise. How much it helps is limited by how **correlated** the trees are.

</KeyIdea>

## Step 3: random forests make the trees less alike

Bootstrap samples overlap a lot (each holds about 63% of the same data), so bagged trees end up similar. Worse, if one feature is very strong, almost every tree asks about it at the root, and their errors become highly correlated. A <Term def="An ensemble of decision trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample, where every split may only choose among a random subset of the features. Predictions are averaged or voted.">random forest</Term> (Breiman, 2001) adds one twist: **at every split, the tree may only choose among a random subset of the features**. The size of the subset is the hyperparameter `max_features`:

- for classification, scikit-learn's default is $\sqrt{p}$ features out of $p$ (for example, 4 out of 16);
- for regression, scikit-learn's default is all the features, which is plain bagging; values such as $p/3$ are common.

Each individual tree gets a little **worse**, since it sometimes cannot use the best feature. But the trees become **less correlated**, which lowers the floor $\rho\,\sigma^2$, so the **average** gets better. Try it on the noisy circle from the last lesson:

<ForestAverager />

Look at the accuracy curve: it rises quickly, then flattens. Adding trees **never makes a forest overfit more**, because each extra tree just makes the average a little more stable. It simply stops helping, while training time keeps growing in proportion to the number of trees. So `n_estimators` is not tuned to fight overfitting: pick "enough" trees (typically a few hundred) and stop when the validation or OOB score levels off. If a forest overfits, look instead at the trees themselves (`max_depth`, `min_samples_leaf`) or at `max_features`.

## Out-of-bag error: a free validation set

Remember that each tree never saw about 37% of the training rows. That gives a free way to estimate how the forest does on unseen data. For every training row, collect the predictions of only the trees for which that row was **out of bag**, and let those trees vote. Comparing these votes with the true labels gives the <Term def="The error of a bagged ensemble measured on its own training data, where each row is predicted only by the trees that did not see it during training. It estimates the test error without a separate validation set.">out-of-bag (OOB) error</Term>. Because no tree is ever tested on a row it trained on, the OOB error behaves like a validation error, and you did not have to set any data aside. Compare the two readouts in the visual above: with 100 trees the OOB accuracy lands within a few percentage points of the validation accuracy. (With only a handful of trees it is too pessimistic, because each row is judged by just the 37% or so of trees that did not see it.) In scikit-learn, pass `oob_score=True` and read `model.oob_score_` after fitting.

<Mistake>

Scoring the forest on its own training rows with **all** the trees is not the OOB score. Every tree has seen most of those rows, so that score is almost 100% and tells you nothing about new data. OOB uses, for each row, only the trees that never saw it.

</Mistake>

## Which features matter? Feature importance

A forest of hundreds of trees is hard to read, but it can still tell you which inputs it relies on. Think of crediting each football player by how many goals their passes led to. There are two common ways.

1. **Impurity importance** (mean decrease in impurity, MDI). Every split reduces the impurity: by $n\,I(\text{node}) - n_\text{yes}\,I(\text{yes}) - n_\text{no}\,I(\text{no})$, the weighted drop from the last lesson. Give that amount to the feature the split used, add it up over all the trees, and scale the totals to add up to 1. This is scikit-learn's `feature_importances_`. It is free to compute, but it is measured on the **training** data.
2. **Permutation importance.** Take held-out data, randomly shuffle one feature's column (which breaks its link to the target while keeping its values realistic), and measure how much the score drops. A big drop means the model really relies on that feature for new data. In scikit-learn: `sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance`.

<ImportanceBars />

What the toggles reveal are the two classic traps:

- **Correlated features share or hide credit.** Two copies of the same information split the impurity credit, so each looks half as important. Under permutation each copy can cover for the other, so both can look unimportant.
- **Impurity importance favours features with many distinct values.** A feature with many possible thresholds, such as an ID number, gives deep trees many chances to find a split that happens to fit noise. Permutation importance on held-out data is not fooled.

Importance also says nothing about **cause and effect**: it describes what this model uses, not what drives prices in the real world.

<ExamTip>

The NOAI favourites, in one place:

- **Bagging reduces variance**; boosting (next lesson) mainly reduces **bias**.
- Bagged trees are trained **in parallel** and independently; boosted trees are trained **in sequence**.
- **Random forest = bagging + a random subset of features at each split**, which decorrelates the trees.
- A bootstrap sample holds about **63.2%** of the distinct rows; the other **36.8%** are out of bag, and the **OOB error** estimates the test error for free.
- **More trees** give diminishing returns but do **not** cause overfitting.
- **Impurity importance** is biased towards high-cardinality features and splits credit between correlated features.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- A **bootstrap sample** draws $n$ rows from $n$ with replacement: about 63.2% distinct rows, about 36.8% out of bag.
- **Bagging** trains a deep tree on each bootstrap sample and votes or averages. It lowers variance by $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$: a lot when the trees are independent, only down to the floor $\rho\sigma^2$ when they are correlated.
- A **random forest** also restricts each split to a random subset of features (`max_features`), which makes the trees less alike and the average better.
- The **out-of-bag** score is a free estimate of test performance; adding trees gives diminishing returns but never more overfitting.
- **Feature importance** (impurity or permutation) shows what the model relies on; watch out for correlated features and for impurity importance's bias towards high-cardinality features.

</Takeaways>
