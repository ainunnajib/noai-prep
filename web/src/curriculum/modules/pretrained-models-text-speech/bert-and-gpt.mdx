In 2018 two models changed how AI handles language: OpenAI's **GPT** (June) and Google's **BERT** (October), both stacks of the [Transformer blocks](#/learn/attention-and-transformers/positional-encoding-and-blocks) you have just built. Neither was trained for a particular task. Each spent days to weeks of computing on a simple word game over hundreds of millions to billions of words, and that game taught it enough about language to be adapted to almost any task.

## Pretrain once, fine-tune many times

Both models follow the same two-stage recipe.

1. <Term def="Training a model on a huge amount of general data first, so that it learns broadly useful knowledge before it ever sees your task.">Pretraining</Term>: train on a huge pile of raw text. The game is designed so that **the text supplies its own answers**, so nobody has to label anything. This is called <Term def="Learning where the labels come from the data itself, for example hiding a word and using the real word as the answer. No human labelling is needed, so it scales to billions of examples.">self-supervised learning</Term>.
2. <Term def="Continuing to train a pretrained model on a small labelled dataset for your task, usually with a small learning rate, so it adapts without forgetting what it learned.">Fine-tuning</Term>: take the pretrained model, add a small task-specific layer if needed, and train briefly on a few thousand labelled examples.

A big lab pretrains once; everyone else downloads the result and fine-tunes it cheaply, as you will in the [Hugging Face lesson](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/hugging-face-in-practice). The two models differ in their game:

| | BERT | GPT |
|---|---|---|
| Full name | **B**idirectional **E**ncoder **R**epresentations from **T**ransformers | **G**enerative **P**re-trained **T**ransformer |
| Built from | encoder blocks only | decoder blocks only |
| Pretraining game | fill in hidden words (masked language modelling) | predict the next word (language modelling) |
| Each token can see | the whole sentence, both sides | only the tokens before it |

## BERT: fill in the blanks

<Analogy title="A cloze passage">

In an English paper's cloze passage you fill each blank using the words **before and after** it: "The train was ___ because of a signal fault." The words after the gap ("because of a signal fault") are often the best clue. BERT is pretrained by doing millions of cloze passages, made automatically by hiding words in Wikipedia and books.

</Analogy>

This game is called <Term def="BERT's pretraining task: hide some tokens of a sentence and train the model to predict the hidden tokens from all the other tokens, on both sides.">masked language modelling (MLM)</Term>. BERT replaces some <Term def="The pieces a tokeniser cuts text into: whole words, parts of words such as '##ing', or punctuation. Models read and predict tokens, not letters.">tokens</Term> with a special `[MASK]` token, runs the whole sentence through its encoder and guesses each hidden token. Because every token attends to every other token, it uses clues from **both sides** of each gap. Try it with two real models: BERT, and GPT-2, which only ever sees the words to the left.

<MaskedWordGame />

What you just saw, written as maths: at each hidden position BERT outputs a probability for each of its 30,522 vocabulary tokens, and the loss is the average <Term def="The loss for one prediction is −log of the probability the model gave to the correct answer: 0 if it was certain and right, large if it gave the right answer a small probability.">cross-entropy</Term> over the hidden positions only:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{MLM} = -\frac{1}{|M|} \sum_{i \in M} \log P\big(x_i \mid \tilde{x}\big)
$$

- $M$ is the set of positions that were chosen for prediction, and $|M|$ is how many there are.
- $x_i$ is the real token at position $i$ (the answer).
- $\tilde{x}$ is the corrupted sentence BERT actually sees, with the chosen tokens hidden.
- $P(x_i \mid \tilde{x})$ is the probability BERT gives the right token. The $-\log$ makes the loss small when that probability is near 1.

<KeyIdea>

BERT learns language by **filling in hidden words using the context on both sides**. No labels are needed: the hidden word itself is the answer.

</KeyIdea>

### The 15% and the 80/10/10 rule

BERT does not hide every word, and it does not always use `[MASK]`. In each sequence:

- **15%** of the ordinary tokens are chosen for prediction (`[CLS]`, `[SEP]` and padding are never chosen).
- Of the chosen tokens, **80%** are replaced by `[MASK]`, **10%** by a random token, and **10%** are left **unchanged**.
- All chosen tokens are predicted, whatever happened to them. Every other position gets the label $-100$, which tells PyTorch's cross-entropy to skip it.

Why not simply `[MASK]` all of them? Because `[MASK]` never appears later, in fine-tuning or real use, and a model tested only at `[MASK]` positions could learn to "work hard only where you see `[MASK]`". With the random and unchanged cases, **any** token might secretly be tested, so BERT must build a good representation of every token.

<MaskingSampler />

<Steps title="Counting masks in a batch of 2,000 tokens">
  <Step title="Choose 15%">
    $0.15 \times 2000 = 300$ tokens are chosen for prediction.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Split 80 / 10 / 10">
    $0.8 \times 300 = 240$ become `[MASK]`, $0.1 \times 300 = 30$ become a random token, and $30$ stay unchanged.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count the loss terms">
    The loss averages over all **300** chosen positions, not just the 240 `[MASK]` ones. The other $1700$ positions have label $-100$ and add nothing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Shortcut">
    The share of all tokens that become `[MASK]` is $0.15 \times 0.8 = 0.12$, so 12%.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Here is the recipe in PyTorch, run on a fake batch of 512 sequences so the proportions are easy to check:

```python
import torch

def mask_tokens(ids, mask_id, vocab_size, special_ids, p=0.15):
    chosen = torch.rand(ids.shape) < p         # about 15% of positions
    for s in special_ids:                      # never [CLS], [SEP], [PAD]
        chosen &= ids != s
    labels = ids.clone()
    labels[~chosen] = -100                     # score chosen positions only
    inputs = ids.clone()
    r = torch.rand(ids.shape)
    to_mask = chosen & (r < 0.8)               # 80%: [MASK]
    to_rand = chosen & (r >= 0.8) & (r < 0.9)  # 10%: a random token
    inputs[to_mask] = mask_id
    inputs[to_rand] = torch.randint(vocab_size, ids.shape)[to_rand]
    return inputs, labels                      # other 10%: unchanged

torch.manual_seed(0)
CLS, SEP, MASK, V = 101, 102, 103, 30522       # BERT's real ids
ids = torch.randint(1000, V, (512, 128))       # 512 fake sequences
ids[:, 0], ids[:, -1] = CLS, SEP
inputs, labels = mask_tokens(ids, MASK, V, special_ids=[CLS, SEP])

chosen = (labels != -100).float().mean().item()
masked = (inputs == MASK).float().mean().item()
print(f"chosen {chosen:.4f}, [MASK] {masked:.4f}")
print("first row labels:", labels[0, :8].tolist())
```

```text
chosen 0.1485, [MASK] 0.1186
first row labels: [-100, 6261, -100, 25588, -100, -100, -100, -100]
```

These are shares of **all** tokens: about 15% chosen (slightly less, as the 2 special tokens per row are never chosen) and 12% masked. In the labels, every unchosen position is $-100$.

<Mistake title="Three classic slips">

Putting $-100$ in the **input** instead of the labels (it is not a token id, and the embedding lookup crashes). Scoring **every** position instead of only the chosen 15% (the model then mostly learns to copy visible words). And thinking the 10% "unchanged" tokens are skipped: they are still predicted, which is the whole point.

</Mistake>

## [CLS]: one vector for the whole sentence

<Analogy title="The class monitor">

The class monitor listens to everyone, then reports the class's overall view to the teacher. BERT's `[CLS]` token sits at the front of every input, attends to every token in every layer, and its final vector is what the classifier hears.

</Analogy>

As in the [tokenisation lesson](#/learn/text-as-data/tokenisation), BERT's tokeniser adds `[CLS]` at the start and `[SEP]` at the end: `[CLS] the laksa was amazing [SEP]`. After 12 layers every token has a 768-number vector, so a batch of $B$ sentences of $T$ tokens gives an output of shape $(B, T, 768)$. To classify a sentence we keep only position 0, the `[CLS]` vector $h_\text{CLS}$, and feed it to a small **classification head**. The figure uses a real BERT fine-tuned for sentiment (it splits "laksa" into `la ##ks ##a`):

<CLSHead />

It gets "shiok" wrong: it learned English from Wikipedia and books and sentiment from film reviews, but never Singlish. Fine-tuning on local examples would fix that. Written as maths, a linear head for $k$ classes is:

$$
p = \text{softmax}\big(W\, h_\text{CLS} + b\big)
$$

- $h_\text{CLS}$ is the final `[CLS]` vector, 768 numbers.
- $W$ is a $k \times 768$ weight matrix and $b$ is a bias vector of length $k$. Together they turn 768 numbers into $k$ scores, called <Term def="The raw scores a model outputs before softmax. They can be any real numbers; softmax turns them into probabilities that add up to 1.">logits</Term>.
- softmax turns the $k$ logits into $k$ probabilities that add up to 1.

The head has $768k + k$ parameters: for 2 classes, $1536 + 2 = 1538$.

<Reveal label="Going further: the pooler and BERT's second game">

BERT's own head first passes $h_\text{CLS}$ through a small pretrained **pooler**, a 768→768 layer with tanh, as the figure shows; the formula above leaves it out to keep the maths simple. The pooler was trained by a second pretraining game, **next sentence prediction**: "does sentence B really follow sentence A?" That gave `[CLS]` a sentence-level training signal. Even so, the raw pretrained `[CLS]` vector is a weak sentence summary (averaging word vectors often does better), and fine-tuning is what makes it a good one for your task. RoBERTa later dropped this second game and still works well.

</Reveal>

You can fine-tune in two ways:

- **Fine-tune everything** (the usual choice): train the head *and* all 110 million BERT weights with a small learning rate ($2 \times 10^{-5}$ to $5 \times 10^{-5}$), so the pretrained knowledge is nudged rather than wiped out.
- **Freeze the encoder** (a *linear probe*): train only the head. Much cheaper, but usually less accurate.

In PyTorch the whole idea is one slice and one `nn.Linear`. Here a random tensor stands in for BERT's output:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

B, T, d, k = 4, 10, 768, 3      # batch, tokens, BERT-base width, classes
hidden = torch.randn(B, T, d)   # stand-in for BERT's last-layer output
cls_vec = hidden[:, 0, :]       # position 0 is [CLS]: shape (B, d)
head = nn.Linear(d, k)          # the classification head
logits = head(cls_vec)          # shape (B, k): one score per class
print("cls_vec:", tuple(cls_vec.shape), " logits:", tuple(logits.shape))
print("head parameters:", sum(p.numel() for p in head.parameters()))
```

```text
cls_vec: (4, 768)  logits: (4, 3)
head parameters: 2307
```

## GPT: predict the next token

<Analogy title="Predictive text, scaled up">

Your phone's keyboard suggests the next word as you type. GPT does the same thing, but it has practised on a large part of everything ever written online, and it looks at the whole conversation so far instead of the last word or two.

</Analogy>

A <Term def="A model that gives a probability to every possible next token, given the text so far. Multiplying these probabilities gives a probability for a whole sentence.">language model</Term> answers one question again and again: given the text so far, how likely is each possible next token? We write this as $P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})$, read "the probability of token $w_t$ **given** all the earlier tokens".

The visual below uses a tiny model you can check by hand. It was "trained" by counting 16 short sentences. A <Term def="A count-based language model that predicts the next word from only the previous one word: P(w | previous word) = count(previous word, w) / count(previous word).">bigram</Term> model looks at the previous word only: $P(\text{mrt} \mid \text{the})$ is the number of times "the mrt" appears divided by the number of times "the" is followed by anything. A **trigram** looks at the previous two words.

<NextTokenBars />

Multiplying these <Term def="A probability that something happens given that something else is already known, written P(A | B).">conditional probabilities</Term> gives the probability of a whole sentence. This is the **chain rule** of probability:

$$
P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})
$$

- $w_1, \dots, w_n$ are the $n$ tokens of the sentence, in order.
- $\prod$ means "multiply together", like $\sum$ means "add together".
- For $t = 1$ there is nothing before, so the first factor is just $P(w_1)$ (in practice, given a start token).

<Steps title="Chain rule by hand: P(i take the mrt to school .) with the bigram model">
  <Step title="Write one factor per token">
    $P(\text{i} \mid \langle s\rangle) \cdot P(\text{take} \mid \text{i}) \cdot P(\text{the} \mid \text{take}) \cdot P(\text{mrt} \mid \text{the}) \cdot P(\text{to} \mid \text{mrt}) \cdot P(\text{school} \mid \text{to}) \cdot P(. \mid \text{school})$
  </Step>
  <Step title="Read each factor from the counts">
    $\tfrac{6}{16} \cdot \tfrac{4}{6} \cdot \tfrac{4}{4} \cdot \tfrac{3}{14} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot \tfrac{5}{5}$
  </Step>
  <Step title="Cancel before multiplying">
    The 6s cancel and $\tfrac44 = \tfrac55 = 1$, leaving $\tfrac{4}{16} \cdot \tfrac{3}{14} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{2}{5} = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{3}{14} \cdot \tfrac{4}{15} = \tfrac{12}{840} = \tfrac{1}{70}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sanity check">
    $\tfrac{1}{70} \approx 0.0143$, which matches the visual. Every extra token multiplies by a number at most 1, so **extending a sentence can never raise its probability**. That is why raw sentence probabilities favour short texts, and why we compare models per token (perplexity, below).
  </Step>
</Steps>

GPT works the same way, but each factor comes from a Transformer reading all the earlier tokens, since most long contexts never appear twice and cannot be counted. Pretraining GPT means minimising the average cross-entropy $-\log P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ over every position. Like BERT's game, this is self-supervised: the next word is the label.

To write text, GPT runs the chain rule forwards: compute the next-token probabilities, pick a token, append it, and repeat. Generating one token at a time, each conditioned on everything generated so far, is called <Term def="Generating a sequence one token at a time, where each new token is predicted from all the tokens before it, including the ones the model just generated.">autoregressive</Term> generation. How to pick (always the most likely, or at random, and how random) is the topic of [decoding strategies](#/learn/large-language-models/decoding-and-sampling).

## Perplexity: how surprised is the model?

To compare language models, we use the probability they give to real text they have not seen, averaged per token. The **surprise** of one token is $-\log P$: 0 for a certain token, large for an unexpected one. <Term def="How surprised a language model is by a text: e raised to the average negative log-probability per token. Lower is better. It equals the number of equally likely options the model is effectively choosing between at each step.">Perplexity</Term> is $e$ raised to the average surprise. See it with real GPT-2 numbers:

<PerplexityMeter />

As a formula:

$$
\text{PPL} = \exp\!\left( -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} \ln P(w_t \mid w_{<t}) \right)
$$

- $N$ is the number of tokens scored.
- $\ln$ is the natural logarithm (base $e$), and $\exp(x)$ means $e^x$, which undoes it.
- The part inside the brackets is exactly the average cross-entropy loss, so **perplexity = $e^{\text{loss}}$**.
- With $\log_2$ the cross-entropy $H$ is in **bits**, and then $\text{PPL} = 2^{H}$: the base of the power must match the base of the log. So $H = 3$ bits gives $2^3 = 8$, not $e^3 \approx 20$.

**Lower is better**, and the best possible value is 1. If a model gave every token probability $\tfrac{1}{k}$, its perplexity would be exactly $k$: perplexity is "the number of equally likely tokens the model is effectively choosing between".

<Steps title="Perplexity without a calculator">
  <Step title="The probabilities">
    A model gives the three tokens of a sentence probabilities $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac18$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Surprises">
    $-\ln \tfrac12 = \ln 2$, $\ -\ln \tfrac14 = 2\ln 2$, $\ -\ln \tfrac18 = 3 \ln 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average">
    $\dfrac{\ln 2 + 2\ln 2 + 3\ln 2}{3} = \dfrac{6 \ln 2}{3} = 2 \ln 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Exponentiate">
    $e^{2 \ln 2} = 2^2 = 4$. The model was as unsure as picking among 4 equally likely tokens. (Shortcut: perplexity is $1 / $ the geometric mean of the probabilities, and the geometric mean of $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18$ is $\sqrt[3]{\tfrac{1}{64}} = \tfrac14$.)
  </Step>
</Steps>

In PyTorch, perplexity is just `loss.exp()`. Below is the actual GPT training loss: the logits at positions $0, \dots, T-2$ are compared with the tokens at positions $1, \dots, T-1$, the "shift by one". The model is untrained, so its perplexity (about 1,200) is a little worse than guessing uniformly among its 1,000 tokens (which would score exactly 1,000), because random initial logits are not perfectly flat.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
V, d, T = 1000, 64, 12                    # vocabulary, width, length
emb, pos = nn.Embedding(V, d), nn.Embedding(T, d)
block = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, nhead=4, batch_first=True).eval()
to_vocab = nn.Linear(d, V)

ids = torch.randint(V, (8, T))            # 8 sequences of 12 token ids
causal = nn.Transformer.generate_square_subsequent_mask(T)
h = block(emb(ids) + pos(torch.arange(T)), src_mask=causal, is_causal=True)
logits = to_vocab(h)                      # (8, 12, 1000)
loss = F.cross_entropy(
    logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, V),        # predictions at positions 0..10
    ids[:, 1:].reshape(-1),               # targets: the NEXT tokens, 1..11
)
print("logits:", tuple(logits.shape))
print(f"loss = {loss.item():.3f}, perplexity = {loss.exp().item():.0f}")
```

```text
logits: (8, 12, 1000)
loss = 7.089, perplexity = 1198
```

Notice that GPT's "decoder" block here is PyTorch's `TransformerEncoderLayer` with a causal mask. That is not a typo, and it leads to the most important idea of this lesson.

## The real difference is the mask

<Analogy title="Writing versus editing">

Writing a story one sentence at a time, without peeking ahead, is GPT: you only know what you have written so far. Editing a finished story, where you can read every page before changing a word, is BERT.

</Analogy>

Inside, BERT's encoder blocks and GPT's decoder blocks are almost the same: attention, feed-forward network, residuals, LayerNorm. What differs is the [attention mask](#/learn/attention-and-transformers/scaled-dot-product-attention). BERT uses **no causal mask** (only a padding mask, so nothing attends to `[PAD]`): every real token attends to every other, in both directions (*bidirectional*). GPT uses a **causal mask**: token $t$ may attend only to positions $1, \dots, t$, a lower-triangular pattern.

<EncoderVsDecoderMasks />

The causal mask is what makes next-token training honest: without it, GPT could see position $t+1$ and simply copy the answer. With it, one forward pass over a $T$-token text gives $T - 1$ prediction exercises (or $T$ with a start token in front), all computed in parallel. BERT's tokens already see the word they would be asked to predict, which is why BERT needs the `[MASK]` trick and **cannot generate text left to right**: it was never trained to continue a sentence.

| | Encoder-only (BERT family) | Decoder-only (GPT family) |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | full, bidirectional | causal, left to right |
| Pretraining | masked language modelling (+ next sentence prediction) | next-token prediction |
| Best at | **understanding**: sentiment and topic classification, named-entity tagging, finding the answer span in a passage, sentence embeddings | **generating**: chatbots, story and code completion, and any task you can phrase as a prompt |
| Examples | BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT, mBERT | GPT-2, GPT-3/4, Llama, Qwen, Gemma |

Large decoder-only models can also classify by prompting ("Is this review positive or negative?"), but a fine-tuned encoder is often far smaller, faster and cheaper for a fixed understanding task.

<ExamTip>

NOAI's favourite facts here: BERT = **bidirectional encoder, masked words**; GPT = **autoregressive decoder, next token, causal mask**; `[CLS]` = **pooled sentence vector for classification**; MLM = **15% chosen, 80/10/10**; perplexity = $e^{\text{average cross-entropy}}$ (or $2^{H}$ in bits), **lower is better**. A common distractor multiplies the probabilities when the question asks for perplexity, or the reverse.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- **BERT** (encoder-only) is pretrained by **masked language modelling**: choose 15% of tokens; 80% become `[MASK]`, 10% a random token, 10% unchanged; predict all chosen ones, using context on **both sides**. Unscored positions get label $-100$.
- The final **`[CLS]`** vector summarises the input; a linear head $\text{softmax}(W h_\text{CLS} + b)$ with $768k + k$ parameters classifies it. Fine-tune everything with a small learning rate, or freeze the encoder and train only the head.
- **GPT** (decoder-only) is a language model: $P(w_1, \dots, w_n) = \prod_t P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. It is trained to predict the next token and generates **autoregressively**.
- **Perplexity** $= \exp\big(\text{mean of } {-\ln P}\big) = e^{\text{loss}}$ (or $2^{H}$ with $H$ in bits): the effective number of choices per token. Lower is better; a model that guesses uniformly scores the vocabulary size.
- The **attention mask** is the key difference: full (bidirectional) for BERT, causal (lower-triangular) for GPT.

</Takeaways>
