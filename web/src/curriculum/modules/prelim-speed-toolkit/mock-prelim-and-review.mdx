Drills sharpen one skill at a time. The Prelim tests all of them at once, against a clock, for {PRELIM.minutes} minutes. A **mock** is how you find out what happens when everything meets the clock, and the **review** afterwards is where you actually get better. This lesson shows you how to sit a mock properly, how to turn every mistake into a specific fix, and how to tell whether your scores are really going up.

## Sit it like the real thing

<Analogy title="A full dress rehearsal">

Weeks before 9 August, the National Day Parade holds full dress rehearsals: the real start time, full costume, the real running order. Problems that never appear in a classroom run-through show up there: a costume change that takes too long, a segment that overruns by 40 seconds. A mock is your dress rehearsal for 19 February.

</Analogy>

A mock only tells you the truth if the conditions match the real Prelim. These are the rules:

1. **{PRELIM.questions} questions in {PRELIM.minutes} minutes**, in one sitting, with no pauses and no "five more minutes".
2. **{PRELIM.materials}** Put your phone in another room.
3. **Run the two-pass plan** from the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination): write the checkpoint numbers (25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150) in the corner of your scrap paper before you start.
4. **Answer every question.** {PRELIM.marking}, so a blank is always the worst choice.
5. **Use a desktop browser**, like the Google Form in your school lab. Try to sit your last mock on a Friday at 9:30 am, the real slot.
6. **Mark it only at the end**, never question by question.

Where to get a mock: this site's [Mock exam](#/mock) page builds a 150-question, 90-minute paper drawn evenly from the five domains, and can include real past-paper questions. AI Singapore's <a href={LINKS.selfAssessment}>self-assessment tool</a> is an interactive copy of the 2026 Prelim; its roughly 300 questions make two full-length mocks.

Try the format at small scale first: ten questions at exactly the real pace.

<KeyIdea>

A mock measures two things at once: what you know, and how fast you can use it. Only timed, closed-book conditions measure both; untimed practice measures only the first.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake>

Pausing a mock to check a formula, or quietly allowing yourself extra time. Your score then describes a different, easier exam, and you lose the most valuable data the mock gives you: where the time went.

</Mistake>

## The error log

<Analogy title="Watching the match footage">

A badminton player who loses a match does not just play more matches. They watch the footage and ask *why* each point was lost: footwork, a weak backhand, or tiredness in the third game. Each reason has a different training fix. Your mock mistakes work the same way.

</Analogy>

An <Term def="A list of every question you got wrong or only guessed right in a mock, with what happened and what kind of mistake it was.">error log</Term> is a list, kept after every mock, of each question you got wrong **or guessed** (a lucky guess hides a gap just as well as a wrong answer). For each one, write the question number and domain, your answer, the right answer, one line on what happened, and which of four types it was:

| Type | The test | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Knowledge gap** | At home, untimed and without notes, you still cannot do it | Study the lesson; re-test it after 3 days and after a week |
| **Misread** | You can do it, but you answered a different question (a missed NOT, max for min, swapped numbers) | Read the question line first; circle the key word |
| **Calculation slip** | Right method, wrong arithmetic (a sign, a $+1$, a dropped factor) | Speed drills; write every intermediate number |
| **Time pressure** | You rushed it, or never reached it | Pacing: checkpoints, and cap-and-flag hard questions |

Misreads and slips are both what people call "careless", but they need different fixes, so keep them apart.

<Steps title="Classifying one mistake in four questions">
  <Step title="Did you have normal time for it?">
    If you rushed it to catch up, or never read it and guessed in the final sweep, it is **time pressure**. Stop here.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Can you do it now, untimed and without notes?">
    If not, it is a **knowledge gap**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Did you answer the question that was asked?">
    Compare your answer with the question line. If you answered a different question, it is a **misread**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Otherwise">
    The method was right and a number went wrong: a **calculation slip**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>

Different mistakes need different fixes. Gaps need lessons, slips need drills, misreads need a reading routine, and time pressure needs pacing. More practice alone fixes none of the last two.

</KeyIdea>

### The review loop

<Steps title="One cycle, every week or two">
  <Step title="Sit">
    A full mock under the rules above.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Log">
    The same day: mark it, and log every wrong or guessed question with its type. This takes about 30 minutes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fix">
    Work through the log by type: lessons for the gaps, drills for the slips, the reading routine for misreads, pacing for time.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Re-test">
    Redo the logged questions after 3 days and again after a week, without looking at your notes. Remove an entry only when you get it right both times.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare">
    At the next mock, check whether the type you worked on has shrunk. If it has not, change the fix, not just the amount of practice.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Now try it on real questions. Give yourself **two minutes for all three**, then mark them and classify any miss.

## Tracking progress: score projection

<Analogy title="Your 2.4 km timings before NAPFA">

Before a NAPFA test, you might time your 2.4 km run every week. One slow run after a late night does not mean you are getting worse, and one fast run on a cool morning does not mean you are ready. It is the trend over several weeks that tells you whether the training is working.

</Analogy>

Mock scores behave the same way, for a reason you can calculate. Think of each question as a coin that lands "right" with probability $p$, your true accuracy. Over 150 such coins, the score varies from sitting to sitting with a <Term def="A measure of how far values typically fall from their average. About 95% of values land within 2 standard deviations of the average.">standard deviation</Term> of

$$
\text{SD} = \sqrt{150 \, p \, (1 - p)}
$$

- $150$ is the number of questions;
- $p$ is the chance you get any one question right, and $1 - p$ the chance you get it wrong;
- the square root turns the spread of all those coin flips into marks.

<Steps title="How much can one mock wobble?">
  <Step title="Pick a true accuracy">
    Say $p = 0.6$, so your true level is $0.6 \times 150 = 90$ marks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply">
    $150 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 36$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the square root">
    $\sqrt{36} = 6$. About 95% of sittings land within $2 \times 6 = 12$ marks of 90: anywhere from 78 to 102, from chance alone.
  </Step>
</Steps>

So never judge your preparation by one mock. A drop of 6 marks is noise. Judge the trend line through four or more mocks: a slope that stays negative over several weeks, or a drop of more than about 17 marks between two mocks, is worth acting on.

Two more numbers belong in the projection. The first is **how many questions you reached**. A random guess on a four-option question is right with probability $\frac{1}{4}$, so each blank you guess is worth $\frac{1}{4}$ of a mark on average: 20 unreached questions guessed in the final sweep add $20 \times \frac{1}{4} = 5$ expected marks. The second is your **trend**: a line through your last few mock scores, which you can extend to 19 February.

<ExamTip>

There is no published pass mark. {PRELIM.advance}. You cannot aim for a fixed number, so aim to raise your trend and shrink your biggest error type each cycle. A sensible rhythm, if you have about 18 weeks: a full mock every two weeks until the December holidays, weekly in January, and two a week in the final three weeks, with the last one on a Friday at 9:30 am.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Sit mocks under real conditions: {PRELIM.questions} questions, {PRELIM.minutes} minutes, no calculator or notes, two-pass pacing, every question answered, marked only at the end.
- Log every wrong or guessed question with one of four types: knowledge gap, misread, calculation slip or time pressure.
- Each type has its own fix: lessons, a reading routine, drills or pacing. "More practice" alone does not fix misreads or time pressure.
- One mock wobbles by about $\pm 2\sqrt{150\,p(1-p)} \approx \pm 12$ marks from chance alone, so judge the trend over several mocks, and guessing every blank is worth $\frac14$ of a mark each.

</Takeaways>
