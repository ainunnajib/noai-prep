When a matrix acts on the plane, almost every arrow gets knocked off its course. A few special directions do not turn at all: the matrix only stretches them. These are the matrix's **eigenvectors** (from the German *eigen*, "own": the matrix's own directions), and the stretch factors are its **eigenvalues**. They are behind PCA, Google's PageRank and the exploding gradients of deep networks, and NOAI asks about them every year.

<Analogy title="Stretching prata dough">

A prata seller grabs a ball of dough by its left and right edges and pulls. Suppose you had drawn some straight lines on it first. A line drawn left to right stays left to right: it just gets longer. A line drawn top to bottom stays top to bottom too (it shrinks a little). But a line drawn diagonally gets twisted round towards the direction of the pull. The two directions that do not twist are the eigenvectors of the stretch, and *how much* each one gets longer or shorter is its eigenvalue.

</Analogy>

## Arrows that turn, and arrows that don't

Recall from the first lesson of this module that a matrix moves every point of the plane. Take

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and feed it three arrows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt]
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt]
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first arrow pointed along the x-axis and came out pointing up and to the right: it was turned. The second came out pointing in exactly the same direction, just 3 times as long. The third came out unchanged. Those last two are eigenvectors of $A$. Go hunting for them yourself:

## The definition: $Av = \lambda v$

<KeyIdea>

A non-zero vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of the square matrix $A$ if

$$
Av = \lambda v
$$

for some number $\lambda$, which is called its **eigenvalue**. In words: multiplying $v$ by the whole matrix gives the same answer as multiplying it by one ordinary number, so $A$ only stretches $v$ along its own line.

</KeyIdea>

Symbol by symbol:

- $A$ is a square matrix ($n \times n$). Only square matrices have eigenvectors, because $Av$ must live in the same space as $v$ to be compared with it.
- $v$ is a non-zero vector: the direction that does not turn.
- $\lambda$ (the Greek letter *lambda*) is a single number, the <Term def="The number λ in Av = λv: the factor by which the matrix stretches its eigenvector. It can be negative (a flip) or zero (a squash).">eigenvalue</Term>: the stretch factor along that direction.

What the value of $\lambda$ tells you:

- $\lambda > 1$: $v$ is stretched. $0 < \lambda < 1$: it is shrunk. $\lambda = 1$: it is left exactly as it was.
- $\lambda < 0$: $v$ is flipped to point backwards along the same line (and scaled by $|\lambda|$). Try the **Flip** preset above: it has $\lambda = -1$.
- $\lambda = 0$: $v$ is squashed to the zero vector. Try **Squash**.

Two pieces of small print:

1. **Eigenvectors come as whole lines.** If $Av = 3v$ then $A(2v) = 2Av = 6v = 3(2v)$, so $2v$, $-v$ and $0.5v$ are eigenvectors too, all with the same $\lambda = 3$. That is why we speak of eigen-*directions*, and why NumPy just reports one of length 1.
2. **The zero vector never counts.** $A0 = 0 = \lambda 0$ is true for *every* number $\lambda$, which would make the idea meaningless.

Not every matrix has real eigenvectors. A rotation by 90° turns every arrow, so it has none (its eigenvalues turn out to be complex numbers, which you will not need for NOAI). A <Term def="A matrix that slides each row of the grid sideways by an amount proportional to its height, like pushing the top of a stack of books.">shear</Term> such as $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has only one eigen-line, the x-axis. At the other extreme, the identity matrix $I$ leaves every arrow alone, so every non-zero vector is an eigenvector with $\lambda = 1$.

<Note title="The roundabout's centre pole">

A spinning roundabout turns every horizontal direction, just as a 2-D rotation does. But in 3-D its centre pole stays exactly where it is. That is why every 3-D rotation has a real eigenvector, its axis, with eigenvalue 1.

</Note>

## Finding the eigenvalues

Hunting with a slider works for a picture, but in an exam you need a method. Start from the definition and move everything to one side:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av &= \lambda v \\
Av - \lambda I v &= 0 \\
(A - \lambda I)\,v &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

We wrote $\lambda v$ as $\lambda I v$ because you cannot subtract a plain number from a matrix, but you can subtract the matrix $\lambda I$ (the identity with $\lambda$ down its diagonal).

Now read the last equation geometrically. The matrix $A - \lambda I$ sends a **non-zero** vector $v$ to the zero vector, so it squashes a whole direction flat. From the module *Vectors and matrices*, that means it is <Term def="A square matrix with determinant 0. It squashes space into fewer dimensions and has no inverse.">singular</Term>:

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0
$$

For a 2×2 matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ this is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det\begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{bmatrix} &= (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc \\
&= \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a quadratic in $\lambda$. Look at its coefficients: $a + d$ is the <Term def="The sum of the entries on the main diagonal of a square matrix, written tr(A).">trace</Term> of $A$, and $ad - bc$ is its determinant. So the eigenvalues of any 2×2 matrix solve

$$
\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A)\,\lambda + \det A = 0
$$

<Steps title="Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$">
  <Step title="Trace and determinant">
    $\operatorname{tr} A = 2 + 2 = 4$ and $\det A = 2 \times 2 - 1 \times 1 = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Solve the quadratic">
    $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$ factorises as $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3) = 0$, so $\lambda = 3$ or $\lambda = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Eigenvector for $\lambda = 3$">
    $A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its first row says $-x + y = 0$, so $y = x$: any multiple of $[1, 1]$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$, and check">
    $A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ gives $x + y = 0$: any multiple of $[1, -1]$. Check: $A[1, -1] = [2 - 1,\ 1 - 2] = [1, -1]$. ✓ These match the two eigen-lines you hunted for with the **Stretch** preset.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Shortcut: diagonal and triangular matrices

A <Term def="A square matrix with only zeros below the main diagonal (upper triangular) or only zeros above it (lower triangular).">triangular matrix</Term> has only zeros below (or above) its main diagonal. Then $A - \lambda I$ is triangular too, and its determinant is just the product of its diagonal:

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ 0 & d - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda)
$$

This is zero exactly when $\lambda = a$ or $\lambda = d$. **The eigenvalues of a diagonal or triangular matrix are its diagonal entries**, read off with no working at all. For example, $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $4$ and $-1$; the 7 makes no difference to them.

<Mistake>

Reading eigenvalues off the diagonal of a matrix that is **not** triangular. $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has 2 and 2 on its diagonal, but its eigenvalues are 3 and 1. A quick self-check catches this: if the eigenvalues were 2 and 2, their product would be 4, but $\det A = 3$.

</Mistake>

## Eigenvalues, determinant and trace

Here is the most useful fact in this lesson for NOAI. If the two roots of the quadratic are $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, it factorises as

$$
(\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2
$$

Matching the coefficients with $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A)\lambda + \det A$:

$$
\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr} A \qquad\qquad \lambda_1 \lambda_2 = \det A
$$

There is a picture for the product too. The determinant is the factor by which a matrix scales areas. Along its eigenvectors the matrix does nothing but stretch, by $\lambda_1$ one way and $\lambda_2$ the other, so a parallelogram built on the eigenvectors has its area multiplied by $\lambda_1 \times \lambda_2$. It is the photocopier from the determinant lesson: stretch the width by 2 and the height by 3, and every area grows by $2 \times 3 = 6$.

<EigenBars />

<KeyIdea>

**Sum of the eigenvalues = trace. Product of the eigenvalues = determinant.** This holds for square matrices of every size (an $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues, counting repeats and any complex ones), so many eigenvalue questions can be answered from the diagonal and the determinant alone.

</KeyIdea>

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={16} />

A big consequence: **a zero eigenvalue means the matrix is singular.** Algebraically, if any $\lambda_i = 0$ then the product $\det A = 0$. Geometrically, $Av = 0 \cdot v = 0$ for some non-zero $v$, so two different inputs ($0$ and $v$) give the same output, and no inverse could know which one to return. It works the other way round as well: if $\det A = 0$, then $\lambda = 0$ solves $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.

<ExamTip title="Eigenvalue facts that save time">

With about 36 seconds per question, never solve for eigenvalues if a shortcut exists:

| Situation | Shortcut |
|---|---|
| Diagonal or triangular matrix | Eigenvalues = the diagonal entries |
| Any square matrix | Sum = trace, product = determinant |
| An eigenvalue is 0 | $\det A = 0$, so $A$ is singular (not invertible) |
| $Av = \lambda v$ | $A^2 v = A(\lambda v) = \lambda^2 v$, so $A^k$ has eigenvalues $\lambda^k$ |
| $Av = \lambda v$, $\lambda \neq 0$ | Multiply both sides by $A^{-1}$ and divide by $\lambda$: $A^{-1}v = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}v$ |
| $Av = \lambda v$ | $(A + cI)v = \lambda v + cv$, so the eigenvalues all shift by $c$ |
| Symmetric matrix ($A = A^T$) | Eigenvalues are always real and eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular (you will need this for PCA) |

In the last four rows the eigenvectors stay the same; only the eigenvalues change.

</ExamTip>

## Multiplying again and again

What happens if you apply the same matrix over and over? Write the starting arrow $[1, 0]$ as a mix of the two eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Each multiplication by $A$ scales the first part by 3 and the second part by 1, so after $k$ steps

$$
A^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac12 \cdot 3^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12 \cdot 1^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

giving $[2, 1]$, then $[5, 4]$, $[14, 13]$ and $[41, 40]$. The $3^k$ part grows so much faster that the arrow swings onto the $[1, 1]$ line while its length grows roughly 3 times per step. In general, the direction ends up along the eigenvector whose eigenvalue is largest in size, the <Term def="The eigenvalue with the largest absolute value |λ|. Repeated multiplication by the matrix lines vectors up with its eigenvector.">dominant eigenvalue</Term> $\lambda_1$, as long as the starting arrow has some part along it. The closer $|\lambda_2|$ is to $|\lambda_1|$, the slower the swing.

<PowerIteration />

This simple loop matters in AI:

- **Power iteration.** Multiply, rescale to length 1, repeat: you get the dominant eigenvector without solving any equation. Google's original PageRank scored web pages using the dominant eigenvector of a huge matrix describing how a random surfer clicks from page to page, computed in exactly this way.
- **Exploding and vanishing gradients.** A recurrent network multiplies its hidden state by the same weight matrix at every time step, and backpropagation multiplies gradients by the transposed matrix (which has the same eigenvalues) on the way back. Over 50 steps, a direction with $|\lambda| = 1.1$ grows about $1.1^{50} \approx 117$ times, while $|\lambda| = 0.9$ shrinks it to $0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$ of its size. That is why careful initialisation tries to keep these factors close to 1.

## In code

`np.linalg.eig` returns the eigenvalues and a matrix whose **columns** are the eigenvectors, each scaled to length 1. The sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary (both ends of the line are equally good), so do not worry if you get $[-0.71, -0.71]$ instead of $[0.71, 0.71]$. For symmetric matrices, `np.linalg.eigh` is faster and always returns real values, sorted from smallest to largest.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0]])

vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
# keep the real parts (they are all real here)
vals, vecs = vals.real, vecs.real
print("eigenvalues:", vals)
print("eigenvectors are the COLUMNS of:")
print(vecs.round(4))

# the eigenvector that goes with vals[0] is a COLUMN
v = vecs[:, 0]
print("A @ v      =", (A @ v).round(4))
print("lambda * v =", (vals[0] * v).round(4))

print("sum:    ", vals.sum().round(6), "  trace:", np.trace(A))
print("product:", vals.prod().round(6), "  det:  ", np.linalg.det(A).round(6))

# Power iteration: multiply, rescale to length 1, repeat
x = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
for k in range(1, 7):
    x = A @ x
    x = x / np.linalg.norm(x)
    print(k, x.round(4))
# x is now (almost) a unit eigenvector,
# so x . (A x) is (almost) its eigenvalue
print("biggest eigenvalue is about", (x @ A @ x).round(4))`} />

The power-iteration loop is a typical "implement it from scratch" task for the Final round. In PyTorch the same tools are `torch.linalg.eig`, `torch.linalg.eigh` and `torch.linalg.eigvals`.

<Takeaways>

- An eigenvector is a non-zero vector that $A$ only stretches: $Av = \lambda v$. The eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the stretch factor; negative means a flip, zero means a squash.
- Eigenvalues solve $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$; for a 2×2 matrix that is $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A)\lambda + \det A = 0$.
- Diagonal or triangular matrix: the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.
- Sum of eigenvalues = trace, product = determinant, so an eigenvalue of 0 means the matrix is singular.
- Multiplying by $A$ again and again lines vectors up with the dominant eigenvector and scales lengths by about $|\lambda_1|$ per step: the root of power iteration and of exploding or vanishing gradients.

</Takeaways>
