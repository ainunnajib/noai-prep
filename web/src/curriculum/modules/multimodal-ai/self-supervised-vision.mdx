Every photo in ImageNet's 1.28 million training images needed a person to label it, and that took years of crowd-work. Meanwhile, billions of photos sit on the internet with no labels at all. The last lesson showed that Vision Transformers are data-hungry. So can a network learn to *see* from unlabelled photos alone? Yes: **self-supervised learning** makes the labels out of the data itself. It is how GPT and BERT learned language, and it is how today's best vision encoders are pretrained.

<Analogy title="Cloze passages">

In an English paper, a cloze passage is a text with words blanked out, and you fill in the blanks. A teacher can make one from *any* book in seconds, without writing a single answer key, because the missing words *are* the answers. To fill the blanks well you must understand grammar and meaning. Self-supervised learning turns unlabelled photos into cloze passages for a neural network.

</Analogy>

## Labels for free: pretext tasks

In ordinary <Term def="Learning from examples that each come with a correct answer (a label) provided by people, such as photos tagged 'cat' or 'dog'.">supervised learning</Term>, every input $x$ comes with a label $y$ that a person provided. In <Term def="Learning in which the training labels are made automatically from the data itself, for example by hiding part of an input and asking the model to predict it. No human labelling is needed.">self-supervised learning</Term> (SSL), a short program takes an unlabelled image, changes it, and keeps a record of what it did. The changed image is the input, and the record is the label. The made-up problem is called a <Term def="A made-up training task whose answers come free with the data (e.g. predict the rotation, fill in missing patches). We do not care about the task itself, only about the features the network learns while solving it.">pretext task</Term>. Try four classic ones.

<PretextTasks />

Nobody cares how well the network predicts rotations. The point is what it has to learn on the way: to tell which way is up, it must recognise skies, paws and wheels. After this **pretraining**, we keep the network's body (the <Term def="The part of a network that turns an input into a vector of features (an embedding). After self-supervised pretraining it is reused for other tasks.">encoder</Term>) and reuse it for the task we actually care about, the <Term def="The real task you want to solve after pretraining, usually with a small labelled dataset (e.g. classifying 10 kinds of hawker food).">downstream task</Term>. There are two common ways to reuse it:

- a <Term def="An evaluation in which the pretrained encoder is frozen and only a single linear layer is trained on its features with labels. High accuracy means the features already separate the classes.">linear probe</Term>: freeze the encoder and train only one linear layer on top, using the labelled training data;
- **fine-tuning**: train the whole network a little further on the labelled data, starting from the pretrained weights.

You have already met self-supervision in language: GPT predicts the next word, BERT fills in hidden words, and word2vec predicts a word's neighbours. All three learn from raw text with no human labels. For audio, HuBERT predicts hidden sound units in the same way ([Speech models](#/learn/pretrained-models-text-speech/speech-models)). For images, early pretext tasks like rotation and jigsaw worked, but the features they learned were tied to those particular tricks. Two families now dominate: **contrastive learning** and **masked image modelling**.

## Contrastive learning: same photo or not?

<Analogy title="Spotting your classmates">

On the first day of school you see your classmates in uniform. Then you meet them at a hawker centre in their own clothes, under different lighting, half hidden behind a pillar, and you still know who is who. Nobody gave you a list of names: you simply learned what stays the same about each person when everything else changes.

</Analogy>

<Term def="Self-supervised learning that trains an encoder to give similar embeddings to two views of the same thing (a positive pair) and different embeddings to views of different things (negative pairs).">Contrastive learning</Term> turns that into a training rule. The best-known recipe, **SimCLR** (Google, 2020), works like this.

<Steps title="One SimCLR training step">
  <Step title="Take a batch of N unlabelled images">
    For example $N = 256$ photos. No labels anywhere.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Augment each image twice">
    Apply two independent random <Term def="Random changes to an image that keep what it shows, such as cropping, flipping, rotating a little, or changing brightness and colour.">augmentations</Term> (as in [transfer learning](#/learn/vision-tasks/transfer-learning-and-augmentation): random crop and resize, flip, colour jitter, blur) to every image. That gives $2N$ views. Two views of the same image form a <Hl>positive pair</Hl>; a view of one image and a view of a different image form a <Hl>negative pair</Hl>.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Encode every view">
    An encoder $f$ (a ResNet or a ViT) turns each view into a feature vector $\mathbf{h}$. A small <Term def="A small MLP after the encoder, used only during contrastive training. Its output z goes into the loss; it is thrown away afterwards and the encoder's output h is used for downstream tasks.">projection head</Term> $g$ maps $\mathbf{h}$ to $\mathbf{z}$, which is scaled to length 1.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Play 'find my partner'">
    For each view, compare its $\mathbf{z}$ with the other $2N - 1$ views. The loss rewards the model when its partner (the other view of the same image) is the most similar, and penalises it when a negative is similar.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Notice *why* the pairs come together: the encoder learns to ignore brightness and zoom, because those are exactly what the augmentations changed. **The choice of augmentations decides what the features ignore.** The SimCLR authors found random cropping plus colour jitter mattered most. Without colour jitter, the network can cheat: two crops of the same photo share the same colours, so it matches colour histograms instead of learning shapes.

### The loss, symbol by symbol

SimCLR's loss is called **NT-Xent** (normalised temperature-scaled cross-entropy), a form of the **InfoNCE** loss. For a view $i$ whose partner is view $j$:

$$
\ell_{i} = -\log \frac{\exp\big(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_j)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp\big(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_k)/\tau\big)}
$$

- $\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \dfrac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert}$ is the <Term def="The dot product of two vectors divided by their lengths: the cosine of the angle between them. 1 means same direction, 0 means at right angles, −1 means opposite.">cosine similarity</Term>, between $-1$ and $1$;
- $\tau$ (tau) is the <Term def="A positive number that divides the similarities before the softmax. Small values make the softmax sharper (one candidate takes almost all the probability); large values make it flatter.">temperature</Term>, the same idea as the sampling temperature $T$ in [Decoding](#/learn/large-language-models/decoding-and-sampling); contrastive-learning papers write it $\tau$;
- the sum on the bottom runs over all $2N - 1$ other views: the 1 positive plus $2N - 2$ negatives;
- $\exp(x)$ means $e^x$, and $\log$ here is the natural log, $\ln$ (as in the worked example below).

In words: turn the similarities into a softmax over "who is my partner?", and take minus the log of the probability given to the true partner. It is ordinary cross-entropy in which the "correct class" is the partner. The loss is near 0 when the partner is by far the most similar view, and large when some negative is closer. The total loss averages $\ell_i$ over all $2N$ views.

<KeyIdea>

Contrastive learning pulls the two augmented views of the same image together and pushes views of different images apart. The "label" is simply *which views came from the same photo*, which the program knows for free.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="A tiny batch by hand: 2 images, 4 views, τ = 0.5">
  <Step title="The embeddings">
    Cat views $\mathbf{a} = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{a}' = (0.8, 0.6)$; car views $\mathbf{b} = (0, 1)$ and $\mathbf{b}' = (-0.6, 0.8)$. All have length 1 (check: $0.8^2 + 0.6^2 = 1$), so cosine similarity is just the dot product.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Similarities from view a">
    $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a}' = 0.8$ (positive), $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}' = -0.6$ (negatives).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Divide by τ and exponentiate">
    $0.8 / 0.5 = 1.6$, $0 / 0.5 = 0$, $-0.6 / 0.5 = -1.2$. Using $e^{1.6} \approx 4.95$, $e^{0} = 1$ and $e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Softmax: probability of the partner">
    $\dfrac{4.95}{4.95 + 1 + 0.30} = \dfrac{4.95}{6.25} \approx 0.79$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Loss for view a">
    $\ell_{\mathbf{a}} = -\ln 0.79 \approx 0.23$. To lower it, training moves $\mathbf{a}'$ closer to $\mathbf{a}$ and pushes the negatives $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{b}'$ away, $\mathbf{b}$ more strongly (about 3 times) because it is the more similar, harder negative: its probability is $1/6.25 \approx 0.16$ against $0.30/6.25 \approx 0.05$ for $\mathbf{b}'$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Temperature matters. With the same vectors but $\tau = 1$, the partner gets only $0.59$ of the probability (loss $0.53$); with $\tau = 0.1$ it gets almost all of it. A low temperature makes the softmax sharp, so the loss concentrates on the few negatives that are most similar (the "hard" ones). Typical values are between about $0.05$ and $0.5$.

Contrastive learning needs **many negatives** to work well, because "be different from 3 random photos" is too easy. SimCLR used batches of 4,096 images, so each view had $2 \times 4096 - 2 = 8190$ negatives. MoCo, another method, keeps a queue of recent embeddings so it can use many negatives without a huge batch.

<Mistake title="A positive pair is NOT 'two pictures of the same class'">

The model never knows classes. A positive pair is two augmented views of **one and the same photo**. Two *different* cat photos in the same batch count as a negative pair, even though both are cats. This sounds wrong, but it rarely matters: on ImageNet (1,000 classes) with SimCLR's batch of 4,096 images, only about 8 of a view's 8,190 negatives (0.1%) come from the same class, and the encoder still learns that cats resemble cats. (Using labels to choose positives is a different, supervised method.)

</Mistake>

The same idea powers CLIP, the next lesson, where a positive pair is a photo and its caption.

## Masked image modelling: fill in the blanks

BERT learns language by hiding about 15% of the words in a sentence and predicting them. **Masked image modelling** does the same for pictures, and a ViT makes it natural: patches are tokens, so you can hide tokens. The best-known method is the <Term def="Masked AutoEncoder (He et al., 2021): a ViT is pretrained by hiding 75% of the image patches and reconstructing their pixels from the visible 25%.">masked autoencoder (MAE)</Term>.

<Steps title="One MAE training step (ViT-B/16, 224 × 224 image)">
  <Step title="Cut into patches">
    $14 \times 14 = 196$ patches, as in the last lesson.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hide 75% at random">
    $0.75 \times 196 = 147$ patches are hidden; $49$ stay visible.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Encode only what is visible">
    The encoder, a ViT, reads just the 49 visible patch tokens (each with its position embedding). It never sees the hidden ones, not even as blanks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decode everything">
    A small decoder receives the 49 encoded tokens plus one shared, learned <Term def="A learned placeholder vector put at each hidden position, so the decoder knows a patch is missing there. Its position embedding tells it which patch it stands for.">mask token</Term> at each of the 147 hidden positions (with position embeddings), and predicts the pixels of every patch.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Score only the hidden patches">
    The loss is the mean squared error between the predicted and true pixels, averaged over the **hidden** patches only.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep the encoder">
    After pretraining, the decoder is thrown away. The encoder is fine-tuned or probed on the downstream task.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Written as a formula, with $M$ the set of hidden patches ($|M| = 147$ of them here), $\mathbf{x}_i$ the true pixels of patch $i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ the prediction (each a list of $16 \times 16 \times 3 = 768$ numbers):

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MAE}} = \frac{1}{|M|} \sum_{i \in M} \frac{1}{768} \big\lVert \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i - \mathbf{x}_i \big\rVert^2
$$

that is, the average squared pixel error over the hidden patches. Now see why the mask ratio is so high.

<MaskedPatches />

Why 75% for images but only 15% for text? A word carries a lot of meaning, so hiding even a few words makes a hard puzzle. Images are **redundant**: neighbouring patches look alike, so with a low mask ratio the model could fill most gaps by smudging nearby pixels, and would never need to understand anything. Hiding most of the image forces it to learn what objects look like. A pleasant bonus: the encoder only processes the 49 visible tokens. Attention cost grows with the square of the token count, so the encoder's attention work drops to $(49/196)^2 = 1/16$, and MAE pretraining is about three times faster or more.

<KeyIdea>

Masked image modelling hides most of the patches (often 75%) and trains the network to reconstruct them from the rest. Because images are redundant, hiding a lot is what forces the model to learn shapes, objects and context rather than copy pixels.

</KeyIdea>

## In PyTorch

The NT-Xent loss fits in a few lines. Rows $2k$ and $2k+1$ of `z` are the two views of image $k$, and `cross_entropy` does the softmax and the $-\log$. Here it checks the worked example:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def nt_xent(z, tau=0.5):
    """z: (2N, d); rows 2k and 2k+1 are the views of image k."""
    # length 1, so a dot product is a cosine similarity
    z = F.normalize(z, dim=1)
    sim = z @ z.T / tau                     # (2N, 2N)
    # a view is never its own partner: set the diagonal to -inf
    self_mask = torch.eye(len(z), dtype=torch.bool)
    sim = sim.masked_fill(self_mask, float('-inf'))
    # ^ is bitwise XOR: n ^ 1 flips the last binary digit,
    # so it pairs 0<->1, 2<->3, ...
    partner = torch.arange(len(z)) ^ 1
    # one loss per view
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, partner, reduction='none')

z = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.0], [0.8, 0.6],     # cat: views 1, 2
                  [0.0, 1.0], [-0.6, 0.8]])   # car: views 1, 2
losses = nt_xent(z)
print(losses)
print(losses.mean())
```

```text
tensor([0.2333, 0.6271, 0.6271, 0.2333])
tensor(0.4302)
```

The first number is the $0.23$ we found by hand. Setting the diagonal to $-\infty$ makes $e^{-\infty} = 0$, so a view never counts itself as a candidate. MAE's random masking is just as short: give every patch a random number, sort, and keep the patches with the smallest numbers.

```python
torch.manual_seed(0)
x = torch.randn(1, 196, 768)     # 196 patch tokens of one image
n_keep = int(196 * (1 - 0.75))   # keep 25% of them
noise = torch.rand(1, 196)       # a random number per patch
# keep the patches with the 49 smallest numbers
keep = noise.argsort(dim=1)[:, :n_keep]
# repeat each kept index for all 768 numbers of a token
idx = keep.unsqueeze(-1).expand(-1, -1, 768)
# gather picks those rows (patches) of x
visible = torch.gather(x, 1, idx)
print(n_keep, visible.shape)
```

```text
49 torch.Size([1, 49, 768])
```

## Contrastive or masked?

| | contrastive (SimCLR, MoCo) | masked image modelling (MAE) |
|---|---|---|
| the free label | which two views came from the same photo | the pixels of the hidden patches |
| needs negatives? | yes, many (big batches or a queue) | no |
| relies on augmentations? | heavily: they decide what to ignore | lightly (masking does the work) |
| typical encoder | ResNet or ViT | ViT (patches make masking easy) |
| good at | features that work with a simple linear probe | very strong results after fine-tuning; cheap to pretrain |

Later methods such as BYOL and DINO even manage without negatives: a "student" network learns to match a slowly updated "teacher" on different views of the same photo. DINO's ViTs learn attention maps that outline objects without ever seeing a label.

<ExamTip>

NOAI Prelim questions here are mostly one-line definitions. Self-supervised learning **creates its own labels from unlabelled data** (pretext tasks). Contrastive learning **pulls positive pairs together and pushes negative pairs apart**, and a positive pair is **two augmentations of the same image**. Masked modelling **predicts hidden parts from the visible context** (BERT for words, MAE for patches). For counting questions: a batch of $N$ images gives $2N$ views, and each view has $1$ positive and $2N - 2$ negatives.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Self-supervised learning makes labels from the data itself through a pretext task (rotation, jigsaw, colourisation, masking); the pretrained encoder is then reused with a linear probe or fine-tuning.
- Contrastive learning (SimCLR) augments each image twice: the two views are a positive pair, views of different images are negatives.
- The NT-Xent loss is a softmax cross-entropy over cosine similarities divided by a temperature $\tau$; the "correct class" is the partner view. Low $\tau$ sharpens it; many negatives help.
- The augmentations decide what the features ignore (crop and colour jitter matter most).
- MAE hides about 75% of a ViT's patches, encodes only the visible 25%, and reconstructs the hidden pixels; high masking is needed because images are redundant.

</Takeaways>
