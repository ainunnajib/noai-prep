Data science keeps asking two questions. **Do these two things move together?** (Is revision time linked to quiz scores? Is this feature linked to the target?) And **is this difference real, or just luck?** (Is the new model really 2% better, or did it get an easy test set?) This lesson answers the first with covariance and correlation, and the second with hypothesis tests and p-values. It ends with the two ways a test can go wrong, which NOAI loves to ask about.

## Moving together: covariance

Suppose four students report how many hours they revised and their quiz score out of 10:

| student | A | B | C | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| hours revised, $x$ | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 |
| quiz score, $y$ | 4 | 2 | 8 | 6 |

Students who revised more *tend* to score higher, but not perfectly. To measure this, compare each student with the average. If a student is above average on $x$ **and** above average on $y$ (or below on both), the two variables are moving together. If one is above and the other below, they are moving in opposite directions. The <Term def="The average of the products (x − x̄)(y − ȳ). Positive when two variables tend to be above or below their means together, negative when one tends to be high while the other is low.">covariance</Term> turns this into a number:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})
$$

- $n$ is the number of data points, and $i$ numbers them $1, 2, \dots, n$.
- $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ ("x-bar", "y-bar") are the means of the $x$ values and the $y$ values.
- $(x_i - \bar{x})$ is how far point $i$ is above (+) or below (−) the mean on $x$; likewise $(y_i - \bar{y})$ on $y$.
- Their **product** is positive when both are on the same side of their means, negative when they are on opposite sides. The covariance is the **average** of these products.

So a positive covariance means "they tend to rise together", a negative one means "when one is high the other tends to be low", and a value near 0 means there is no straight-line trend.

<Steps title="Covariance of the four students">
  <Step title="Means">
    $\bar{x} = (1 + 3 + 5 + 7)/4 = 16/4 = 4$ hours, and $\bar{y} = (4 + 2 + 8 + 6)/4 = 20/4 = 5$ marks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deviations from the means">
    $x - \bar{x}$: $-3, -1, 1, 3$. $\quad y - \bar{y}$: $-1, -3, 3, 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply in pairs">
    $(-3)(-1) = 3$, $(-1)(-3) = 3$, $(1)(3) = 3$, $(3)(1) = 3$. All four are positive: every student is on the same side of both means.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average the products">
    $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \dfrac{3 + 3 + 3 + 3}{4} = 3$ hour·marks.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Notice the units: hours times marks. Covariance carries the units of **both** variables, which is its big weakness, as you will see in a moment.

A covariance of a variable with *itself* has every product equal to $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X)$ is just the average squared distance from the mean: the **variance**.

## Correlation: covariance without the units

Measure the same revision times in **minutes** instead of hours. Every $x - \bar{x}$ becomes 60 times bigger, so the covariance becomes $3 \times 60 = 180$. Nothing about the students changed, yet the number jumped. So the size of a covariance tells you little about how *strong* the relationship is.

The fix is to divide by both standard deviations. That gives the (Pearson) <Term def="Pearson's correlation coefficient r = Cov(X, Y) / (σx σy). A unit-free number from −1 to 1 measuring how close the points are to a straight line.">correlation coefficient</Term>:

$$
r = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}
$$

- $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$ (with the same $\tfrac1n$ convention).
- Changing the units of $X$ multiplies $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and $\sigma_X$ by the same factor, which cancels. So $r$ has **no units** and does not change when you rescale either variable by a positive number. (Scaling by a negative number flips the sign of $r$.)
- $r$ always lies between $-1$ and $+1$. $r = 1$: all points on a rising straight line. $r = -1$: all on a falling straight line. $r = 0$: no straight-line trend.

For the four students, both variances are $(9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5$, so $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = \sqrt{5}$ and

$$
r = \frac{3}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6
$$

whether you measure in hours *or* in minutes.

<CorrelationScatter />

<KeyIdea>

Covariance tells you the **direction** of a linear relationship but its size depends on the units. Correlation divides out both standard deviations, giving a unit-free number from −1 to 1 that measures how tightly the points hug a **straight line**.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="r = 0 does not mean 'unrelated'">

Correlation only detects *straight-line* relationships. In the U-shape above, the score is almost completely determined by revision time, yet $r = 0$, because the falling left half cancels the rising right half. Always look at the scatter plot. A single outlier can also create or destroy a correlation.

</Mistake>

## Correlation is not causation

<Analogy title="Ice cream and drowning">

Across a year, days with high ice-cream sales also have more drownings. The correlation is strong and real. But ice cream does not make people drown: **hot weather** drives both. More people buy ice cream *and* more people go swimming.

</Analogy>

A hidden third variable that drives both, like the hot weather, is called a <Term def="A hidden variable that affects both variables you are comparing, creating a correlation between them even though neither causes the other.">confounder</Term>. A correlation between $X$ and $Y$ can come from:

- $X$ causing $Y$;
- $Y$ causing $X$ (reverse causation: do good students revise more, or does revision make good students?);
- a confounder causing both;
- pure coincidence, especially when you search through many variables.

The reliable way to show causation is an **experiment**: randomly assign who gets the treatment (say, a new revision app), so that confounders are spread evenly between the groups. In tech this is an **A/B test**.

<ExamTip title="Why this matters for AI">

Models learn correlations, not causes. A famous failure: an image classifier that "recognised" cows mostly by the green grass behind them, because in its training photos cows and grass always appeared together. Shown a cow on a beach, it failed. When an option says a model or a correlation "proves" that one thing *causes* another, be suspicious.

</ExamTip>

## Real effect or luck? Hypothesis tests

Your friend's coin lands heads **62 times in 100 tosses**. Is the coin biased, or did a fair coin just get lucky? Even a fair coin rarely gives exactly 50 heads, so we need a way to decide how far from 50 is "too far to be luck".

<Analogy title="Innocent until proven guilty">

A court starts by assuming the accused is innocent and convicts only if the evidence would be very unlikely for an innocent person. If the evidence is weak, the verdict is "not guilty", which is **not** the same as proving innocence. A hypothesis test works the same way, with "no effect" playing the innocent party.

</Analogy>

<Steps title="A hypothesis test, step by step">
  <Step title="State the null hypothesis">
    The <Term def="The 'nothing interesting is going on' assumption that a test tries to find evidence against, e.g. 'the coin is fair' or 'the new model is no better'. Written H₀.">null hypothesis</Term> $H_0$ is the boring, no-effect story: the coin is fair, $P(\text{heads}) = 0.5$. The <Term def="What you suspect instead of the null hypothesis, e.g. 'the coin is biased'. Written H₁ (or Hₐ).">alternative hypothesis</Term> $H_1$ is what you suspect: the coin is biased.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose the significance level α">
    Before looking at the data, decide how rare a result must be to convince you. The usual choice is the <Term def="The threshold α (often 0.05) chosen before the test: reject H₀ if the p-value is at or below α. It is also the probability of rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true.">significance level</Term> $\alpha = 0.05$ (5%).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Work out what luck looks like">
    Suppose $H_0$ is true. One toss scores 1 (heads) or 0 (tails), each with probability 0.5, so its mean is 0.5 and its variance is $0.5 \times (1 - 0.5)^2 + 0.5 \times (0 - 0.5)^2 = 0.25$. The tosses are independent, so for 100 tosses the means add to $50$ and the variances add to $25$: the number of heads has mean 50 and standard deviation $\sqrt{25} = 5$. By the Central Limit Theorem it is approximately normal. This is the **null distribution**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compute the p-value">
    The <Term def="The probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one actually observed.">p-value</Term> is the probability, **if $H_0$ is true**, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Here "at least as extreme" means 12 or more away from 50: 62 or more heads, or 38 or fewer. That probability is about $0.021$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide">
    $p = 0.021 \le \alpha = 0.05$, so we **reject $H_0$**: a fair coin would do this only about 2% of the time. The result is <Term def="A result is statistically significant when its p-value is at or below the chosen significance level α, so the null hypothesis is rejected.">statistically significant</Term>. Had $p$ been above $0.05$ we would **fail to reject** $H_0$, which is weaker than proving the coin fair.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<PValueTail />

<KeyIdea>

A p-value is a **tail area under the null distribution**: how often pure luck, with no real effect, would give data at least as extreme as yours. Small p means "luck is a poor explanation"; then compare it with the $\alpha$ you chose in advance.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="Three things a p-value is not">

With $p = 0.021$ it is tempting to say "there is a 2.1% chance the coin is fair" or "a 97.9% chance the result is real". Both are wrong: the p-value is calculated *assuming* $H_0$ is true, so it cannot be the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is also not the size of the effect: a tiny, useless difference can have a tiny p-value if the sample is huge.

</Mistake>

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={62} />

**One-tailed or two-tailed?** If you only care whether the coin favours heads, $H_1$ says "$P(\text{heads}) > 0.5$" and only the upper tail counts as extreme: $p = P(62 \text{ or more heads}) \approx 0.010$, half the two-tailed value. You must choose the tails *before* seeing the data; switching afterwards to get a smaller p is cheating.

### Quick estimates with z-scores

In an exam you will not add up binomial bars. Use the z-score from the last lesson with the null distribution's mean and standard deviation, then the 68-95-99.7 rule.

<Steps title="Is 62 heads out of 100 significant at α = 0.05 (two-tailed)?">
  <Step title="z-score under H₀">
    $z = \dfrac{62 - 50}{5} = \dfrac{12}{5} = 2.4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare with ±2">
    About 95% of a normal distribution lies within $z = \pm 2$, so results beyond $\pm 2$ have a two-tailed probability below about 5%. Since $2.4 > 2$, $p < 0.05$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide">
    Reject $H_0$. (Exact answer: $p \approx 0.021$.) For 58 heads, $z = 1.6 < 2$, so $p > 0.05$ and we would not reject. Exactly 60 heads sits on the borderline at $z = 2$; the exact p-value there is $0.057$, just above 0.05.
  </Step>
</Steps>

You can also *see* the p-value as a frequency. Simulate 100,000 experiments with a fair coin and count how often luck alone is at least as extreme as 62 heads:

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# 100,000 experiments of 100 tosses of a FAIR coin
heads = rng.binomial(n=100, p=0.5, size=100_000)

observed = 62
extreme = np.abs(heads - 50) >= abs(observed - 50)
print("p-value, two-tailed:", extreme.mean())
print("p-value, one-tailed:", (heads >= observed).mean())`} />

The library way uses SciPy, which also works out the p-value of a correlation:

```python
from scipy import stats

result = stats.binomtest(62, n=100, p=0.5)   # two-sided by default
print(round(result.pvalue, 4))

r, p = stats.pearsonr([1, 3, 5, 7], [4, 2, 8, 6])
print(round(r, 3), round(p, 3))
```

```text
0.021
0.6 0.4
```

The four students' correlation of 0.6 has $p = 0.4$: with only four data points, a correlation that large turns up by luck 40% of the time, so it is **not** significant.

## Two ways to be wrong: Type I and Type II errors

A test ends in a decision, and the decision can be wrong in two different ways:

| | $H_0$ is actually true (coin fair) | $H_0$ is actually false (coin biased) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reject $H_0$** | **Type I error**: false positive | correct: a true discovery |
| **Do not reject $H_0$** | correct | **Type II error**: false negative |

- A <Term def="Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true: a false positive or false alarm. Its probability is α.">Type I error</Term> is a **false positive**: you announce an effect that is not there. Its probability is the significance level $\alpha$, because the rejection region is chosen so that a true $H_0$ lands in it only $\alpha$ of the time (or slightly less for counts like heads, where the region cannot be cut exactly).
- A <Term def="Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false: a false negative or missed detection. Its probability is written β.">Type II error</Term> is a **false negative**: a real effect slips past you. Its probability is called $\beta$, and $1 - \beta$, the chance of catching a real effect, is the test's <Term def="The probability that a test rejects H₀ when H₀ is false, i.e. 1 − β. Bigger samples and bigger effects give more power.">power</Term>.

<Analogy title="The over-eager smoke alarm">

A smoke alarm is a test of $H_0$: "there is no fire". Set it too sensitive (a large $\alpha$) and it shrieks every time someone fries prata: false alarms, Type I errors. Make it too relaxed (a small $\alpha$) and it may stay silent during a real fire: a Type II error. You cannot turn one knob to remove both.

</Analogy>

<ErrorTradeoff />

The visual shows the trade-off with the coin, using a one-tailed test ("the coin favours heads"). With 100 tosses, a coin that truly lands heads 60% of the time, and $\alpha = 0.05$, the test misses the bias about 36% of the time ($\beta \approx 0.36$). Tightening to $\alpha = 0.01$ cuts false alarms but raises $\beta$ to about 0.63. Only **more data** helps on both fronts: with 400 tosses and $\alpha = 0.05$, $\beta$ drops below 0.01, because the two curves narrow like $1/\sqrt{n}$ (the CLT again) and stop overlapping.

<Note title="Testing many things at once">

Each test of a useless feature at $\alpha = 0.05$ has a 5% chance of a false positive. Test 20 useless features and you should *expect* $20 \times 0.05 = 1$ "significant" result by luck alone. Trying many models, features or prompts and reporting only the winner is how false discoveries are made.

</Note>

<ExamTip title="Remember which is which">

Think of the boy who cried wolf. First the villagers believe him when there is **no** wolf: a false positive, **Type I**. Later they ignore him when there **is** a wolf: a false negative, **Type II**. And these are the same false positives and false negatives as in a classifier's confusion matrix: for a spam filter with $H_0$ "this email is fine", sending a real email to spam is a Type I error; letting spam into your inbox is a Type II error.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Covariance $\frac1n\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ gives the direction of a linear relationship but depends on units; $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- Correlation $r = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y)$ is unit-free, lies in $[-1, 1]$ and measures only straight-line association. Correlation is not causation: look for confounders.
- The p-value is the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of data at least as extreme as observed. Reject $H_0$ when $p \le \alpha$. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
- Type I error = rejecting a true $H_0$ (false positive, probability $\alpha$); Type II error = missing a real effect (false negative, probability $\beta$). Lowering $\alpha$ raises $\beta$; more data lowers both.

</Takeaways>
