Data science keeps asking two questions. **Do these two things move together?** (Is revision time linked to quiz scores?) And **is this difference real, or just luck?** (Is the new model really 2% better, or did it get an easy test set?) Covariance and correlation answer the first; hypothesis tests and p-values answer the second.

## Moving together: covariance

Suppose four students report how many hours they revised and their quiz score out of 10:

| student | A | B | C | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| hours revised, $x$ | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 |
| quiz score, $y$ | 4 | 2 | 8 | 6 |

More revision *tends* to go with higher scores, but not perfectly. To measure the trend, compare each student with the average. The <Term def="The average of the products (x − x̄)(y − ȳ). Positive when two variables tend to be above or below their means together, negative when one tends to be high while the other is low.">covariance</Term> turns this into a number:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})
$$

Here $n$ is the number of points and $\bar{x}$, $\bar{y}$ ("x-bar", "y-bar") are the means. Each product is positive when point $i$ is on the same side of both means, and negative when it is on opposite sides. The covariance averages these products: positive means "they tend to rise together", negative means "when one is high the other tends to be low", and 0 means no straight-line trend.

<Steps title="Covariance of the four students">
  <Step title="Means">
    $\bar{x} = (1 + 3 + 5 + 7)/4 = 16/4 = 4$ hours, and $\bar{y} = (4 + 2 + 8 + 6)/4 = 20/4 = 5$ marks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Deviations from the means">
    $x - \bar{x}$: $-3, -1, 1, 3$. $\quad y - \bar{y}$: $-1, -3, 3, 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply in pairs">
    $(-3)(-1) = 3$, $(-1)(-3) = 3$, $(1)(3) = 3$, $(3)(1) = 3$. All four are positive: every student is on the same side of both means.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average the products">
    $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \dfrac{3 + 3 + 3 + 3}{4} = 3$ hour·marks.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Notice the units: hours times marks. Covariance carries the units of **both** variables, which is its big weakness. And the covariance of a variable with *itself* averages $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X)$ is just the **variance**.

## Correlation: covariance without the units

Measure the same revision times in **minutes** instead of hours. Every $x - \bar{x}$ becomes 60 times bigger, so the covariance becomes $3 \times 60 = 180$, though nothing about the students changed. The size of a covariance tells you little about how *strong* the relationship is.

The fix is to divide by both standard deviations. That gives the (Pearson) <Term def="Pearson's correlation coefficient r = Cov(X, Y) / (σx σy). A unit-free number from −1 to 1 measuring how close the points are to a straight line.">correlation coefficient</Term>:

$$
r = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}
$$

- $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$ (also dividing by $n$).
- Rescaling $X$ by a positive factor multiplies $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and $\sigma_X$ by that same factor, which cancels, so $r$ has **no units**. (A negative factor flips the sign of $r$.)
- $r$ always lies between $-1$ and $+1$: $r = 1$ means all points lie on a rising straight line, $r = -1$ on a falling one, and $r = 0$ means no straight-line trend.

For the four students, both variances are $(9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5$, so $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = \sqrt{5}$ and

$$
r = \frac{3}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6
$$

whether you measure in hours *or* in minutes.

<CorrelationScatter />

<KeyIdea>

Covariance tells you the **direction** of a linear relationship but its size depends on the units. Correlation divides out both standard deviations, giving a unit-free number from −1 to 1 that measures how tightly the points hug a **straight line**.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="r = 0 does not mean 'unrelated'">

Correlation only detects *straight-line* relationships. In the hill shape above, revision time almost fixes the score, yet $r = 0$, because the rising left half cancels the falling right half. Always look at the scatter plot: a single outlier can also create or destroy a correlation.

</Mistake>

## Correlation is not causation

<Analogy title="Ice cream and drowning">

Across a year, days with high ice-cream sales also have more drownings. The correlation is strong and real. But ice cream does not make people drown: **hot weather** drives both. More people buy ice cream *and* more people go swimming.

</Analogy>

A hidden third variable that drives both, like the hot weather, is called a <Term def="A hidden variable that affects both variables you are comparing, creating a correlation between them even though neither causes the other.">confounder</Term>. A correlation between $X$ and $Y$ can come from $X$ causing $Y$, from $Y$ causing $X$ (reverse causation), from a confounder causing both, or from pure coincidence, especially when you search through many variables.

The reliable way to show causation is an **experiment**: randomly assign who gets the treatment (say, a new revision app), so confounders are spread evenly between the groups. In tech this is an **A/B test**.

<Note title="Why this matters for AI">

Models learn correlations, not causes. A famous failure: an image classifier that "recognised" cows mostly by the green grass behind them, because cows and grass always appeared together in its training photos, so a cow on a beach fooled it.

</Note>

## Real effect or luck? Hypothesis tests

Your friend's coin lands heads **62 times in 100 tosses**. Is the coin biased, or did a fair coin just get lucky? A fair coin rarely gives exactly 50 heads, so how far from 50 is "too far to be luck"?

<Analogy title="Innocent until proven guilty">

A court assumes the accused is innocent and convicts only if the evidence would be very unlikely for an innocent person. Weak evidence gives "not guilty", which is **not** the same as proving innocence. A hypothesis test works the same way, with "no effect" playing the innocent party.

</Analogy>

<Steps title="A hypothesis test, step by step">
  <Step title="State the null hypothesis">
    The <Term def="The 'nothing interesting is going on' assumption that a test tries to find evidence against, e.g. 'the coin is fair' or 'the new model is no better'. Written H₀.">null hypothesis</Term> $H_0$ is the boring, no-effect story: the coin is fair, $P(\text{heads}) = 0.5$. The <Term def="What you suspect instead of the null hypothesis, e.g. 'the coin is biased'. Written H₁ (or Hₐ).">alternative hypothesis</Term> $H_1$ is what you suspect: the coin is biased.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose the significance level α">
    Before looking at the data, decide how rare a result must be to convince you. The usual choice is the <Term def="The threshold α (often 0.05) chosen before the test: reject H₀ if the p-value is at or below α. It is also the probability of rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true.">significance level</Term> $\alpha = 0.05$ (5%). (This $\alpha$ is not the learning rate $\alpha$ from the [Σ lesson](#/learn/maths-foundations/sigma-notation-and-subscripts); in a hypothesis test $\alpha$ always means the significance level.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="Work out what luck looks like">
    Suppose $H_0$ is true. Then the number of heads is $\text{Binomial}(100, 0.5)$, with mean $np = 50$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1 - p)} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, and by the Central Limit Theorem it is approximately normal. This is the **null distribution**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compute the p-value">
    The <Term def="The probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one actually observed.">p-value</Term> is the probability, **if $H_0$ is true**, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Here "at least as extreme" means 12 or more away from 50: 62 or more heads, or 38 or fewer. That probability is about $0.021$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide">
    $p = 0.021 \le \alpha = 0.05$, so we **reject $H_0$**: a fair coin would do this only about 2% of the time. The result is <Term def="A result is statistically significant when its p-value is at or below the chosen significance level α, so the null hypothesis is rejected.">statistically significant</Term>. Had $p$ been above $0.05$ we would **fail to reject** $H_0$, which is weaker than proving the coin fair.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<PValueTail />

<KeyIdea>

A p-value is a **tail area under the null distribution**: how often pure luck, with no real effect, would give data at least as extreme as yours. Reject $H_0$ when it is at or below the $\alpha$ you chose in advance.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="Three things a p-value is not">

With $p = 0.021$ it is tempting to say "there is a 2.1% chance the coin is fair" or "a 97.9% chance the result is real". Both are wrong: the p-value is calculated *assuming* $H_0$ is true, so it cannot be the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is also not the size of the effect: a tiny, useless difference can have a tiny p-value if the sample is huge.

</Mistake>

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={62} />

**One-tailed or two-tailed?** If you only care whether the coin favours heads, $H_1$ is $P(\text{heads}) > 0.5$ and only the upper tail counts: $p = P(62 \text{ or more heads}) \approx 0.010$, half the two-tailed value. Choose the tails *before* seeing the data; switching afterwards to get a smaller p is cheating.

<ExamTip title="Estimate p with a z-score">

Without a calculator, use a z-score under the null distribution, then the 68-95-99.7 rule. For 62 heads, $z = (62 - 50)/5 = 2.4$. About 95% of a normal lies within $z = \pm 2$, so the two tails beyond hold about 5%; since $2.4 > 2$, $p < 0.05$ and we reject $H_0$. Exactly 60 heads ($z = 2$) is the borderline: its exact p-value is $0.057$, just above 0.05.

</ExamTip>

## Two ways to be wrong: Type I and Type II errors

A test's decision can be wrong in two different ways:

| | $H_0$ is actually true (coin fair) | $H_0$ is actually false (coin biased) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reject $H_0$** | **Type I error**: false positive | correct: a true discovery |
| **Do not reject $H_0$** | correct | **Type II error**: false negative |

- A <Term def="Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true: a false positive or false alarm. Its probability is α.">Type I error</Term> is a **false positive**: announcing an effect that is not there. Its probability is the significance level $\alpha$, since a true $H_0$ lands in the rejection region only $\alpha$ of the time (or slightly less for whole-number counts like heads).
- A <Term def="Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false: a false negative or missed detection. Its probability is written β.">Type II error</Term> is a **false negative**: a real effect slips past you. Its probability is called $\beta$, and $1 - \beta$, the chance of catching a real effect, is the test's <Term def="The probability that a test rejects H₀ when H₀ is false, i.e. 1 − β. Bigger samples and bigger effects give more power.">power</Term>.

<Analogy title="The over-eager smoke alarm">

A smoke alarm tests $H_0$: "there is no fire". Too sensitive (a large $\alpha$) and it shrieks every time someone fries prata: false alarms, Type I errors. Too relaxed (a small $\alpha$) and it may stay silent during a real fire: a Type II error. One knob cannot remove both.

</Analogy>

<ErrorTradeoff />

The visual uses a one-tailed test ("the coin favours heads") and the normal approximation. With 100 tosses of a coin that truly lands heads 60% of the time and $\alpha = 0.05$, the test misses the bias about 36% of the time ($\beta \approx 0.36$). Tightening to $\alpha = 0.01$ cuts false alarms but raises $\beta$ to about 0.63. Only **more data** helps on both fronts: with 400 tosses and $\alpha = 0.05$, $\beta$ drops below 0.01, because both curves narrow like $1/\sqrt{n}$ (the CLT again).

One warning follows from $\alpha$: test 20 useless features at $\alpha = 0.05$ and you should *expect* $20 \times 0.05 = 1$ "significant" result by luck alone.

<ExamTip title="Remember which is which">

Think of the boy who cried wolf. First the villagers believe him when there is **no** wolf: a false positive, **Type I**. Later they ignore him when there **is** a wolf: a false negative, **Type II**. For a spam filter with $H_0$ "this email is fine", sending a real email to spam is Type I; letting spam into your inbox is Type II.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Covariance $\frac1n\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ gives the direction of a linear relationship but depends on units; $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.
- Correlation $r = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y)$ is unit-free, lies in $[-1, 1]$ and detects only straight-line patterns. It is not causation: look for confounders.
- The p-value is the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of data at least as extreme as observed (not the probability that $H_0$ is true). Reject $H_0$ when $p \le \alpha$.
- Type I error = rejecting a true $H_0$ (false positive, probability $\alpha$); Type II error = missing a real effect (false negative, probability $\beta$). Lowering $\alpha$ raises $\beta$; more data lowers both.

</Takeaways>
