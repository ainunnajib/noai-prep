Before any model learns from data, someone has to *look* at it. The first two questions are always the same: **what is a typical value?** and **how spread out are the values?** This lesson gives you the handful of numbers that answer them. They come back everywhere in AI (averaging a loss, standardising features) and are worth several quick marks in every NOAI paper.

<Analogy>
Two hawker stalls both have an *average* queue of 4 minutes. At the first stall you always wait 3 to 5 minutes. At the second you sometimes walk straight up to the counter and sometimes wait 8 minutes. Same centre, very different experience. To describe data honestly you need one number for the **centre** and another for the **spread**.
</Analogy>

## The mean: add up, then share out equally

The <Term def="The sum of all the values divided by how many values there are. Also called the average or arithmetic mean.">mean</Term> is the everyday "average": add all the values, then divide by how many there are.

$$
\bar{x} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \;=\; \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n}
$$

Reading the symbols one at a time:

- $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are the data values, and $x_i$ means "the $i$-th value".
- $n$ is how many values there are.
- $\sum$ (capital Greek sigma) means "add up": $\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ says "add $x_i$ for every $i$ from 1 to $n$".
- $\bar{x}$ (read "x-bar") is the mean. For a whole population it is often written $\mu$ ("mu").

Seven friends wait for a bus for 2, 3, 5, 5, 5, 7 and 8 minutes. The total is 35 minutes, so the mean is $35 \div 7 = 5$ minutes.

Why is the mean called the **balance point**? Picture each value as an equal weight sitting on a see-saw. There is exactly one place to put the pivot so that the see-saw stays level, and that place is the mean.

<BalanceBeam />

The see-saw balances because the distances below the mean cancel the distances above it. A value's <Term def="How far a value is from the mean, with a sign: positive above the mean, negative below. Written x_i minus x-bar.">deviation</Term> is $x_i - \bar{x}$. For the bus data the deviations are $-3, -2, 0, 0, 0, +2, +3$, and they add up to exactly 0. This happens for **every** data set:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0
$$

Remember this fact. In a few minutes it will explain why variance has to use squares.

## The median: the middle of the sorted list

The <Term def="The middle value once the data is sorted. With an even number of values, it is the mean of the two middle values.">median</Term> is the value in the middle once you sort the data.

- **Odd** number of values: take the middle one. $2, 3, 5, \mathbf{5}, 5, 7, 8$ has median 5.
- **Even** number of values: take the mean of the two middle ones. $2, 3, 5, \mathbf{5}, \mathbf{5}, 7, 8, 21$ has median $\frac{5 + 5}{2} = 5$.

That second list is the bus data plus one friend whose bus broke down (21 minutes). The mean jumped from 5 to $56 \div 8 = 7$ minutes, but the median did not move at all: it only cares about the *order* of the values, so a huge value is just "one more value on the right". We say the median is <Term def="Hardly affected by a few extreme values. The median is robust; the mean is not.">robust</Term> to <Term def="A value far away from the rest of the data, such as a typing error or a rare extreme case.">outliers</Term>, and the mean is not. That is why reports on incomes and house prices quote the **median**: one billionaire moving in would drag the mean up by millions.

## The mode: the most common value

The <Term def="The value that appears most often in the data. There can be more than one mode, or none.">mode</Term> is the value that appears most often: 5 in the bus data. A data set can have two modes (it is then *bimodal*), or none at all if every value appears once. The mode is also the only "average" that works for categories: there is no mean bubble-tea flavour, but there is a most popular one.

## Skew: which way does the tail point?

Many real data sets are lopsided: most people earn a modest income and a few earn enormous amounts. A distribution like this is <Term def="Lopsided: one side has a long, thin tail of extreme values. Right-skewed (positive skew) means the long tail is on the side of large values.">skewed</Term>. We name the skew after the side where the long tail is:

- **Right-skewed** (positive skew): a long tail of large values on the right.
- **Left-skewed** (negative skew): a long tail of small values on the left.
- **Symmetric**: both sides are mirror images, like a bell.

Slide between the three shapes and watch where the mean, median and mode sit.

<SkewTail />

<KeyIdea>
The mean is dragged towards the tail, the median follows a little, and the mode stays at the peak. Right-skewed data typically has **mean > median > mode**, left-skewed data has **mean < median < mode**, and symmetric, single-peaked data has all three equal.
</KeyIdea>

The *skewness* readout turns this into one number: the average **cube** of the standardised distances, $\frac{1}{n}\sum \big(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sigma}\big)^3$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation (coming up next). Cubing keeps the sign, so a long right tail makes it positive. For NOAI you only need the sign and the ordering rule.

<ExamTip>
NOAI likes the ordering question: "In a right-skewed distribution, which is typically largest?" The mean, because it chases the tail: **mean > median > mode**. Incomes, house prices, waiting times and follower counts are the standard right-skewed examples. *Typically* matters: it is a rule of thumb, and odd shapes can break it.
</ExamTip>

## Spread: how far do values wander from the centre?

The quickest measure of spread is the <Term def="The largest value minus the smallest value.">range</Term>, the largest value minus the smallest, but a single outlier wrecks it. A more robust partner for the median is the <Term def="Interquartile range: the spread of the middle half of the sorted data (the third quartile minus the first quartile). Outliers barely affect it.">interquartile range (IQR)</Term>, the width of the middle half of the data. Sort the data and split it into a lower and an upper half: the first quartile $Q_1$ is the median of the lower half, the third quartile $Q_3$ the median of the upper half, and $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$. For $1, 2, \dots, 8$ that gives $Q_1 = 2.5$, $Q_3 = 6.5$ and an IQR of 4. (NumPy interpolates slightly differently, so its quartiles can differ a little.)

The measure used most in maths and AI, though, is built from the deviations. A natural first idea is to average the deviations $x_i - \bar{x}$, but you already know they always add up to 0, whatever the data. The fix is to **square** each deviation first. Squares are never negative, so nothing cancels.

$$
\sigma^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2
\qquad\qquad
\sigma \;=\; \sqrt{\sigma^2}
$$

In words:

- $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ squares each deviation, so it is positive and far values count much more.
- $\frac{1}{n}\sum$ averages those squares. The result $\sigma^2$ ("sigma squared") is the <Term def="The average of the squared distances from the mean. Its units are the data's units squared.">variance</Term>.
- Squaring also squares the units (minutes become "minutes²"), so taking the square root gives the <Term def="The square root of the variance. It measures a typical distance from the mean, in the same units as the data.">standard deviation</Term> $\sigma$, back in minutes.

<VarianceSquares />

<KeyIdea>
**Variance** is the average squared distance from the mean. The **standard deviation** is its square root, in the same units as the data. A point twice as far from the mean adds four times as much to the variance, so far-out points dominate it.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Variance and SD of the queue times 1, 3, 4, 5, 7 minutes (the visual's starting data)">
  <Step title="Find the mean">
    $1 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 7 = 20$, and $20 \div 5 = 4$. So $\bar{x} = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find each deviation">
    Subtract the mean from each value: $-3, -1, 0, +1, +3$. Check: they add to 0.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square each deviation">
    $9, 1, 0, 1, 9$. Every square is positive, and the two values 3 away contribute 9 each, far more than the two values 1 away.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average the squares">
    $9 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 9 = 20$, and $20 \div 5 = 4$. The variance is $\sigma^2 = 4$ minutes².
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the square root">
    $\sigma = \sqrt{4} = 2$ minutes. A typical queue time is about 2 minutes away from the mean of 4.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Divide by n, or by n − 1?

The visual's toggle shows two versions of the variance.

- Divide by $n$ when your numbers are the **whole population** you care about (every student in the class). This is the <Term def="Variance computed by dividing the sum of squared deviations by n. Used when the data is the entire population.">population variance</Term> $\sigma^2$.
- Divide by $n - 1$ when your numbers are a **sample** used to estimate a bigger population (50 students surveyed out of a whole school). This is the <Term def="Variance computed by dividing by n minus 1. Used when the data is a sample and you want to estimate the population's variance.">sample variance</Term>, usually written $s^2$.

Why $n - 1$? A sample's points sit a little closer to their own mean than to the true population mean, so dividing by $n$ comes out slightly too small on average; the smaller divisor $n - 1$ corrects this. For large $n$ the two are almost identical.

<Mistake>
Python libraries disagree about the default. NumPy's `np.var` and `np.std` divide by $n$ unless you pass `ddof=1`. pandas' `.var()` and `.std()` divide by $n - 1$ by default. PyTorch's `torch.var` and `torch.std` also divide by $n - 1$ by default. If a result doesn't match the expected output, check the divisor first.
</Mistake>

### Shifting and scaling

Two facts save time in the exam:

- **Add** the same number $c$ to every value: the mean goes up by $c$, but the variance and standard deviation do **not** change, because the distances between values stay the same.
- **Multiply** every value by $k$: the mean is multiplied by $k$, the standard deviation by $|k|$ and the variance by $k^2$. Doubling every mark doubles the SD and multiplies the variance by 4.

These two facts are why <Term def="Rescaling a feature as (x minus mean) divided by standard deviation, so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.">standardising</Term> a feature with $z = (x - \bar{x}) / \sigma$ gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1. You will meet $z$-scores properly in the lesson on the normal curve.

<Note title="Why AI cares: squared error versus absolute error">
The mean is the number $c$ that makes the total squared distance $\sum (x_i - c)^2$ smallest; a median makes the total absolute distance $\sum |x_i - c|$ smallest. So a model trained with mean squared error (MSE) gets pulled around by outliers, like the mean, while one trained with mean absolute error (MAE) is more robust, like the median.
</Note>

<ExamTip>
Speed tricks for a 36-second question:
- The mean of evenly spaced numbers is $(\text{first} + \text{last}) / 2$: the mean of $2, 4, 6, 8$ is 5.
- If every value is identical, the variance and standard deviation are 0.
- The SD is the **square root** of the variance: variance 9 means SD 3, and a distractor of 81 is a trap.
- To find the median, sort first. Unsorted lists are a favourite trap.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>
- **Mean** = sum ÷ count, the balance point. One outlier can drag it a long way.
- **Median** = middle of the sorted data, robust to outliers. **Mode** = most common value, and the only one that works for categories.
- In skewed data the mean chases the tail: right skew typically gives **mean > median > mode**.
- **Variance** = average squared distance from the mean. **Standard deviation** = its square root, in the data's own units.
- NumPy divides by $n$ by default; pandas and PyTorch divide by $n - 1$.
</Takeaways>
