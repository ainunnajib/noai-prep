Before any model learns from data, someone has to *look* at the data. The first two questions are always the same: **what is a typical value?** and **how spread out are the values?** This lesson gives you the handful of numbers that answer them. They come back everywhere in AI (averaging a loss, standardising features, batch normalisation), and they are worth several quick marks in every NOAI paper.

<Analogy>
Two hawker stalls both have an *average* queue of 4 minutes. At the first stall you always wait 3 to 5 minutes. At the second you sometimes walk straight up to the counter and sometimes wait 8 minutes. Same centre, very different experience. To describe data honestly you need one number for the **centre** and another for the **spread**.
</Analogy>

## The mean: add up, then share out equally

The <Term def="The sum of all the values divided by how many values there are. Also called the average or arithmetic mean.">mean</Term> is the everyday "average": add all the values, then divide by how many there are.

$$
\bar{x} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \;=\; \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n}
$$

Reading the symbols one at a time:

- $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ are the data values, and $x_i$ means "the $i$-th value".
- $n$ is how many values there are.
- $\sum$ is the capital Greek letter sigma and means "add up". $\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i$ says "add $x_i$ for every $i$ from 1 to $n$".
- $\bar{x}$ (read "x-bar") is the mean. For a whole population it is often written $\mu$ (the Greek letter "mu").

Seven friends wait for a bus for 2, 3, 5, 5, 5, 7 and 8 minutes. The total is 35 minutes, so the mean is $35 \div 7 = 5$ minutes.

Why is the mean called the **balance point**? Picture each value as an equal weight sitting on a see-saw. There is exactly one place to put the pivot so that the see-saw stays level, and that place is the mean.

<BalanceBeam />

The see-saw balances because the distances below the mean cancel the distances above it. A value's <Term def="How far a value is from the mean, with a sign: positive above the mean, negative below. Written x_i minus x-bar.">deviation</Term> is $x_i - \bar{x}$. For the bus data the deviations are $-3, -2, 0, 0, 0, +2, +3$, and they add up to exactly 0. This happens for **every** data set:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0
$$

Remember this fact. In a few minutes it will explain why variance has to use squares.

## The median: the middle of the sorted list

The <Term def="The middle value once the data is sorted. With an even number of values, it is the mean of the two middle values.">median</Term> is the value in the middle once you sort the data.

- **Odd** number of values: take the middle one. $2, 3, 5, \mathbf{5}, 5, 7, 8$ has median 5.
- **Even** number of values: take the mean of the two middle ones. $2, 3, 5, \mathbf{5}, \mathbf{5}, 7, 8, 21$ has median $\frac{5 + 5}{2} = 5$.

That second list is the bus data plus one friend whose bus broke down (21 minutes). Look at what the new value did. The mean jumped from 5 to $56 \div 8 = 7$ minutes. The median did not move at all. The median only cares about the *order* of the values, so a huge value is just "one more value on the right". It does not matter how far right it is. We say the median is <Term def="Hardly affected by a few extreme values. The median is robust; the mean is not.">robust</Term> to <Term def="A value far away from the rest of the data, such as a typing error or a rare extreme case.">outliers</Term>, and the mean is not.

<Analogy title="A billionaire moves into the street">
The average household income in a street of 50 HDB flats jumps by millions of dollars if one billionaire moves in. Yet the household in the middle of the street is exactly as well off as before, so the median barely changes. This is why reports on income and house prices quote the **median**.
</Analogy>

## The mode: the most common value

The <Term def="The value that appears most often in the data. There can be more than one mode, or none.">mode</Term> is the value that appears most often. In the bus data, 5 appears three times, so the mode is 5. A data set can have two modes (it is then called *bimodal*), or no mode at all if every value appears once. The mode is also the only "average" that works for categories. You cannot find the mean of favourite bubble-tea flavours, but you can find the most popular one.

## Skew: which way does the tail point?

Many real data sets are lopsided. Most people earn a modest income and a few earn enormous amounts. Most YouTube videos have a few hundred views and a handful have millions. A distribution like this is <Term def="Lopsided: one side has a long, thin tail of extreme values. Right-skewed (positive skew) means the long tail is on the side of large values.">skewed</Term>. We name the skew after the side where the long tail is:

- **Right-skewed** (positive skew): a long tail of large values on the right.
- **Left-skewed** (negative skew): a long tail of small values on the left.
- **Symmetric**: both sides are mirror images, like a bell.

Slide between the three shapes and watch where the mean, median and mode sit.

<SkewTail />

<KeyIdea>
The mean is dragged towards the tail, the median follows a little, and the mode stays at the peak. Right-skewed data typically has **mean > median > mode**, left-skewed data has **mean < median < mode**, and symmetric data has all three equal.
</KeyIdea>

The readout labelled *skewness* turns this into a single number. It averages the **cubes** of the standardised distances, $\frac{1}{n}\sum \big(\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{\sigma}\big)^3$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation (coming up next). Cubing keeps the sign, so a long right tail makes it positive and a long left tail makes it negative. For NOAI you only need the sign and the ordering rule above.

<ExamTip>
NOAI likes the ordering question: "In a right-skewed distribution, which is typically largest?" The answer is the mean, giving **mean > median > mode**. A quick way to remember it: the mean chases the tail. Incomes, house prices, waiting times and follower counts are the standard right-skewed examples. The word *typically* matters, because the ordering is a strong rule of thumb rather than a law. Odd shapes can break it.
</ExamTip>

## Spread: how far do values wander from the centre?

The quickest measure of spread is the <Term def="The largest value minus the smallest value.">range</Term>, the largest value minus the smallest. It uses only two values, so a single outlier wrecks it. A more robust partner for the median is the <Term def="Interquartile range: the spread of the middle half of the sorted data (the third quartile minus the first quartile). Outliers barely affect it.">interquartile range (IQR)</Term>, the width of the middle half of the data. The measure used most in maths and AI, though, is built from the deviations.

A natural first idea is to average the deviations $x_i - \bar{x}$. But you already know they always add up to 0, so their average is always 0, whatever the data. The negative ones cancel the positive ones. The fix is to **square** each deviation first. Squares are never negative, so nothing cancels.

$$
\sigma^2 \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2
\qquad\qquad
\sigma \;=\; \sqrt{\sigma^2}
$$

In words:

- $x_i - \bar{x}$ is how far value $i$ is from the mean.
- $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ squares it, so it is positive and far values count much more.
- $\frac{1}{n}\sum$ averages those squares. The result $\sigma^2$ ("sigma squared") is the <Term def="The average of the squared distances from the mean. Its units are the data's units squared.">variance</Term>.
- Squaring also squares the units: the variance of times in minutes is in "minutes²", which is hard to picture. Taking the square root gives the <Term def="The square root of the variance. It measures a typical distance from the mean, in the same units as the data.">standard deviation</Term> $\sigma$, back in minutes.

Drag the points below and watch the squares.

<VarianceSquares />

<KeyIdea>
**Variance** is the average squared distance from the mean. The **standard deviation** is its square root, in the same units as the data. A point twice as far from the mean adds four times as much to the variance, so far-out points dominate it.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Variance and standard deviation of 1, 3, 4, 5, 7">
  <Step title="Find the mean">
    $1 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 7 = 20$, and $20 \div 5 = 4$. So $\bar{x} = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find each deviation">
    Subtract the mean from each value: $-3, -1, 0, +1, +3$. Check: they add to 0.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square each deviation">
    $9, 1, 0, 1, 9$. Every square is positive, and the two values 3 away contribute 9 each, far more than the two values 1 away.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average the squares">
    $9 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 9 = 20$, and $20 \div 5 = 4$. The variance is $\sigma^2 = 4$ minutes².
  </Step>
  <Step title="Take the square root">
    $\sigma = \sqrt{4} = 2$ minutes. A typical queue time is about 2 minutes away from the mean of 4.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Divide by n, or by n − 1?

The visual's toggle shows two versions of the variance.

- Divide by $n$ when your numbers are the **whole population** you care about (every student in the class). This is the <Term def="Variance computed by dividing the sum of squared deviations by n. Used when the data is the entire population.">population variance</Term> $\sigma^2$.
- Divide by $n - 1$ when your numbers are a **sample** used to estimate a bigger population (50 students surveyed out of a whole school). This is the <Term def="Variance computed by dividing by n minus 1. Used when the data is a sample and you want to estimate the population's variance.">sample variance</Term>, usually written $s^2$.

Why $n - 1$? The sample's own mean sits in the middle of the sample, so the sample's points are a little closer to it than they are to the true population mean. The squared distances therefore come out slightly too small on average. Dividing by the smaller number $n - 1$ scales them back up. For large $n$ the two versions are almost identical.

<Mistake>
Python libraries disagree about the default. NumPy's `np.var` and `np.std` divide by $n$ unless you pass `ddof=1`. pandas' `.var()` and `.std()` divide by $n - 1$ by default. PyTorch's `torch.var` and `torch.std` also divide by $n - 1$ by default. The same list can give two different answers, so if your result doesn't match the expected output, check the divisor first.
</Mistake>

### Shifting and scaling

Two facts save time in the exam:

- **Add** the same number $c$ to every value: the mean goes up by $c$, but the variance and standard deviation do **not** change. Every value moves together, so the distances between them stay the same.
- **Multiply** every value by $k$: the mean is multiplied by $k$, the standard deviation by $|k|$ (just $k$ when $k$ is positive), and the variance by $k^2$. For example, doubling every mark doubles the SD and multiplies the variance by 4.

These two facts are why <Term def="Rescaling a feature as (x minus mean) divided by standard deviation, so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.">standardising</Term> a feature with $z = (x - \bar{x}) / \sigma$ always gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1. You will meet $z$-scores properly in the lesson on the normal curve.

<Note title="Why AI cares: squared error versus absolute error">
The mean is the single number $c$ that makes the total squared distance $\sum (x_i - c)^2$ as small as possible. The median is the number that makes the total absolute distance $\sum |x_i - c|$ as small as possible. This is why a model trained with mean squared error (MSE) gets pulled around by outliers, just as the mean does, while one trained with mean absolute error (MAE) is more robust, just like the median.
</Note>

<ExamTip>
Speed tricks for a 36-second question:
- The mean of evenly spaced numbers is the middle one: the mean of $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$ is 6.
- If every value is identical, the variance and standard deviation are 0.
- The standard deviation is the **square root** of the variance. If the variance is 9, the SD is 3, and a distractor of 81 is a trap.
- To find the median, sort first. Unsorted lists are a favourite trap.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>
- **Mean** = sum ÷ count, the balance point. One outlier can drag it a long way.
- **Median** = middle of the sorted data, robust to outliers. **Mode** = most common value, and the only one that works for categories.
- In skewed data the mean chases the tail: right skew typically gives **mean > median > mode**.
- **Variance** = average squared distance from the mean. **Standard deviation** = its square root, in the data's own units.
- NumPy divides by $n$ by default; pandas and PyTorch divide by $n - 1$.
</Takeaways>
