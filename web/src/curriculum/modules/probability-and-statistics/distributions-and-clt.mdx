Machine learning runs on randomness: random starting weights, random mini-batches, noisy measurements. You cannot predict one random outcome, but you *can* predict how random outcomes behave on average. This lesson gives you three tools for that: the **expected value**, the **normal curve**, and the **Central Limit Theorem**, which explains why the normal curve turns up almost everywhere.

## Random variables and their distributions

A <Term def="A number whose value is decided by chance, such as the result of a die roll or the height of a randomly picked student.">random variable</Term> is a number decided by chance. We name it with a capital letter such as $X$, and write a particular value it might take in lower case, $x$. Its <Term def="The list of values a random variable can take, together with how likely each one is.">probability distribution</Term> lists every possible value and how likely each one is. For one roll of a fair die:

| $x$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P(X = x)$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ | $\tfrac16$ |

Two rules hold for every distribution: no probability is negative, and all the probabilities add up to 1, because *something* must happen.

## Expectation: the long-run average

<Analogy title="The lucky dip at the school carnival">

A lucky dip ticket at your CCA's carnival stall costs \$2. One ticket in ten wins a \$10 voucher; the rest win nothing. Tonight you win \$10 or \$0, never "\$1". But sell 1000 tickets and about 100 win, so the stall pays out about \$1000: **\$1 per ticket on average**. That \$1 is the *expected* payout: a promise about the long run, not about tonight.

</Analogy>

The <Term def="The long-run average of a random variable: each possible value times its probability, all added up. Also called the mean of the distribution.">expected value</Term> of $X$ is

$$
E[X] = \sum_{x} x \cdot P(X = x)
$$

- $E[X]$ is read "the expected value of $X$". It is also called the **mean** of the distribution and written $\mu$ (the Greek letter mu).
- $\sum_x$ means "add up over every possible value $x$", each value **weighted** by its probability.

In words: *multiply each outcome by its probability, then add.* For the lucky dip, the expected payout is $10 \times 0.1 + 0 \times 0.9 = 1$ dollar.

<Steps title="Expected value of one fair die roll">
  <Step title="Weight each face">
    Each face has probability $\tfrac16$, so the terms are $1 \cdot \tfrac16,\ 2 \cdot \tfrac16,\ \dots,\ 6 \cdot \tfrac16$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add them up">
    $E[X] = \dfrac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = \dfrac{21}{6} = 3.5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Notice">
    You can never roll a 3.5. An expected value does not have to be a possible outcome: it is where the **average** of many rolls ends up.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<RunningAverage />

Press **Play** above and watch the blue line settle onto the orange one. That is the <Term def="The fact that the average of many independent repeats of a random experiment gets closer and closer to the expected value as the number of repeats grows.">law of large numbers</Term>: the more rolls you average, the closer the average sits to $E[X]$. It is why a model's accuracy measured on 10,000 test images is far more trustworthy than one measured on 10.

<Mistake title="The gambler's fallacy">

After five 6s in a row, the next roll is **not** "due" to be low. A die has no memory: each face still has probability $\tfrac16$. The average drifts back to 3.5 not because later rolls make up for the streak, but because thousands of ordinary rolls swamp it.

</Mistake>

### Spread: the variance of a random variable

Two random variables can share an expected value yet spread very differently. The <Term def="The expected squared distance from the mean, Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]. It is large when values are often far from the mean.">variance</Term> measures spread as the average *squared* distance from the mean, and the <Term def="The square root of the variance, written σ (sigma). It measures spread in the same units as the data.">standard deviation</Term> $\sigma$ (sigma) is its square root, which is back in the original units:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big], \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}
$$

For the fair die $\mu = 3.5$, so the distances from the mean are $\pm 2.5$, $\pm 1.5$ and $\pm 0.5$. Their squares, $6.25$, $2.25$ and $0.25$ (total $8.75$), each occur for two of the six faces, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{2 \times 8.75}{6} = \frac{17.5}{6} \approx 2.92
$$

and the standard deviation is $\sigma = \sqrt{2.92} \approx 1.71$.

Four rules let you find means and variances without listing every outcome. Here $a$ and $b$ are fixed numbers (constants):

| Rule | In words |
| --- | --- |
| ${E[aX + b] = a\,E[X] + b}$ | scaling and shifting the values does the same to the mean |
| ${\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)}$ | shifting does not change the spread; scaling by $a$ multiplies the variance by $a^2$ (and $\sigma$ by $\lvert a \rvert$) |
| ${E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]}$ | means always add |
| ${\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)}$ | variances add, **if $X$ and $Y$ are independent** |

<Term def="Two random variables are independent if knowing the value of one tells you nothing about the other, like two separate dice.">Independent</Term> means that knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Two separate dice are independent, so their total has mean $3.5 + 3.5 = 7$ and variance $2.92 + 2.92 \approx 5.83$. It is the *variances* that add, not the standard deviations.

So the total of 64 independent dice has variance $64 \times 2.92 \approx 187$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{64} \times 1.71 \approx 13.7$, not $64 \times 1.71 \approx 109$. In general, the total of $n$ independent values has standard deviation $\sqrt{n}\,\sigma$: the ups and downs partly cancel.

<Note title="Three distributions worth naming">

- $\textbf{Bernoulli}(p)$: one trial that scores 1 ("success") with probability $p$ and 0 otherwise. Its mean is $p$ and its variance is $p(1 - p)$.
- $\textbf{Binomial}(n, p)$: the number of successes in $n$ independent $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ trials. Adding $n$ copies gives mean $np$ and variance $np(1 - p)$. For 100 fair coin tosses: mean 50, variance 25, standard deviation 5.
- **Uniform**: every value in a range is equally likely, like one fair die or a random time between 0 and 10 minutes.

</Note>

<Reveal label="For later: why attention in Transformers divides by √d">

Transformers (later in the course) score two lists of $d$ numbers with the dot product $q_1k_1 + q_2k_2 + \dots + q_dk_d$. If every entry is independent with mean 0 and variance 1, each product $q_ik_i$ has mean 0 and variance 1, so the sum has variance $d$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{d}$. With $d = 64$ the scores spread out with $\sigma = 8$, and the model puts almost all its attention on a single item. Dividing by $\sqrt{d} = 8$ brings the spread back to 1: that is the $\sqrt{d_k}$ in $\operatorname{softmax}\big(QK^\top / \sqrt{d_k}\big)$.

</Reveal>

## The normal curve

Heights are not like dice: a height can be 163.2 cm or 163.27 cm, with no gaps between possible values. Such a <Term def="A random variable that can take any value in a range (like height or time), not just separate values like die faces.">continuous</Term> random variable is described by a smooth curve called a <Term def="A curve describing a continuous random variable. The probability of landing in a range is the area under the curve over that range, and the total area is 1.">probability density</Term>. The probability of landing between two values is the **area under the curve** between them, and the total area is 1. Two traps follow: the *height* of a density is not a probability (it can even be bigger than 1), and $P(X = \text{exactly } x) = 0$, because only ranges of values have a positive probability.

The most important density is the <Term def="The bell-shaped, symmetric distribution fixed by its mean μ and standard deviation σ. Also called the Gaussian distribution.">normal distribution</Term> (also called the Gaussian), written $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Note that the second number is the *variance*. Its density is

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\; e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
$$

You will not need to evaluate it by hand. Just notice three things:

- The curve peaks at $x = \mu$ and is symmetric around it, so its mean, median and mode are all $\mu$.
- $\sigma$ sets the width: the exponent depends on $\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$, the distance from the centre in standard deviations.
- $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ only makes the total area 1, so a wider bell must be lower.

<Analogy title="Heights in your cohort">

Line up every Sec 3 student in Singapore by height. Most cluster around the average, and very short or very tall students are rare, so the histogram of heights looks like a bell.

</Analogy>

For **every** normal distribution, whatever its $\mu$ and $\sigma$, the same fractions lie within the same number of standard deviations of the mean:

- about **68%** lie within $1\sigma$ (between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$),
- about **95%** lie within $2\sigma$,
- about **99.7%** lie within $3\sigma$.

This is the **68-95-99.7 rule**, also called the empirical rule.

### z-scores: one ruler for every normal curve

The purple ruler in the visual measures the <Term def="z = (x − μ)/σ: how many standard deviations a value x lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean.">z-score</Term>:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

- $x - \mu$ is how far $x$ is from the mean in the original units (cm, marks, dollars); dividing by $\sigma$ turns it into a *number of standard deviations*.
- $z > 0$ means above the mean, $z < 0$ below it, and $z = 0$ exactly average.

Replacing every value by its z-score is called <Term def="Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, so the result has mean 0 and standard deviation 1.">standardisation</Term>. By the scaling rules above, standardised values have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and standardised normal data follows the **standard normal** $N(0, 1)$. This is exactly what scikit-learn's `StandardScaler` does to each feature, putting centimetres and dollars on one common scale.

<Steps title="Which result is more impressive?">
  <Step title="The data">
    Mei scores 72 in Maths (class mean 60, sd 6) and 80 in English (class mean 70, sd 10). Assume both classes' marks are roughly normal. The English mark is higher, but the two classes spread differently.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Maths z-score">
    $z = \dfrac{72 - 60}{6} = \dfrac{12}{6} = 2$: two standard deviations above the mean.
  </Step>
  <Step title="English z-score">
    $z = \dfrac{80 - 70}{10} = \dfrac{10}{10} = 1$: one standard deviation above the mean.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Turn each z into a percentage">
    95% lie within $\pm 2\sigma$, so 5% lie outside, 2.5% in each tail. So $z = 2$ beats about $100\% - 2.5\% = 97.5\%$ of the class (exactly 97.7%; the rule is rounded). For $z = 1$, 32% lie outside $\pm 1\sigma$, 16% in the top tail, so $z = 1$ beats about 84%.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Answer">
    The Maths result is more impressive, even though the mark itself is lower.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<ExamTip title="Percentages you can do in your head">

Learn the rule and its halves. Between $\mu$ and $\mu + 1\sigma$: about 34%. Below $\mu + 1\sigma$: about 84% (50% + 34%). Below $\mu + 2\sigma$: about 97.5%, so above it: about 2.5%. Questions often hide the z-score: "mean 50, sd 5: what fraction scored above 60?" is just $z = 2$, so about 2.5%.

</ExamTip>

In code, `torch.randn` draws from the standard normal $N(0, 1)$. To get $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, run the z-score formula backwards: $x = \mu + \sigma z$.

<Reveal label="See it in PyTorch">

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
z = torch.randn(100_000)        # standard normal: mean 0, sd 1
x = 160 + 8 * z                 # heights: mean 160 cm, sd 8 cm

print(round(z.mean().item(), 3), round(z.std().item(), 3))
print(round(x.mean().item(), 2), round(x.std().item(), 2))
within_1sd = ((x > 152) & (x < 168)).float().mean()
print(round(within_1sd.item(), 3))
z_back = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()   # standardise again
print(round(z_back.mean().item(), 3), round(z_back.std().item(), 3))
```

```text
-0.002 1.001
159.98 8.01
0.681
0.0 1.0
```

The fraction between 152 cm and 168 cm (within $1\sigma$) comes out at 0.681, the 68% of the rule.

</Reveal>

<Mistake title="NumPy's scale is σ, not σ²">

`np.random.normal(loc=5, scale=2, size=1000)` draws from a normal with mean 5 and **standard deviation** 2, so its variance is 4. The maths notation $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ puts the variance second, but the code puts the standard deviation second. Mixing them up is a classic bug and a classic exam trap.

</Mistake>

## The Central Limit Theorem

Why does the bell curve turn up so often, from heights to measurement errors? The answer is one of the most important results in statistics.

<Analogy title="Waiting at your favourite hawker stall">

One visit's wait is unpredictable: usually a minute or two, occasionally ten when a tour group arrives first. Those waiting times are **skewed**, nothing like a bell. But record the *average* wait over 30 visits, again and again, and those averages pile up in a neat bell shape.

</Analogy>

Take a <Term def="A group of n values picked at random from a population.">sample</Term> of $n$ independent values from any population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and compute its <Term def="The average of the n values in one sample, written x̄ (x-bar).">sample mean</Term> $\bar{x}$ (read "x-bar"). Repeat this many times. The **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** says that, for large enough $n$,

$$
\bar{x} \ \text{ is approximately } \ N\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).
$$

- The sample means are centred on the population mean $\mu$.
- Their standard deviation is $\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, called the <Term def="The standard deviation of a sample mean, σ/√n. It shrinks as the sample size n grows.">standard error</Term>.
- Their shape is approximately normal **whatever the shape of the population**.

<CLTSampler />

The $\sqrt{n}$ comes straight from the variance rules. A sum of $n$ independent values has variance $n\sigma^2$, because variances add. The mean divides that sum by $n$, which divides the variance by $n^2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}
$$

Taking the square root, the standard deviation of $\bar{x}$ is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

Averaging 4 values halves the spread, and averaging 100 values cuts it to a tenth. To halve the noise you need **four times** as much data.

<Mistake title="The CLT is about averages, not the data">

More data does **not** make the data itself normal: a million hawker waiting times are still skewed. It is the **sample means** that become approximately normal. "Large enough" is often taken as $n \ge 30$, more for very skewed populations; if the population is already normal, any $n$ works.

</Mistake>

Here is the CLT in NumPy, with skewed waiting times (mean 2 minutes, standard deviation 2 minutes):

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
sigma = 2.0  # mean 2 min, sd 2 min

for n in [1, 4, 16, 64]:
    # 20,000 samples of n waits each
    waits = rng.exponential(
        scale=2.0, size=(20_000, n))
    means = waits.mean(axis=1)
    se = sigma / np.sqrt(n)  # CLT
    print(f"n={n:2d}:",
          f"sd of means={means.std():.3f},",
          f"sigma/sqrt(n)={se:.3f}")`} />

Every fourfold increase in $n$ halves the spread of the means, exactly as $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ predicts.

<Note title="Why AI cares about the CLT">

A mini-batch gradient is an average over the $B$ examples in the batch, so its noise shrinks like $1/\sqrt{B}$. A test accuracy is an average of "right or wrong" scores, so its uncertainty shrinks like $1/\sqrt{\text{test-set size}}$.

</Note>

<ExamTip>

In NOAI the CLT is usually asked in words. The key phrase is "**sample means** are approximately normal for large $n$", not "all data is normal".

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- The expected value $E[X] = \sum x \, P(X = x)$ is the long-run average, not a prediction for one trial.
- Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean. For independent variables the variances add, not the SDs.
- For a normal distribution about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean, and $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ counts standard deviations from the mean.
- CLT: sample means are approximately $N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ for large $n$, whatever the population's shape. Their spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ shrinks as $n$ grows.

</Takeaways>
