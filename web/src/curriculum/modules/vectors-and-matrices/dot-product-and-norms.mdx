How does a music app know two songs are alike, or a search engine find the page that answers your question? It turns each thing into a vector, then **measures** the vectors. This lesson gives you AI's three everyday measuring tools: the **dot product** (how much two vectors agree), **norms** (how long a vector is, and so how far apart two points are) and **cosine similarity** (how closely two vectors point the same way).

## The dot product: multiply, then add

<Analogy title="Your hawker bill">
You order 2 plates of chicken rice, 1 teh and 3 kaya toasts at \$4, \$2 and \$1 each. Your bill multiplies each quantity by its price and adds: $2 \times 4 + 1 \times 2 + 3 \times 1 = 8 + 2 + 3 = 13$ dollars. Two lists in, one total out. That recipe, "multiply matching pairs, then add", is the dot product.
</Analogy>

The <Term def="A way of combining two vectors of the same length into ONE number: multiply matching components and add up the results.">dot product</Term> of two vectors of the same dimension is found by multiplying matching components and adding up the results:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i
$$

Here $a_i$ and $b_i$ are the $i$-th components of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, and $\sum_{i=1}^{n}$ ("sum for $i$ from 1 to $n$") adds all $n$ products $a_i b_i$. The answer is a single number (a <Term def="A single ordinary number, as opposed to a vector.">scalar</Term>), **not** a vector.

For example, $[1, 2] \cdot [3, 4] = 1 \times 3 + 2 \times 4 = 3 + 8 = 11$, and the hawker bill above is $[2, 1, 3] \cdot [4, 2, 1] = 13$. A dot product is a **weighted sum**, the core of a neuron in a neural network: inputs times weights, added up. The neuron then adds a bias $b$ and applies an activation function, $\text{activation}(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b)$.

## What the dot product means: do two vectors agree?

<Analogy title="Pushing a trolley at an angle">
You push a supermarket trolley with your arms pointing slightly sideways. Only the part of your push **along** the trolley's direction moves it forward. Push straight along and every bit counts; push at right angles and it does not move forward at all; pull backwards and you work against it. The dot product measures that useful part.
</Analogy>

The same number has a second, geometric formula:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta
$$

Here $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert$ and $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert$ are the **lengths** of the arrows, found by Pythagoras (the figure's $\mathbf{a} = [4, 1]$ has length $\sqrt{4^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{17} \approx 4.12$; more on lengths soon), and $\theta$ (theta) is the angle between them.

The <Term def="cos θ is a number between -1 and 1 that depends only on the angle θ: 1 at 0°, 0 at 90°, -1 at 180°.">cosine</Term> $\cos\theta$ comes from trigonometry: in a right-angled triangle, $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\text{adjacent side}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$. If you have not met cosine yet, this table is all you need:

| angle $\theta$ | 0° | 60° | 90° | 120° | 180° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\cos\theta$ | 1 | 0.5 | 0 | −0.5 | −1 |

In pictures, drop a line from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ at 90° onto the line through $\mathbf{a}$. The stretch from the origin to where it lands is the **shadow** of $\mathbf{b}$, its <Term def="The shadow of one vector on the line of another: drop a perpendicular from the tip of b onto the line through a.">projection</Term>. In the right-angled triangle this makes, $\mathbf{b}$ is the hypotenuse and the shadow is the side next to $\theta$, so the shadow is $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$ long (negative when it falls behind the origin, for angles over 90°). So the dot product is "length of $\mathbf{a}$ times the shadow of $\mathbf{b}$ on it".

<DotProductProjector />

<KeyIdea>
The **sign** of the dot product tells you how two vectors relate. Positive: roughly the same way (angle under 90°). Zero: at right angles. Negative: roughly opposite ways (angle over 90°).
</KeyIdea>

Two vectors whose dot product is 0 are called <Term def="At right angles to each other. Two non-zero vectors are orthogonal exactly when their dot product is 0.">orthogonal</Term>, the linear-algebra word for perpendicular. It is the fastest right-angle test: $[3, 1] \cdot [-1, 3] = -3 + 3 = 0$, so those arrows meet at exactly 90°.

## Norms: how long is a vector?

<Analogy title="The crow and the taxi">
Two HDB blocks are 3 streets east and 4 streets north of each other. A crow flies straight: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ streets. A taxi follows the roads: $3 + 4 = 7$ streets. Both honestly answer "how far?" with different rulers.
</Analogy>

A <Term def="A rule that gives the length (size) of a vector. It is never negative and is 0 only for the zero vector.">norm</Term> measures the length of a vector, written with double bars $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$; the subscript names the ruler. The three you need are:

- **L2 norm** (straight-line length): $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2}$
- **L1 norm** (add up the sizes): $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_1 = |v_1| + |v_2| + \dots + |v_n|$
- **L∞ norm** (the biggest single size): $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty = \max(|v_1|, |v_2|, \dots, |v_n|)$

$|v_i|$ is the size of $v_i$ ignoring its sign ($\lvert -4 \rvert$ and $\lvert 4 \rvert$ are both 4), and $\max$ picks the largest. L2 is Pythagoras in any number of dimensions, the <Term def="The ordinary straight-line length or distance, from Pythagoras' theorem. Same as the L2 norm.">Euclidean</Term> length; L1 is the <Term def="Distance measured along a grid, like a taxi on Manhattan's streets: add the sizes of the components. Same as the L1 norm.">Manhattan</Term> length, after New York's street grid. **With no subscript, $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$ means the L2 norm.**

One link to remember: $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_2^2 = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}$. For $[3, 4]$: $3 \times 3 + 4 \times 4 = 25 = 5^2$.

<KeyIdea>
A norm is a ruler for vectors. **L2** = square root of the sum of squares (the crow), **L1** = sum of absolute values (the taxi), **L∞** = the largest absolute value.
</KeyIdea>

<NormBalls />

<Steps title="All three norms of $\mathbf{v} = [3, -4]$">
  <Step title="L2: square, add, square root">
    $3^2 + (-4)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, and $\sqrt{25} = 5$. So $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_2 = 5$. The minus sign disappears when you square.
  </Step>
  <Step title="L1: add the sizes">
    $\lvert 3 \rvert + \lvert -4 \rvert = 3 + 4 = 7$. So $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_1 = 7$. Without absolute values you would get $3 + (-4) = -1$, and a length is never negative.
  </Step>
  <Step title="L∞: take the biggest size">
    $\max(\lvert 3 \rvert, \lvert -4 \rvert) = \max(3, 4) = 4$. So $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Sanity check">
    $7 \ge 5 \ge 4$. For every vector, $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_1 \ge \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_2 \ge \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert_\infty$: the taxi never beats the crow.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Mistake>

</Mistake>

The L1 diamond's sharp corners sit on the axes, where one component is 0. When you meet <Term def="Adding a penalty on the size of a model's weights to the loss, to discourage overfitting. L1 regularisation uses the L1 norm; L2 regularisation uses the squared L2 norm.">regularisation</Term>, later, this shape explains why an L1 penalty (Lasso) pushes some weights to exactly zero while an L2 penalty (Ridge) only shrinks them.

**Unit vectors.** A vector of length 1 is a <Term def="A vector whose L2 norm is exactly 1. It describes a pure direction.">unit vector</Term>. Dividing a non-zero vector by its length **normalises** it: same direction, length 1. $\dfrac{[3, 4]}{5} = [0.6, 0.8]$, and $\sqrt{0.36 + 0.64} = 1$.

## Distance is the norm of the difference

In the last lesson you saw that $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$ is the arrow from the tip of $\mathbf{b}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{a}$, so its length is the distance between the two points:

$$
d(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert
$$

Use the L2 norm and you get the **Euclidean distance** (the crow); use the L1 norm and you get the **Manhattan distance** (the taxi). For $\mathbf{p} = [1, 2]$ and $\mathbf{q} = [4, 6]$: $\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p} = [3, 4]$, so the Euclidean distance is $5$ and the Manhattan distance is $7$.

## Cosine similarity: compare directions, ignore lengths

<Analogy title="Two playlists">
Your playlist has 30 K-pop and 10 Mandopop songs; your friend's has 300 K-pop and 100 Mandopop. Very different sizes, exactly the same taste: three K-pop songs for every Mandopop one. A good "similar taste" score should say they match perfectly, and cosine similarity does.
</Analogy>

Rearrange the geometric formula to get the cosine on its own:

$$
\text{cosine similarity}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert}
$$

The top is the dot product; the bottom divides out both lengths, so only the **direction** is left. The result always lies between $-1$ and $1$:

- $1$: same direction (the playlists above, or any $\mathbf{b} = c\,\mathbf{a}$ with $c > 0$);
- $0$: perpendicular, nothing in common;
- $-1$: exactly opposite directions.

It is undefined if either vector is the zero vector (you cannot divide by a length of 0). For word counts, which are never negative, it lies between 0 and 1; negative values need negative entries, as in learned embeddings.

<KeyIdea>
Cosine similarity compares **directions only**: 1 for the same direction, 0 for perpendicular, $-1$ for opposite, however long the vectors are.
</KeyIdea>

<CosineDial />

<Steps title="Cosine similarity of $\mathbf{a} = [3, 4]$ and $\mathbf{b} = [4, 3]$">
  <Step title="Dot product">
    $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 3 \times 4 + 4 \times 3 = 12 + 12 = 24$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Both lengths">
    $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ and $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Divide">
    $\cos\theta = \dfrac{24}{5 \times 5} = \dfrac{24}{25} = 0.96$: very similar directions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stretch one and check">
    Replace $\mathbf{b}$ with $10\mathbf{b} = [40, 30]$. The dot product becomes $240$ and $\lVert 10\mathbf{b} \rVert = 50$, so $\cos\theta = \dfrac{240}{5 \times 50} = 0.96$ again. Scaling by a positive number never changes cosine similarity.
  </Step>
</Steps>

A long and a short document on the same topic have word-count vectors pointing the same way but with very different lengths, so cosine similarity compares them fairly. Learned text <Term def="A learned vector of numbers that represents a word, sentence or document, placed so that similar meanings get similar directions.">embeddings</Term> also store meaning in their direction, so search engines and RAG systems (retrieval-augmented generation: a chatbot first looks up relevant documents) compare them by cosine similarity. Many systems normalise every embedding to length 1 first; then the bottom of the fraction is $1 \times 1$ and **cosine similarity is just the dot product**. **Cosine distance** $= 1 - \text{cosine similarity}$ is 0 for vectors pointing the same way.

## In code

In NumPy, `a @ b` (or `np.dot(a, b)`) is the dot product, and `np.linalg.norm` gives norms: L2 by default, `ord=1` for L1, `ord=np.inf` for L∞. Press **Run**, then try your own vectors.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

a = np.array([3, 4])
b = np.array([4, 3])
print("dot product:", a @ b)
print("element-wise a * b:", a * b)
print("L2 norm of a:", np.linalg.norm(a))
print("L1 norm of a:", np.linalg.norm(a, ord=1))
print("L-inf norm of a:", np.linalg.norm(a, ord=np.inf))
print("distance a to b:", np.linalg.norm(a - b))
cos = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print("cosine similarity:", cos)
print("cosine after stretching b:", a @ (10 * b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(10 * b)))`} />

<Mistake>
In NumPy, `a * b` is **not** the dot product: it multiplies matching components and keeps a vector (`[12 12]` above). The dot product also adds them up: `a @ b`, `np.dot(a, b)` or `(a * b).sum()` give 24.
</Mistake>

The same operations in PyTorch, which you will use in the Final (it cannot run in the browser, so this is its real output):

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

a = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0])
b = torch.tensor([4.0, 3.0])
print(torch.dot(a, b))
print(torch.linalg.norm(a))
print(torch.linalg.norm(a, ord=1))
print(F.cosine_similarity(a, b, dim=0))
```

<ExamTip>
These were among the few calculation questions in the 2026 Prelim; each should take under 20 seconds:

- **Dot product**: multiply pairs, add. Watch the signs.
- **Norms**: know the triples 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 6-8-10 and 8-15-17 by heart. L1 ≥ L2 ≥ L∞ eliminates options fast.
- **Cosine similarity**: if one vector is a positive multiple of the other, the answer is 1 (a negative multiple: −1), with no arithmetic. A zero dot product gives 0. Any option outside $[-1, 1]$ is impossible.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>
- Dot product: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum a_i b_i$, one number. Positive: similar direction; 0: perpendicular (orthogonal); negative: opposite.
- Geometrically, $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta$: length of $\mathbf{a}$ times the shadow of $\mathbf{b}$.
- L2 norm $= \sqrt{\text{sum of squares}}$ (crow), L1 norm $=$ sum of absolute values (taxi), L∞ $=$ biggest absolute value. Distance $= \lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert$.
- Cosine similarity $= \dfrac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert}$ lies in $[-1, 1]$ and ignores length, so it is used to compare documents and embeddings.
- In code: `a @ b` for the dot product, `np.linalg.norm(v)` for the L2 norm; `a * b` is element-wise, not a dot product.
</Takeaways>
