Where the gradient is zero the ground is flat, but flat ground could be a valley floor, a hilltop or a mountain pass. Telling them apart needs **curvature**, how the ground bends, measured by second derivatives. Curvature also explains which losses are easy to minimise, and it powers Newton's method, a faster but very expensive cousin of gradient descent.

<Analogy title="A mountain pass">

Along the road over a mountain pass, the pass is the highest point. Walking across the road from one peak to the other, it is the lowest point. The ground there is flat, yet it is neither a valley floor nor a summit.

</Analogy>

## Second derivatives measure bending

Recall from the rules lesson: the <Term def="The derivative of the derivative, written f″(x). It measures how fast the slope is changing: positive means the curve bends upwards, negative means it bends downwards.">second derivative</Term> $f''(x)$ measures how fast the slope changes. $f'' > 0$ bends up like a bowl ($\cup$); $f'' < 0$ bends down like a cap ($\cap$). So $x^2$, with $f'' = 2$, bends up everywhere, while $x^3$, with $f'' = 6x$, bends down for $x < 0$ and up for $x > 0$. Where the bending switches, like $x = 0$ here, is an <Term def="A point where a curve switches from bending up to bending down, or the other way round.">inflection point</Term>.

A <Term def="A point where the derivative (with many inputs: the whole gradient) is zero, so the ground is flat there. Also called a stationary point.">critical point</Term> is a point where $f'(x) = 0$. The **second-derivative test** classifies it:

- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) > 0$: flat and bending up, the bottom of a $\cup$. A **local minimum**.
- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) < 0$: flat and bending down, the top of a $\cap$. A **local maximum**.
- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) = 0$: the test cannot decide. At $x = 0$, $x^4$ has a minimum, $-x^4$ a maximum and $x^3$ neither, yet all three have $f' = f'' = 0$ there.

<Steps title="Find and classify the critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x">
  <Step title="Slope">
    $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set the slope to zero">
    $3x^2 - 3 = 0$, so $x^2 = 1$: $x = 1$ or $x = -1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the bending">
    $f''(x) = 6x$. At $x = 1$, $f''(1) = 6 > 0$: a local minimum, with $f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2$. At $x = -1$, $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$: a local maximum, with $f(-1) = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Local is not global">
    $f(-3) = -27 + 9 = -18$, far below $-2$, and $f$ keeps falling as $x$ goes further left. So $x = 1$ is only a *local* minimum: this function has no global minimum at all.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Convex functions: one valley only

<Analogy title="A marble in a wok">

Drop a marble anywhere into a wok and it always rolls to the same spot, the single bottom: there are no side pockets to get stuck in. Functions shaped like a wok are called **convex**.

</Analogy>

The precise test: join any two points on the graph with a straight line, a <Term def="A straight line segment joining two points on a curve.">chord</Term>. A function is <Term def="A function whose graph never rises above any chord: every straight line joining two points on the graph lies on or above the graph. For a smooth function of one input this is the same as f″(x) ≥ 0 everywhere.">convex</Term> if **every chord lies on or above the graph**:

$$
f\big(\lambda a + (1-\lambda)\,b\big) \;\le\; \lambda f(a) + (1-\lambda)\,f(b)
$$

for all inputs $a$, $b$ and every $\lambda$ between 0 and 1. As $\lambda$ (lambda) goes from 0 to 1, the input $\lambda a + (1-\lambda)b$ slides from $b$ to $a$; the left side is the height of the **curve** there and the right side the height of the **chord**.

For smooth functions of one input there is a quicker test: $f$ is convex exactly when $f''(x) \ge 0$ everywhere, i.e. it never bends downwards.

<ConvexChord />

| Function | Second derivative | Convex? |
|---|---|---|
| $x^2$ | $2$ | yes |
| $e^x$ | $e^x > 0$ | yes, even though it has no minimum at all |
| $\lvert x \rvert$ | $0$, undefined at the kink | yes: the chord test still works |
| $x^4 - x^2$ | $12x^2 - 2$, negative near $x = 0$ | no |

<KeyIdea>

For a convex function, **every local minimum is a global minimum**. There is only one valley to be trapped in, so gradient descent with a small enough learning rate converges to the global minimum (when one exists and the loss is smooth).

</KeyIdea>

<Reveal label="Why is every local minimum global?">

Suppose a convex $f$ had a local minimum at $a$, but some point $b$ was lower. The chord from $(a, f(a))$ to $(b, f(b))$ slopes down towards $b$, and the curve is on or below it, so just next to $a$ the curve dips below $f(a)$. Then $a$ was not a local minimum after all.

</Reveal>

Which machine-learning losses are convex?

- **Linear regression with mean squared error: convex.** The Grab loss from the previous lesson, $L(w) = 7.5\,(w - 2)^2 + 0.75$, has $L''(w) = 15 > 0$: a perfect bowl.
- **Logistic regression with cross-entropy (log loss): convex.**
- **Neural networks: not convex.** Swapping two hidden neurons (with their weights) gives a different weight setting with the same loss, but averaging the two settings usually does worse: the curve rises above the chord. Their landscapes have many valleys and saddle points.

## Many inputs: the Hessian

With two inputs, take partial derivatives of the partial derivatives. $f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$ means "differentiate with respect to $x$ twice", and the **mixed** partial $f_{xy}$ means "with respect to $x$, then $y$" (for smooth functions the order does not matter: $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$). Arranged in a square table they form the <Term def="The square matrix of all second-order partial derivatives of a function. With n inputs it is n × n and symmetric. It describes how the surface bends in every direction.">Hessian</Term>:

$$
H = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix}
$$

With $n$ inputs it is a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix. The gradient (first derivatives) says which way is downhill; the Hessian (second derivatives) says how the ground bends.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={37} />

<Steps title="The Hessian of f(x, y) = x² + 3xy + y²">
  <Step title="First partial derivatives">
    $f_x = 2x + 3y$ and $f_y = 3x + 2y$. Both are 0 at $(0, 0)$, so the origin is a critical point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Second partial derivatives">
    $f_{xx} = 2$, $f_{yy} = 2$, and $f_{xy} = f_{yx} = 3$. So $H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Along the axes it curves up">
    Walking along the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) gives $f = x^2$; along the $y$-axis, $f = y^2$. Both bend up, so it *looks* like a minimum.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Along a diagonal it curves down">
    Walk along the line $y = -x$: $f(t, -t) = t^2 - 3t^2 + t^2 = -t^2$, which bends **down**. So the origin is not a minimum: it is a saddle.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The curvature in a direction $u$ (a unit vector) is $u^\top H u$. Its largest and smallest values over all directions are the two <Term def="Special numbers attached to a square matrix. For a symmetric matrix such as a Hessian, they are the largest and smallest curvatures over all directions. You will meet them properly in the linear algebra modules.">eigenvalues</Term> of $H$. Here they are $5$ (along $y = x$) and $-1$ (along $y = -x$). Their signs classify the critical point:

- All positive ($H$ is <Term def="A symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are all positive. As a Hessian it means the surface curves upwards in every direction.">positive definite</Term>): it curves up in every direction, a **local minimum**.
- All negative (negative definite): a **local maximum**.
- Mixed signs: a <Term def="A critical point where the surface curves up in some directions and down in others, like a horse saddle or a mountain pass.">saddle point</Term>, the mountain pass from the start of this lesson.
- Some zero and the rest of one sign: the test cannot decide.

<SaddleSurface />

For a 2 × 2 Hessian you rarely need the eigenvalues themselves, because $\det H = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{trace} H = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ (from the vectors-and-matrices module). So:

- $\det H < 0$: opposite signs, a **saddle**.
- $\det H > 0$: the same sign, so a **minimum** if $\operatorname{trace} H > 0$ and a **maximum** if $\operatorname{trace} H < 0$.
- $\det H = 0$: the test cannot decide.

For $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $\det H = 2 \times 2 - 3 \times 3 = -5 < 0$, a saddle, with no eigenvalues needed.

<Mistake title="Two traps">

"The gradient is zero, so it is a minimum." Maxima and saddles have zero gradient too: it only means the ground is **flat**. And "both diagonal entries of $H$ are positive, so it is a minimum." The diagonal gives only the curvature along the axes; $x^2 + 3xy + y^2$ has positive diagonal entries and is a saddle. Check $\det H$ (or the eigenvalues).

</Mistake>

Saddles matter in deep learning: a true minimum must curve up in *every one* of millions of directions, so saddles are far more common, and near one the tiny gradient can slow plain gradient descent to a crawl (the randomness in training usually pushes it off). The Hessian also explains the previous lesson: its largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ caps the learning rate at $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and a big ratio $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the long, narrow valley that makes gradient descent zig-zag.

## Taylor approximation: every smooth curve is nearly a parabola

Zoom in on a smooth function near a point $x_0$ and it looks like a simple polynomial, its <Term def="An approximation of a smooth function near a point x₀ by a polynomial built from its derivatives at x₀: a line (first order), a parabola (second order), and so on.">Taylor approximation</Term>:

$$
f(x_0 + d) \;\approx\; f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,d + \tfrac{1}{2}\, f''(x_0)\, d^2
$$

Here $d$ is a small step away from $x_0$. The first two terms are the tangent line, the **first-order** approximation. The last term adds the right amount of bending to make a parabola, the **second-order** approximation (the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ makes its second derivative exactly $f''(x_0)$).

The full Taylor series keeps adding higher-order terms, but optimisation stops at first or second order. The approximation is only trustworthy **locally**: excellent close to $x_0$, possibly badly wrong far away.

<Steps title="Estimate e^0.1 without a calculator">
  <Step title="Pick a nearby point you know">
    Use $f(x) = e^x$ at $x_0 = 0$. Every derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x$, so $f(0) = f'(0) = f''(0) = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="First order">
    $e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 1 \times 0.1 = 1.1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Second order">
    $e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 0.1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.1^2 = 1 + 0.1 + 0.005 = 1.105$. The true value is $1.10517\ldots$
  </Step>
</Steps>

Gradient descent trusts only the tangent line. A line has no bottom, so you must choose how far to follow it: the learning rate. A parabola *has* a bottom, and you can jump straight to it.

## Newton's method: jump to the bottom of the parabola

The parabola $f(x) + f'(x)\,d + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x)\,d^2$ has slope $f'(x) + f''(x)\,d$ in $d$. Setting that to zero gives $d = -f'(x)/f''(x)$, so <Term def="An optimisation method that fits a parabola using the first and second derivatives, then jumps to its lowest point: x ← x − f′(x)/f″(x).">Newton's method</Term> repeats

$$
x_{\text{new}} = x - \frac{f'(x)}{f''(x)}.
$$

Compared with gradient descent, $x - \eta\,f'(x)$, this is gradient descent with $\eta = 1/f''(x)$, the "land on the bottom in one step" value from the previous lesson, recomputed at every point: cautious where the curve bends sharply, bold where it is gently curved.

<Steps title="Newton's method on f(x) = x² − 6x + 5, starting at x = 10">
  <Step title="Derivatives">
    $f'(x) = 2x - 6$, so $f'(10) = 14$. And $f''(x) = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="One Newton step">
    $x_{\text{new}} = 10 - \dfrac{14}{2} = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check">
    $f'(3) = 0$ and $f'' > 0$: the minimum, in one step. For a quadratic the parabola *is* the function, so Newton always lands in one jump. Gradient descent with $\eta = 0.1$ would only reach $10 - 1.4 = 8.6$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<TaylorFit />

Two lessons from the visual. Near a minimum Newton's method is extremely fast: the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step. But it is only as good as its parabola: where $f'' < 0$ the parabola opens downwards and Newton jumps to a **maximum**, and where $f''$ is near 0 it jumps absurdly far.

With many parameters the update becomes

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - H^{-1}\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w})
$$

where $H^{-1}$, the inverse of the Hessian, plays the role of $1/f''$. It rescales every direction by its own curvature, so a long, narrow valley is treated like a round bowl: on a quadratic loss, Newton reaches the minimum in one step with no zig-zag. The catch is the cost. With $n$ parameters the Hessian has $n^2$ entries, and solving with it takes about $n^3$ operations every step, written $O(n^3)$.

<HessianCost />

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={74} />

That is why deep learning sticks to **first-order** methods, gradient descent and its variants (SGD and Adam, later): they need only the gradient, $n$ numbers, which backpropagation computes at roughly the cost of a couple of forward passes. Small models can afford some curvature information: scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` uses `lbfgs` by default, a <Term def="An optimiser that builds a cheap estimate of the curvature from recent gradients instead of computing the full Hessian.">quasi-Newton</Term> method.

## In code

NumPy can check a Hessian's eigenvalues and take a Newton step. `np.linalg.solve(A, g)` computes $A^{-1} g$ without forming the inverse, which is faster and more accurate.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

H = np.array([[2.0, 3.0],
              [3.0, 2.0]])          # Hessian of x² + 3xy + y²
print("eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(H))
print("det:", round(np.linalg.det(H), 6), " trace:", np.trace(H))

# The long valley L = 0.5 * (0.1 * w1² + w2²) from the last lesson
A = np.array([[0.1, 0.0],
              [0.0, 1.0]])          # its Hessian
w0 = np.array([-4.0, 2.0])

# Newton: w - H⁻¹ ∇L (solve() avoids forming the inverse)
w = w0 - np.linalg.solve(A, A @ w0)
print("Newton, after 1 step:", w)

# Gradient descent with a safe learning rate
w = w0.copy()
for step in range(1, 1000):
    w = w - 1.8 * (A @ w)
    if np.linalg.norm(w) < 0.01:
        print("Gradient descent: within 0.01 of the minimum after", step, "steps")
        break`} />

`np.linalg.eigvalsh` (for symmetric matrices) prints the eigenvalues $-1$ and $5$: mixed signs, a saddle. On the long valley from the previous lesson, Newton lands on $[0, 0]$ in one step, while gradient descent needs 31 zig-zagging steps to get within 0.01 of it.

<ExamTip>

NOAI asks these mostly as quick recall and diagnosis:

- **Hessian** = matrix of **second-order partial derivatives**. Positive definite at a critical point means a **minimum**; for 2 × 2, $\det H < 0$ means a **saddle**.
- **Taylor series** = a **local approximation** near one point.
- **Newton's method** is fast near a minimum but costs $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ time per step, so deep networks avoid it.
- **Convex** = every local minimum is global (linear and logistic regression, not neural networks).

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- The second derivative measures bending. At a critical point ($f' = 0$), $f'' > 0$ means a local minimum and $f'' < 0$ a local maximum.
- Convex means every chord lies on or above the graph ($f'' \ge 0$ in 1-D); then every local minimum is global.
- The Hessian holds the second partial derivatives. Its eigenvalues are the extreme curvatures: all positive means minimum, all negative maximum, mixed signs saddle.
- A Taylor approximation replaces a function near a point by a line (first order) or a parabola (second order).
- Newton's method jumps to the bottom of that parabola, $\mathbf{w} - H^{-1}\nabla L$: very fast, but impractical for millions of weights.

</Takeaways>
