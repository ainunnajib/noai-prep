Where the gradient is zero the ground is flat, but flat ground could be a valley floor, a hilltop or a mountain pass. Telling them apart needs **curvature**: how the ground bends. It also explains which losses are easy to minimise, and it powers Newton's method, a fast but expensive cousin of gradient descent.

<Analogy title="A mountain pass">

Along the road over a mountain pass, the pass is the highest point. Walking across the road from one peak to the other, it is the lowest point. The ground there is flat, yet it is neither a valley floor nor a summit.

</Analogy>

## Second derivatives measure bending

The <Term def="The derivative of the derivative, written f″(x). It measures how fast the slope is changing: positive means the curve bends upwards, negative means it bends downwards.">second derivative</Term> $f''(x)$ from [Derivative rules you must know cold](#/learn/calculus-and-gradient-descent/derivative-rules) measures bending: $f'' > 0$ bends up like a bowl ($\cup$), $f'' < 0$ bends down like a cap ($\cap$). For $x^3$, $f'' = 6x$, so it bends down for $x < 0$ and up for $x > 0$. Where the bending switches, like $x = 0$ here, is an <Term def="A point where a curve switches from bending up to bending down, or the other way round.">inflection point</Term>.

A <Term def="A point where the derivative (with many inputs: the whole gradient) is zero, so the ground is flat there. Also called a stationary point.">critical point</Term> is a point where $f'(x) = 0$. The **second-derivative test** classifies it:

- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) > 0$: flat and bending up, the bottom of a $\cup$. A **local minimum**.
- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) < 0$: flat and bending down, the top of a $\cap$. A **local maximum**.
- $f'(x) = 0$ and $f''(x) = 0$: the test cannot decide. At $x = 0$, $x^4$ has a minimum, $-x^4$ a maximum and $x^3$ neither, yet all have $f' = f'' = 0$.

<Steps title="Find and classify the critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x">
  <Step title="Slope">
    $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set the slope to zero">
    $3x^2 - 3 = 0$, so $x^2 = 1$: $x = 1$ or $x = -1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the bending">
    $f''(x) = 6x$. At $x = 1$, $f''(1) = 6 > 0$: a local minimum, with $f(1) = -2$. At $x = -1$, $f''(-1) = -6 < 0$: a local maximum, with $f(-1) = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Local is not global">
    $f(-3) = -18$, far below $-2$. So $x = 1$ is only a *local* minimum.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Convex functions: one valley only

<Analogy title="A marble in a wok">

Drop a marble anywhere into a wok and it always rolls to the same spot, the single bottom: there are no side pockets to get stuck in. Functions shaped like a wok are called **convex**.

</Analogy>

The precise test: join any two points on the graph with a straight line, a <Term def="A straight line segment joining two points on a curve.">chord</Term>. A function is <Term def="A function whose graph never rises above any chord: every straight line joining two points on the graph lies on or above the graph. For a smooth function of one input this is the same as f″(x) ≥ 0 everywhere.">convex</Term> if **every chord lies on or above the graph**:

$$
f\big(\lambda a + (1-\lambda)\,b\big) \;\le\; \lambda f(a) + (1-\lambda)\,f(b)
$$

for all inputs $a$, $b$ and every $\lambda$ (lambda) between 0 and 1: the left side is the **curve**'s height at a point between $a$ and $b$, the right side the **chord**'s height there. For smooth one-input functions, convex simply means $f''(x) \ge 0$ everywhere.

<ConvexChord />

So $x^2$, $|x|$ and $e^x$ are convex ($e^x$ even though it has no minimum), while $x^4 - x^2$ is not.

<KeyIdea>

For a convex function, **every local minimum is a global minimum**. There is only one valley to be trapped in, so gradient descent with a small enough learning rate converges to the global minimum (when one exists and the loss is smooth).

</KeyIdea>

Which machine-learning losses are convex?

- **Linear regression with mean squared error: convex.** The previous lesson's Grab loss has $L''(w) = 15 > 0$: a perfect bowl.
- **Logistic regression with cross-entropy (log loss): convex.** (This classifier comes in [the classification module](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression).)
- **Neural networks: not convex.** Their landscapes have many valleys and saddle points.

## Many inputs: the Hessian

With two inputs, take partial derivatives of the partial derivatives. $f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$ means "differentiate with respect to $x$ twice", and the **mixed** partial $f_{xy}$ means "with respect to $x$, then $y$" (for smooth functions the order does not matter: $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$). Arranged in a square table they form the <Term def="The square matrix of all second-order partial derivatives of a function. With n inputs it is n × n and symmetric. It describes how the surface bends in every direction.">Hessian</Term>:

$$
H = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix}
$$

With $n$ inputs it is a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix. The gradient says which way is downhill; the Hessian says how the ground bends.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={37} />

<Steps title="The Hessian of f(x, y) = x² + 3xy + y²">
  <Step title="First partial derivatives">
    $f_x = 2x + 3y$ and $f_y = 3x + 2y$. Both are 0 at $(0, 0)$, so the origin is a critical point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Second partial derivatives">
    $f_{xx} = 2$, $f_{yy} = 2$, and $f_{xy} = f_{yx} = 3$. So $H = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Along the axes it curves up">
    Along the $x$-axis ($y = 0$), $f = x^2$; along the $y$-axis, $f = y^2$. Both bend up, so it *looks* like a minimum.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Along a diagonal it curves down">
    Along the line $y = -x$: $f(t, -t) = t^2 - 3t^2 + t^2 = -t^2$, which bends **down**. So the origin is a saddle, not a minimum.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The curvature in a direction $u$ (a unit vector) is $u^T H u$. Its largest and smallest values over all directions are the two <Term def="Special numbers attached to a square matrix. For a symmetric matrix such as a Hessian, they are the largest and smallest curvatures over all directions. You will meet them properly in the linear algebra modules.">eigenvalues</Term> of $H$. Here they are $5$ (along $y = x$) and $-1$ (along $y = -x$). Their signs classify the critical point:

- All positive ($H$ is <Term def="A symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are all positive. As a Hessian it means the surface curves upwards in every direction.">positive definite</Term>): it curves up in every direction, a **local minimum**.
- All negative (negative definite): a **local maximum**.
- Mixed signs: a <Term def="A critical point where the surface curves up in some directions and down in others, like a horse saddle or a mountain pass.">saddle point</Term>, the mountain pass from the start of this lesson.
- Some zero and the rest of one sign: the test cannot decide.

<SaddleSurface />

For a 2 × 2 Hessian you rarely need the eigenvalues themselves, because $\det H = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{trace} H = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$ (from [the determinant lesson](#/learn/vectors-and-matrices/identity-inverse-determinant)). So:

- $\det H < 0$: opposite signs, a **saddle**. For the example above, $\det H = 2 \times 2 - 3 \times 3 = -5$.
- $\det H > 0$: the same sign, so a **minimum** if $\operatorname{trace} H > 0$ and a **maximum** if $\operatorname{trace} H < 0$.
- $\det H = 0$: the test cannot decide.

<Mistake title="Two traps">

"The gradient is zero, so it is a minimum." Maxima and saddles have zero gradient too. And "both diagonal entries of $H$ are positive, so it is a minimum." The diagonal is only the curvature along the axes: $x^2 + 3xy + y^2$ is a saddle. Check $\det H$.

</Mistake>

Saddles matter in deep learning: a minimum must curve up in *every one* of millions of directions, so saddles are far more common, and their tiny gradients slow training. The largest eigenvalue also caps the learning rate: $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$.

## Taylor approximation: every smooth curve is nearly a parabola

Zoom in on a smooth function near a point $x_0$ and it looks like a simple polynomial, its <Term def="An approximation of a smooth function near a point x₀ by a polynomial built from its derivatives at x₀: a line (first order), a parabola (second order), and so on.">Taylor approximation</Term>:

$$
f(x_0 + d) \;\approx\; f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,d + \tfrac{1}{2}\, f''(x_0)\, d^2
$$

Here $d$ is a small step away from $x_0$. The first two terms are the tangent line (**first order**); the last term bends it into a parabola (**second order**). It is only trustworthy **locally**: excellent close to $x_0$, possibly badly wrong far away.

<Steps title="Estimate e^0.1 without a calculator">
  <Step title="Pick a nearby point you know">
    Use $f(x) = e^x$ at $x_0 = 0$. Every derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x$, so $f(0) = f'(0) = f''(0) = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="First order">
    $e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 1 \times 0.1 = 1.1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Second order">
    $e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 0.1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.1^2 = 1.105$. The true value is $1.10517\ldots$
  </Step>
</Steps>

Gradient descent uses only the tangent line, which has no bottom, so it needs a learning rate to say how far to go. A parabola *has* a bottom, and you can jump straight to it.

## Newton's method: jump to the bottom of the parabola

The parabola $f(x) + f'(x)\,d + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x)\,d^2$ has slope $f'(x) + f''(x)\,d$ in $d$. Setting that to zero gives $d = -f'(x)/f''(x)$, so <Term def="An optimisation method that fits a parabola using the first and second derivatives, then jumps to its lowest point: x ← x − f′(x)/f″(x).">Newton's method</Term> repeats

$$
x_{\text{new}} = x - \frac{f'(x)}{f''(x)}.
$$

This is gradient descent with $\eta = 1/f''(x)$, the one-step learning rate from [Gradient descent on a loss landscape](#/learn/calculus-and-gradient-descent/gradient-descent), recomputed at every point.

<Steps title="Newton's method on f(x) = x² − 6x + 5, starting at x = 10">
  <Step title="Derivatives">
    $f'(x) = 2x - 6$, so $f'(10) = 14$. And $f''(x) = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="One Newton step">
    $x_{\text{new}} = 10 - \dfrac{14}{2} = 3$, where $f'(3) = 0$: the minimum, in one jump. For a quadratic the parabola *is* the function. Gradient descent with $\eta = 0.1$ would only reach $8.6$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<TaylorFit />

Near a minimum Newton's method is extremely fast: the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step. But where $f'' < 0$ the parabola opens downwards and Newton jumps to a **maximum**.

With many parameters the update becomes

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - H^{-1}\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w})
$$

where the inverse Hessian $H^{-1}$ plays the role of $1/f''$, rescaling every direction by its own curvature, so a long, narrow valley is treated like a round bowl. The catch: with $n$ parameters the Hessian has $n^2$ entries, and solving with it takes about $n^3$ operations, written $O(n^3)$, every step.

<HessianCost />

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={74} />

That is why deep learning sticks to **first-order** methods such as gradient descent: they need only the gradient, $n$ numbers. (Small models can afford a cheap curvature estimate: scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` uses `lbfgs`, a <Term def="An optimiser that builds a cheap estimate of the curvature from recent gradients instead of computing the full Hessian.">quasi-Newton</Term> method.)

## In code

Check the saddle, then race Newton against gradient descent on the long valley:

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

H = np.array([[2.0, 3.0],
              [3.0, 2.0]])          # Hessian of x² + 3xy + y²
print("eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(H))
print("det:", round(np.linalg.det(H), 6), " trace:", np.trace(H))

# The long valley L = 0.5 * (0.1 * w1² + w2²) from the last lesson
A = np.array([[0.1, 0.0],
              [0.0, 1.0]])          # its Hessian
w0 = np.array([-4.0, 2.0])

# Newton: w - H⁻¹ ∇L (solve() avoids forming the inverse)
w = w0 - np.linalg.solve(A, A @ w0)
print("Newton, after 1 step:", w)

# Gradient descent with a safe learning rate
w = w0.copy()
for step in range(1, 1000):
    w = w - 1.8 * (A @ w)
    if np.linalg.norm(w) < 0.01:
        print("Gradient descent: within 0.01 of the minimum after", step, "steps")
        break`} />

Newton lands on the minimum $[0, 0]$ in one step; gradient descent needs 31 zig-zagging steps to get within 0.01 of it.

<ExamTip>

NOAI asks these mostly as quick recall:

- **Hessian** = matrix of **second-order partial derivatives**; positive definite at a critical point means a **minimum**.
- **Taylor series** = a **local approximation** near one point.
- **Newton's method** costs $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ time per step, so deep networks avoid it.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- At a critical point, $f'' > 0$ means a local minimum and $f'' < 0$ a local maximum.
- Convex: every chord lies on or above the graph, so every local minimum is global.
- The Hessian holds the second partial derivatives. All eigenvalues positive: minimum; all negative: maximum; mixed: saddle (for 2 × 2, $\det H < 0$).
- Taylor: near a point, a function is almost a line (first order) or a parabola (second order).
- Newton's method jumps to that parabola's bottom: fast, but impractical for millions of weights.

</Takeaways>
