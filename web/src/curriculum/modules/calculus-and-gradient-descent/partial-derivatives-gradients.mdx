So far almost every function in this module had **one** input. A real model has many knobs: an HDB price model has weights for floor area, storey and age, and a neural network has millions. Training needs to know which way to turn **every** knob. The answer is one arrow, the **gradient**, that points uphill.

<Analogy title="Standing on Bukit Timah Hill">

On the side of the hill, walking due east has one slope and walking due north another. Each is a **partial derivative**: the slope when you move in one direction only. Together the two numbers tell you which way is straight up the hill, and how steep it is.

</Analogy>

## Functions with two inputs

A function like $f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y$ takes two numbers and returns one: $f(1, 2) = 1^2 \times 2 + 3 \times 2 = 8$. Think of $(x, y)$ as a position on a map and $f(x, y)$ as the height of the ground there. You can draw it as a **surface**, or as a **map from above** where each <Term def="A curve on a map joining points that have the same height (the same value of f). Hiking maps use them to show hills.">contour line</Term> joins points of equal height, like a hiking map. In machine learning, $x$ and $y$ are weights and $f$ is the loss.

## Partial derivatives: freeze everything else

To find a slope with two inputs, change **one** input and keep the other fixed. The <Term def="The derivative of a many-input function with respect to one input, while all the other inputs are held fixed (treated as constants).">partial derivative</Term> of $f$ with respect to $x$ is

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h,\ y) - f(x,\ y)}{h}
$$

- $\partial$ is a curly "d", read "partial": a reminder that $f$ has other inputs, held still.
- $h$ is a tiny nudge to $x$ only ($y$ is the same in both terms); otherwise this is the ordinary derivative.

Freezing $y$ cuts the surface along a line, leaving an ordinary one-input curve called a **slice**; the partial derivative is the slope of that slice. In practice: when you differentiate with respect to $x$, treat every other letter **as if it were a number**.

<Steps title="Both partial derivatives of f(x, y) = x²y + 3y">
  <Step title="Freeze y, differentiate in x">
    Pretend $y$ is a number, say 5: then $f = 5x^2 + 15$, whose derivative is $10x$. In general, $x^2 y$ becomes $2x \cdot y$, and $3y$ has no $x$, so it vanishes. So $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Freeze x, differentiate in y">
    Now $x^2$ is just a coefficient, like the 5 in $5y$. So $x^2 y$ becomes $x^2$, and $3y$ becomes $3$: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Evaluate at the point (1, 2)">
    $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2 \times 1 \times 2 = 4$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 1^2 + 3 = 4$. Check these against the slices in the visual.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What the numbers mean">
    Near $(1, 2)$, nudging $x$ up by $0.01$ raises $f$ by about $4 \times 0.01 = 0.04$, and so does nudging $y$ up by $0.01$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

This answers the visual's puzzle: with $x$ frozen, $f = (x^2 + 3)\,y$ is a constant times $y$, a straight line in $y$, so its slope does not depend on $y$.

<Mistake title="Two classic slips">

Writing $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(3y) = 3$. It is $0$: with $y$ frozen, $3y$ is a constant. The opposite slip is dropping a frozen letter that multiplies $x$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 y) = 2xy$, not $2x$, just as $\frac{d}{dx}(5x^2) = 10x$ keeps its 5.

</Mistake>

## The gradient: all the slopes in one arrow

Collect the partial derivatives into a vector and you get the <Term def="The vector of all partial derivatives of a function, one entry per input. It points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is the steepest slope.">gradient</Term>, written $\nabla f$ (read "grad" or "nabla"):

$$
\nabla f(x, y) = \left[\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\ \right]
$$

With $n$ inputs the gradient has $n$ entries, so it has the **same shape as the input**. For $f = x^2 + y^2$, $\nabla f = [2x,\ 2y]$, which at $(1, 2)$ is $[2, 4]$. Every point on the map gets its own arrow.

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={30} />

Why is this arrow so useful? Walk a tiny distance $t$ in a direction given by a <Term def="A vector of length 1. It only describes a direction.">unit vector</Term> $u = [u_1, u_2]$. Your $x$ changes by $t\,u_1$ and your $y$ by $t\,u_2$, and each change raises the height by about "slope × distance moved". Divide the total by $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{slope in direction } u &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,u_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,u_2 \\
&= \nabla f \cdot u \;=\; \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

using the dot-product rule $a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert \cos\theta$ from [the dot-product lesson](#/learn/vectors-and-matrices/dot-product-and-norms), with $\theta$ the angle between $u$ and $\nabla f$ and $\lVert u \rVert = 1$. Now read off three facts:

- $\cos\theta = 1$ when $\theta = 0$: the ground rises fastest in the **direction of $\nabla f$**, with slope $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$.
- $\cos\theta = 0$ when $\theta = 90°$: at right angles to $\nabla f$ the height does not change, so you are walking **along a contour line**. The gradient is perpendicular to the contours.
- $\cos\theta = -1$ when $\theta = 180°$: the ground falls fastest along $-\nabla f$, the direction of **steepest descent**.

<Steps title="Steepest direction on f(x, y) = x² + 3y² at P = (−2, 0.5)">
  <Step title="Gradient">
    $\nabla f = [2x,\ 6y] = [2 \times (-2),\ 6 \times 0.5] = [-4,\ 3]$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Two compass directions">
    East, $u = [1, 0]$: slope $= -4 \times 1 + 3 \times 0 = -4$ (downhill). North, $u = [0, 1]$: slope $= 3$ (uphill).
  </Step>
  <Step title="The best direction">
    $\lVert \nabla f \rVert = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = 5$, so straight uphill is the unit direction $[-0.8,\ 0.6]$, with slope $(-4)(-0.8) + 3(0.6) = 5$. No direction beats 5.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Where rain flows">
    Rain runs straight downhill, along $-\nabla f$, towards $[0.8,\ -0.6]$: down the nearest wall of the oval bowl, not straight at the bottom $(0, 0)$, which lies in direction $[0.97,\ -0.24]$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>

The gradient $\nabla f$ points in the direction of **steepest ascent**, its length $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$ is how steep that is, and it is **perpendicular to the contour lines**. Its opposite, $-\nabla f$, is the direction of steepest descent, which is the direction training walks in.

</KeyIdea>

Two things to take from the map: where contour lines (drawn at equal height steps) are **packed closely** the ground is steep, so $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$ is large; and in the long valley, $-\nabla f$ points across the valley, not at the lowest point (this matters in the next lesson).

## Gradients of vector expressions

With many weights in one vector $w = [w_1, \dots, w_n]$, two gradients come up again and again.

**The dot product.** For a fixed input vector $x$, let $f(w) = w^\top x = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n$ (the dot product $w \cdot x$ written as a matrix product). Differentiating with respect to $w_1$, every term without $w_1$ vanishes and $w_1 x_1$ becomes $x_1$; the same happens for each weight, so

$$
\nabla_w \big(w^\top x\big) = x .
$$

The subscript in $\nabla_w$ means "with respect to $w$". At a hawker centre, if $w_i$ is how many plates of dish $i$ you order and $x_i$ its price, one more plate of dish $i$ raises the bill by exactly $x_i$.

**The squared length.** Let $f(w) = \lVert w \rVert^2 = w_1^2 + w_2^2 + \dots + w_n^2$, the squared L2 norm. With all other weights frozen, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_i} = 2w_i$, so

$$
\nabla_w \lVert w \rVert^2 = 2w .
$$

It points straight out from the origin, at right angles to the circular contours.

<VectorGradientCheck />

<Reveal label="Answer to the visual's “Why?”">

$\frac{(w_1 + h)^2 - w_1^2}{h} = \frac{2w_1 h + h^2}{h} = 2w_1 + h$, so the nudge estimate overshoots by exactly $h$. For $w^\top x$ the function is a flat, tilted plane, so every nudge gives the exact slope.

</Reveal>

### Putting them together: the gradient of a loss

Take a linear model with prediction $w^\top x$ for one example $(x, y)$, and the squared error $\ell(w) = (w^\top x - y)^2$. The chain rule says: differentiate the outside (the square gives $2(\text{inside})$), then multiply by the gradient of the inside, which is $x$ ($y$ is a constant). So

$$
\nabla_w\,\ell = 2\,(w^\top x - y)\,x .
$$

In words: **error × input × 2**. Averaging over $n$ examples gives the mean squared error gradient $\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i} (w^\top x_i - y_i)\,x_i$. Adding <Term def="A penalty λ‖w‖² added to the loss to keep the weights small; λ (lambda) sets how strong the penalty is.">L2 regularisation</Term> $\lambda \lVert w \rVert^2$ simply adds $2\lambda w$.

<Steps title="One example, by hand">
  <Step title="Set up">
    $w = [1, 2]$, input $x = [3, 1]$, target $y = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Prediction and error">
    $w^\top x = 1 \times 3 + 2 \times 1 = 5$, so the error is $5 - 4 = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Gradient of the squared error">
    $2 \times 1 \times [3, 1] = [6, 2]$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add L2 regularisation with λ = 0.5">
    $2\lambda w = 2 \times 0.5 \times [1, 2] = [1, 2]$, so the total gradient is $[6, 2] + [1, 2] = [7, 4]$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## In code: trust, but check

A **numerical gradient check** compares a formula with nudge estimates. This one nudges each weight up and down by $h$ (a "central difference"), which is far more accurate than nudging one way. The first row of `X` is your hand example.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

X = np.array([[3.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0],
              [2.0, 0.0]])        # 3 examples, 2 features each
y = np.array([4.0, 3.0, 5.0])
w = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
lam = 0.5                         # L2 strength

def loss(w):
    errors = X @ w - y
    return np.mean(errors ** 2) + lam * np.sum(w ** 2)

def grad_formula(w):
    n = len(y)
    return (2 / n) * X.T @ (X @ w - y) + 2 * lam * w

def grad_numeric(f, w, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(w)
    for i in range(len(w)):
        e = np.zeros_like(w)
        e[i] = h                  # nudge weight i only
        g[i] = (f(w + e) - f(w - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

print("formula:", grad_formula(w))
print("numeric:", grad_numeric(loss, w))`} />

`X.T @ (X @ w - y)` computes $\sum_i (w^\top x_i - y_i)\,x_i$ in one matrix product. Both lines print about $[0.333,\ 5.333]$ (averages over three examples). In PyTorch, <Term def="Automatic differentiation: the library records each operation as it runs, then applies the chain rule backwards to compute every gradient exactly.">autograd</Term> computes the gradient for you when you call `.backward()`.

<Reveal label="The hand example in PyTorch">

```python
import torch

w = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0], requires_grad=True)  # track gradients
x = torch.tensor([3.0, 1.0])                       # features
y = torch.tensor(4.0)                              # target

loss = (w @ x - y) ** 2 + 0.5 * (w ** 2).sum()    # error² + L2
loss.backward()                                    # fills w.grad

print(loss.item())
print(w.grad)
```

```text
3.5
tensor([7., 4.])
```

`w.grad` has the same shape as `w`, and it matches the $[7, 4]$ you computed by hand.

</Reveal>

<ExamTip>

- **Partial derivative**: freeze every other letter; terms without the variable vanish.
- **Gradient**: a vector, e.g. $\nabla(x^2 + y^2) = [2x, 2y]$, not the sum $2x + 2y$.
- **Memorise**: $\nabla_w \lVert w \rVert^2 = 2w$ (not $w$) and $\nabla_w (w^\top x) = x$.
- **Direction**: $\nabla f$ points to steepest ascent, $-\nabla f$ to steepest descent, at right angles to the contours; closer contours mean a longer gradient.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- A partial derivative is the slope of a one-input slice: freeze every other input and differentiate as usual.
- The gradient $\nabla f$ lists all the partial derivatives and has the same shape as the input.
- The slope in direction $u$ is $\nabla f \cdot u$: $\nabla f$ points straight uphill, its length is the steepest slope, and it is perpendicular to contours.
- $\nabla_w (w^\top x) = x$ and $\nabla_w \lVert w \rVert^2 = 2w$; with the chain rule they give the squared-error gradient $2(w^\top x - y)\,x$.
- A numerical gradient check catches mistakes in gradient formulas.

</Takeaways>
