A neural network is a function inside a function inside a function: multiply by weights, squash, multiply again, squash again, and finally score the result with a loss. To train it you need the slope of that loss with respect to every weight, even the ones buried deep inside. The tool that digs them out is the **chain rule**, and **backpropagation**, the algorithm that trains every network, is nothing more than the chain rule applied carefully, one step at a time.

<Analogy title="Gears">

Gear B turns 3 times for every turn of gear A, and gear C turns 2 times for every turn of B. How many times does C turn for each turn of A? You do not need to watch them spin: $3 \times 2 = 6$. When one thing drives another, which drives a third, the rates **multiply**.

</Analogy>

## Functions inside functions

Look at $y = (3x + 1)^2$. To compute it you do two jobs in order: first the **inner** job $u = 3x + 1$, then the **outer** job $y = u^2$. A function built by feeding one function's output into another is a <Term def="A function made by feeding the output of one function into another, written f(g(x)): first apply g, then apply f.">composite function</Term>, written $f(g(x))$: here $g(x) = 3x + 1$ is the inner function and $f(u) = u^2$ is the outer one.

Each job has its own rate of change. The inner one changes $u$ by $\frac{du}{dx} = 3$ for each unit of $x$. The outer one changes $y$ by $\frac{dy}{du} = 2u$ for each unit of $u$. The gears below show what happens when rates are chained.

## The chain rule

If $y$ depends on $u$, and $u$ depends on $x$, then

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}
$$

- $\frac{du}{dx}$ is the inner rate: how fast $u$ changes when $x$ changes.
- $\frac{dy}{du}$ is the outer rate: how fast $y$ changes when $u$ changes.
- Their product is the overall rate. The notation helps you remember: the $du$'s look as if they cancel.

One difference from gears: a gear's ratio is fixed, but here the outer rate $2u$ depends on where you are. So the chain rule always multiplies the rates **at the current point**.

Written with function names, the same rule reads

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\, f\big(g(x)\big) = f'\big(g(x)\big) \cdot g'(x).
$$

In words: **the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function**. "Evaluated at the inner function" matters. The outer function only ever sees $u = g(x)$, never $x$ itself, so its slope must be measured at $u$.

<KeyIdea>

The chain rule: rates multiply along a chain. $\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$: differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Differentiate $y = (3x + 1)^2$">
  <Step title="Name the inner part">
    $u = 3x + 1$, so $y = u^2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Differentiate each part">
    Outer: $\frac{dy}{du} = 2u$. Inner: $\frac{du}{dx} = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply, then put $u$ back">
    $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2u \times 3 = 6u = 6(3x + 1)$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check by expanding">
    $(3x+1)^2 = 9x^2 + 6x + 1$, whose derivative is $18x + 6 = 6(3x + 1)$. The same answer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="At $x = 1$">
    $u = 4$, so the outer rate is $2 \times 4 = 8$ and the inner rate is $3$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 8 \times 3 = 24$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Read that last step as nudges, as in the first lesson. Nudge $x$ from 1 to 1.01: $u$ moves by $3 \times 0.01 = 0.03$, from 4 to 4.03. Then $y$ moves by about $8 \times 0.03 = 0.24$, from 16 to about 16.24. Overall, $0.01$ in became $0.24$ out: a rate of 24. (Exactly, $4.03^2 = 16.2409$.)

The same move handles everything you will meet. In each row, the first factor is the outer slope (measured at the inner part) and the second is the inner slope:

| Function | Outer slope × inner slope |
|---|---|
| $e^{-x}$ | $e^{-x} \cdot (-1) = -e^{-x}$ |
| $e^{3x}$ | $e^{3x} \cdot 3 = 3e^{3x}$ |
| $\ln(2x + 1)$ | $\frac{1}{2x + 1} \cdot 2 = \frac{2}{2x + 1}$ |
| $(x^2 + 1)^3$ | $3(x^2 + 1)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2 + 1)^2$ |
| $\sigma(wx + b)$, as $x$ changes | $\sigma'(wx + b) \cdot w$ |

The first row proves the mirror rule for $e^{-x}$ from the previous lesson. The last row is a neuron: its slope is the sigmoid's slope at $wx + b$, times the weight.

<Mistake title="Evaluating the outer derivative at the wrong place">

For $(3x + 1)^2$, writing "$2x \times 3 = 6x$" uses the outer slope $2u$ at $u = x$ instead of at $u = 3x + 1$. At $x = 1$ that gives 6 instead of 24. The other classic slip is forgetting the inner factor altogether: $2(3x + 1)$, without the $\times 3$.

</Mistake>

## Long chains: the computational graph

Real models have many steps, so we draw them as a <Term def="A diagram of a calculation in which each node is one simple operation (add, multiply, sigmoid, square) and arrows show which values feed into which.">computational graph</Term>: one node per small operation, with arrows showing what feeds into what. Here is one neuron with a squared-error loss:

$$
z = w x + b, \qquad a = \sigma(z), \qquad L = (a - y)^2
$$

- $x$ is the input and $y$ the correct answer (the target). The model's knobs are the weight $w$ and the bias $b$.
- $z$ is the neuron's raw score, $a$ its output after the sigmoid, and $L$ the loss.

$L$ depends on several things at once ($w$, $b$, $x$), so we write $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ with a curly $\partial$. It is read "the <Term def="The slope of a function in one input's direction while all the other inputs are held fixed. Written with a curly ∂.">partial derivative</Term> of $L$ with respect to $w$": the slope in the $w$ direction while everything else stays fixed. (The next lesson covers partial derivatives properly.)

Training needs $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$. The chain from $w$ to $L$ runs $w \to z \to a \to L$, so the chain rule multiplies three local rates:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}}_{2(a - y)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}}_{\sigma'(z)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}}_{x}
$$

The efficient way to compute this is to walk the graph **backwards**, starting from $L$. Each node receives the slope of the loss with respect to its own value, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial (\text{node})}$, from the loss side. This slope is usually called the <Term def="Here: the derivative of the loss L with respect to one node's value, ∂L/∂(node). The next lesson extends the word to many inputs at once.">gradient</Term>, and the one arriving from the loss side is the **upstream** gradient. Each node multiplies it by its own **local** derivative and passes the result on towards the inputs. Step through it:

<GraphChainStepper />

With the starting numbers ($x = 2$, $w = 0.5$, $b = -1$, $y = 1$): $z = 0$, $a = 0.5$, $L = 0.25$, and

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(0.5 - 1) \times 0.25 \times 2 = (-1)(0.25)(2) = -0.5.
$$

The slope is negative, so increasing $w$ a little would lower the loss. That is exactly the information gradient descent needs. A network with millions of weights does the same thing: one forward pass to get the values, then one backward pass in which every node does "upstream × local". That backward pass is called <Term def="The algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight by applying the chain rule backwards through the computational graph, from the loss to the inputs.">backpropagation</Term>.

<ExamTip>

For a squared-error loss $L = (\hat{y} - y)^2$ with a prediction $\hat{y} = wx$, the gradient is the outer slope times the inner slope: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(\hat{y} - y) \cdot x$. Wrong options usually drop a piece: the $2$ (from the square) or the $x$ (from the inner function). Check each factor in turn.

</ExamTip>

PyTorch builds the computational graph as your code runs and does the backward pass for you when you call `.backward()`. Here is the neuron above:

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor(2.0)
y = torch.tensor(1.0)
w = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)
b = torch.tensor(-1.0, requires_grad=True)

z = w * x + b            # forward pass, one small step at a time
a = torch.sigmoid(z)
L = (a - y) ** 2

L.backward()             # backward pass: the chain rule, done for us
print("L =", L.item())
print("dL/dw =", w.grad.item())
print("dL/db =", b.grad.item())
```

```text
L = 0.25
dL/dw = -0.5
dL/db = -0.25
```

## Many small factors: vanishing gradients

A deep network is a long chain, so the gradient for an early weight is a product with **one factor per layer**. Each sigmoid layer multiplies the gradient by $\sigma'(z)$ times that layer's weight, and from the previous lesson you know $\sigma'(z) \le 0.25$. With weights of moderate size (around 1), ten sigmoid layers shrink the gradient to at most about

$$
0.25^{10} = \frac{1}{4^{10}} = \frac{1}{2^{20}} \approx \frac{1}{1\,000\,000} = 10^{-6}.
$$

(Mental-maths trick: $2^{10} = 1024 \approx 10^3$, so $2^{20} \approx 10^6$.) The first layers receive a gradient about a million times smaller than the last ones, so they barely change. This is the <Term def="When the gradients reaching the early layers of a deep network become so small that those layers almost stop learning. Caused by multiplying many factors smaller than 1 during backpropagation.">vanishing gradient</Term> problem. Factors bigger than 1 cause the opposite, an <Term def="When gradients grow huge as they are passed back through many layers, making training updates wildly too big and unstable.">exploding gradient</Term>: $1.5^{10} \approx 58$, and $2^{10} = 1024$. Very large weights can make a layer's factor bigger than 1, which is one way gradients explode.

<Analogy title="A photocopy of a photocopy">

Photocopy a page, then photocopy the copy, and keep going. Each copy loses only a little, but after ten rounds the picture is gone. Each layer of a deep sigmoid network passes back a slightly weakened copy of the gradient, and the losses multiply.

</Analogy>

<VanishingProduct />

This is why modern networks are built the way they are. **ReLU** has slope exactly 1 for positive inputs, so active units pass gradients back without shrinking them. Careful **weight initialisation** keeps each layer's overall factor close to 1. **Residual connections** add a shortcut around each block, giving the gradient a path whose factor is 1. You will meet all three in later modules.

<ExamTip>

Vanishing-gradient questions come in two flavours. **Why** it happens: backpropagation multiplies many derivatives smaller than 1 (the sigmoid's is at most 0.25), so the product shrinks exponentially with depth. **What fixes it**: ReLU-type activations, good initialisation, residual (skip) connections and <Term def="Rescaling the values inside a network (for example batch normalisation) so each layer's inputs keep a steady size.">normalisation</Term>; <Term def="Long short-term memory: a recurrent network designed to keep gradients from vanishing over long sequences.">LSTMs</Term> for recurrent networks. Exploding gradients are the same maths with factors above 1, and <Term def="Capping the size of the gradient before each update, so one huge gradient cannot wreck the weights.">gradient clipping</Term> is the usual fix.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- The chain rule: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}$, or $\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\, g'(x)$. Rates multiply.
- Evaluate the outer derivative at the inner value, and never forget the inner factor: $\frac{d}{dx}(3x+1)^2 = 6(3x+1)$.
- On a computational graph, the backward pass gives every node "upstream gradient × local derivative". For one neuron, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(a - y) \cdot \sigma'(z) \cdot x$.
- Backpropagation is this rule run backwards through the whole network; `loss.backward()` does it in PyTorch.
- A deep chain multiplies one factor per layer: factors below 1 vanish (sigmoid layers with weights near 1: $0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$), factors above 1 explode.

</Takeaways>
