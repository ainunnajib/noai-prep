In the last lesson a ball rolled downhill as if by magic. The real algorithm behind it trains almost every model you will meet, from linear regression to large language models, and it fits on one line: **gradient descent**. This lesson is about that line, and about the one number in it that most often makes training fail: the **learning rate**.

<Analogy title="Walking down Bukit Timah Hill in thick haze">

The haze is so thick you can only see your own feet. So you feel the slope under your shoes, take a step downhill, feel again, and step again. Where the ground is steep you take a big stride; where it is almost flat you shuffle. That is gradient descent.

</Analogy>

## The recipe: feel the slope, step the other way

The slope of the loss at the current parameter value is its derivative $L'(w)$, and its **sign** tells you which way is uphill:

- If $L'(w) > 0$, the loss goes *up* as $w$ increases, so downhill is to the left: make $w$ smaller.
- If $L'(w) < 0$, the loss goes *down* as $w$ increases, so downhill is to the right: make $w$ bigger.

Both cases are handled by one rule: **subtract a small multiple of the slope**.

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta\, L'(w)
$$

- $w$ is the current value of the parameter, and $w_{\text{new}}$ is the value after one step.
- $L'(w)$ is the slope at the current $w$.
- $\eta$ (the Greek letter eta) is the <Term def="A small positive number that sets how big each gradient descent step is. You choose it before training.">learning rate</Term>. It is a <Term def="A setting you choose before training (such as the learning rate or the number of steps), as opposed to a parameter, which training learns.">hyperparameter</Term>: you choose it, training does not learn it.
- The minus sign makes the step go *against* the slope, i.e. downhill.

One application of this rule is one <Term def="One update of the parameters: one step of gradient descent.">iteration</Term>, and training repeats it many times. Notice that the step is *proportional* to the slope. Far from the bottom the ground is steep, so the steps are big; near the bottom the slope shrinks towards 0, so the steps shrink too and the walker settles instead of jumping about (as long as $\eta$ is sensible).

With many parameters, the slopes in every parameter's direction form the **gradient** vector $\nabla L$, and the rule becomes

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w})
$$

where the arrow $\leftarrow$ means "replace with". The gradient points in the direction of **steepest ascent** (straight uphill), so $-\nabla L$ points in the direction of **steepest descent**. That is where the name comes from.

<Steps title="Two steps of gradient descent by hand">
  <Step title="Set up">
    Loss $L(w) = w^2$, whose lowest point is at $w = 0$. Start at $w = 3$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 1: slope">
    $L'(w) = 2w$, so at $w = 3$ the slope is $2 \times 3 = 6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 1: update">
    $w_{\text{new}} = 3 - 0.1 \times 6 = 3 - 0.6 = 2.4$. The loss drops from $3^2 = 9$ to $2.4^2 = 5.76$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 2">
    Slope $2 \times 2.4 = 4.8$, step $0.1 \times 4.8 = 0.48$, so $w = 2.4 - 0.48 = 1.92$ and the loss is $3.6864$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spot the pattern">
    $2.4 = 0.8 \times 3$ and $1.92 = 0.8 \times 2.4$. Every step multiplies $w$ by the same number, $0.8 = 1 - 2\eta$. Remember this: it explains everything in the next section.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## How big a step? The learning rate

Play with the presets above and you will see four behaviours:

- **Too small** ($\eta = 0.02$): the ball crawls. Each step only removes 4% of the distance to the bottom, so training wastes time and may stop long before the minimum.
- **About right** ($\eta = 0.3$): fast, smooth convergence.
- **Large but below the limit** ($\eta = 0.85$): every step *overshoots* the bottom and lands on the other side, but a bit closer each time. The ball zig-zags in.
- **Above the limit** ($\eta = 1.05$): every overshoot is bigger than the last. The walk <Term def="Gets further and further from the minimum instead of settling: the loss grows, often until it becomes infinity or NaN.">diverges</Term>.

Why is the limit exactly 1 for $L(w) = w^2$? Put the slope $2w$ into the update rule:

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \cdot 2w = (1 - 2\eta)\, w
$$

Every step multiplies $w$, which is the distance from the minimum at 0, by $r = 1 - 2\eta$. If $r$ is between $-1$ and $1$, the distance shrinks every step; if $r$ is negative, $w$ flips sign every step (the zig-zag); if $r < -1$, it grows. And $1 - 2\eta > -1$ means $\eta < 1$.

The same algebra works for any bowl $L(w) = \tfrac{c}{2}w^2$. Its slope is $L'(w) = c\,w$, and the number $c = L''(w)$ is its <Term def="How sharply a curve bends: its second derivative. A big curvature means a steep, narrow bowl.">curvature</Term>, how sharply the bowl bends. Then $w_{\text{new}} = (1 - \eta c)\,w$, so gradient descent converges exactly when

$$
0 < \eta < \frac{2}{c}.
$$

The steeper the bowl, the smaller the largest safe learning rate. One more special value: $\eta = 1/c$ gives $r = 0$, which lands on the minimum in a single step.

<StepMultiplier />

<KeyIdea>

Each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $1 - \eta c$. Gradient descent converges only if $\eta < 2/c$, where $c$ is the curvature of the **steepest** direction. Too small crawls; just below the limit zig-zags; above it diverges.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Is this learning rate safe?">
  <Step title="Find the curvature">
    Loss $L(w) = 3w^2$. Its slope is $L'(w) = 6w$, so its curvature is $c = L''(w) = 6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the limit">
    Safe learning rates satisfy $\eta < 2/c = 2/6 = 1/3 \approx 0.33$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test η = 0.5">
    $r = 1 - 0.5 \times 6 = -2$, so $|r| > 1$. Starting from $w = 1$: $1 \to -2 \to 4 \to -8$. It diverges.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test η = 0.1">
    $r = 1 - 0.1 \times 6 = 0.4$. Starting from $w = 1$: $1 \to 0.4 \to 0.16 \to 0.064$. Smooth convergence.
  </Step>
</Steps>

In real training you cannot watch the ball, but you can watch the loss after every step. The small chart in the first visual shows the four signatures: a nearly flat line (too small), a quick drop that levels off (good), a drop that wobbles (large), and a curve that shoots upwards (diverging). A diverging run often ends with the loss printed as `nan` ("not a number") once the numbers become too large for the computer.

<Mistake title="“A bigger learning rate always trains faster”">

Only up to the limit $2/c$. Past it, a bigger $\eta$ makes training fail completely. And if the loss is growing or swinging wildly, training for more epochs does not help: lower the learning rate first (a common first try is 10 times smaller).

</Mistake>

## Two parameters: zig-zags in long valleys

Real models have many parameters. With two, you can draw the landscape from above like a hiking map. Each <Term def="A line joining points that have the same loss, like the height lines on a hiking map.">contour line</Term> joins points of equal loss. The gradient now has two entries, $\nabla L = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}\right]$, each a <Term def="The slope in one parameter's direction while all the other parameters are held fixed.">partial derivative</Term>, and it always crosses the contour lines at right angles, pointing straight uphill.

Take the bowl $L(w_1, w_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(0.1\,w_1^2 + w_2^2\right)$. Its gradient is $\nabla L = [\,0.1\,w_1,\; w_2\,]$, so the curvature is $0.1$ along $w_1$ (a gentle slope) and $1$ along $w_2$ (steep walls). The contours are long, thin ovals: a narrow valley. Now the trouble starts:

- The **steep** direction sets the speed limit: $\eta < 2/1 = 2$.
- At $\eta = 1.8$, the $w_2$ coordinate is multiplied by $1 - 1.8 = -0.8$ each step, so it bounces from wall to wall.
- Meanwhile $w_1$ is multiplied by $1 - 1.8 \times 0.1 = 0.82$, so each step only moves 18% of the remaining way along the valley.
- You cannot raise $\eta$ to hurry $w_1$ along, because $w_2$ would then diverge.

<Analogy title="A ball in a sloping drain">

Roll a ball down a narrow, sloping drain and it bangs from one wall to the other instead of rolling neatly along the bottom. The walls are steep and the slope along the drain is gentle: exactly the shape of this loss.

</Analogy>

<GD2DContours />

With many parameters the rule is the same: the curvature of the steepest direction (textbooks write it $\lambda_{\max}$: the largest curvature in any direction, which comes from the Hessian you met in the last lesson) caps the learning rate at $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and the shallowest direction then decides how many steps you need.

Where do long valleys come from? For a linear model, the curvature along a weight grows with the size of its feature's values. For example, the Grab loss $L(w) = \text{mean}\big((y - wx)^2\big)$ has curvature $L''(w) = 2 \times \text{mean}(x^2)$. So a feature measured in large units (income in dollars, about 50,000) makes its weight's direction enormously steep compared with a feature such as age in years. The fix changes the data, not the algorithm. <Term def="Rescaling a feature so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1: z = (x − mean) / standard deviation.">Standardising</Term> each feature,

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma},
$$

where $\mu$ (mu) is the feature's mean and $\sigma$ (sigma) its standard deviation, gives every feature a similar spread. The curvatures become similar, the valley becomes a round bowl (when the features are not strongly correlated), the gradient points straight at the minimum, and one learning rate suits every direction.

## Gradient descent in code

Here is gradient descent from scratch for the Grab model from the last lesson, $\text{fare} = w \times \text{distance}$. The gradient of the MSE loss is $L'(w) = \text{mean}\big(-2x\,(y - wx)\big)$, and the curvature is $2 \times \text{mean}(x^2) = 2 \times 7.5 = 15$, so the safe limit is $\eta < 2/15 \approx 0.13$. Run it, then try `lr = 0.01` (crawl), `lr = 0.12` (zig-zag) and `lr = 0.14` (diverge).

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])   # distance (km)
y = np.array([3.0, 4.0, 7.0, 7.0])   # fare ($)

def loss(w):
    return np.mean((y - w * x) ** 2)

def grad(w):
    # dL/dw = mean of -2 * x * (y - w x)
    return np.mean(-2 * x * (y - w * x))

w = 0.0      # starting guess
lr = 0.05    # learning rate (try 0.01, 0.12 and 0.14)
for step in range(8):
    g = grad(w)
    print(f"step {step}: w = {w:.4f}  loss = {loss(w):.4f}  gradient = {g:.4f}")
    w = w - lr * g
print(f"final w = {w:.4f}")`} />

With `lr = 0.05` the multiplier is $1 - 0.05 \times 15 = 0.25$, so the distance to $w = 2$ shrinks by a factor of 4 every step: $2 \to 0.5 \to 0.125 \to \dots$. NOAI Final programming tasks ask for from-scratch loops like this one in NumPy (the 2025 Final included gradient descent for linear regression). In PyTorch, the library computes the gradient for you (`loss.backward()`) and an optimiser applies the update (`optimizer.step()`):

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])   # distance (km)
y = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0, 7.0, 7.0])   # fare ($)

w = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)   # the parameter to learn
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD([w], lr=0.05)    # plain gradient descent

for step in range(5):
    loss = ((y - w * x) ** 2).mean()   # MSE loss
    optimizer.zero_grad()              # clear the old gradient
    loss.backward()                    # compute dL/dw into w.grad
    print(f"step {step}: w = {w.item():.4f}  loss = {loss.item():.4f}  grad = {w.grad.item():.4f}")
    optimizer.step()                   # w <- w - lr * w.grad

print(f"final w = {w.item():.4f}")
```

```text
step 0: w = 0.0000  loss = 30.7500  grad = -30.0000
step 1: w = 1.5000  loss = 2.6250  grad = -7.5000
step 2: w = 1.8750  loss = 0.8672  grad = -1.8750
step 3: w = 1.9688  loss = 0.7573  grad = -0.4688
step 4: w = 1.9922  loss = 0.7505  grad = -0.1172
final w = 1.9980
```

The numbers match the NumPy version exactly: `torch.optim.SGD` with one parameter and the whole dataset is the same update $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,L'(w)$. Forgetting `optimizer.zero_grad()` is a classic bug: PyTorch *adds* new gradients to the old ones, so the steps become wrong.

<ExamTip>

How NOAI asks about this, and what to have ready:

- **One-step arithmetic**: $w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \times \text{gradient}$. Watch the signs: a *negative* gradient makes $w$ *increase*.
- **Diagnosis**: loss oscillating wildly, exploding or becoming NaN means the learning rate is **too high**; loss falling very slowly means it is **too low**.
- **Direction**: the gradient points to steepest *ascent*; the negative gradient to steepest *descent*.
- **Limit**: converges only for $\eta < 2/c$. For $L = a\,w^2$ the curvature is $c = 2a$, so $\eta < 1/a$.
- **Feature scaling** makes gradient descent converge faster and more stably.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Gradient descent repeats $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$: feel the slope, then step against it.
- The learning rate $\eta$ scales every step: too small crawls, too large overshoots and zig-zags, and above $2/c$ it diverges.
- For a bowl with curvature $c$, each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $1 - \eta c$.
- In long, narrow valleys the steepest direction caps $\eta$, so the path zig-zags and crawls along the shallow direction.
- Standardising features makes the bowl rounder, so one learning rate suits every direction.

</Takeaways>
