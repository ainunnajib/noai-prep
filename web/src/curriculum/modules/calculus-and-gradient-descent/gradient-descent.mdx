Every model, from a straight-line fit to a chatbot, learns the same way: it adjusts its numbers to shrink a score of how wrong it is. The search uses one short rule, **gradient descent**: the gradient from the previous lesson points uphill, so step the other way. This lesson builds the **loss landscape** the rule walks on, then the rule itself, and the **learning rate**, the number that most often makes training fail.

<Analogy title="Downhill in thick haze">

You are in Bukit Batok Nature Park in haze so thick you can only see your own feet, and you want the lowest ground. You can feel which way the ground slopes under your shoes, so you step downhill, feel again and step again: big strides where it is steep, small shuffles where it is nearly flat. A model in training is in the same position: it can compute its loss and gradient *here*, but never sees the whole landscape.

</Analogy>

## The loss landscape

Start with the smallest possible model. You record four Grab rides:

| Ride | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| distance $x$ (km) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| fare $y$ (\$) | 3 | 4 | 7 | 7 |

The model guesses **fare = $w$ × distance**, where $w$ (dollars per km) is its one <Term def="A number inside a model that training is allowed to change. Different parameter values give different predictions.">parameter</Term>. To compare different values of $w$ we need a <Term def="A formula that turns all of a model's mistakes on the data into one number: 0 means perfect, bigger means worse.">loss function</Term>, a score of how wrong the model is. For predicting numbers the usual choice is the mean squared error:

$$
L(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - w\,x_i\big)^2
$$

Here $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the distance and fare of ride $i$, $n = 4$ rides, and $y_i - w\,x_i$ is the error on ride $i$ (real minus predicted fare): square each error, then average. For $w = 1$ the errors are $2, 2, 4, 3$, so $L(1) = \frac{4 + 4 + 16 + 9}{4} = 8.25$. For $w = 2$ they are $1, 0, 1, -1$, so $L(2) = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75$: much better.

Every value of $w$ gives one loss value. Plot them all and you get the <Term def="The graph of the loss against the model's parameters. Low points are good parameter values; training searches for them.">loss landscape</Term>.

<LossBuilder />

<KeyIdea>

**Training means searching the loss landscape for the parameters at its lowest point.** The Grab landscape is a single bowl, $L(w) = 7.5\,(w - 2)^2 + 0.75$, so the best fare is \$2 per km.

</KeyIdea>

With two parameters ($\text{fare} = w\,x + b$) the landscape becomes a surface, drawn as the contour maps of the previous lesson. A neural network's landscape has millions of dimensions, and nobody can draw it.

Real landscapes are rarely one smooth bowl. A <Term def="A point that is lower than everything close to it, but not necessarily the lowest point overall.">local minimum</Term> is lower than everything *nearby*; the <Term def="The lowest point of the whole function: no other parameter value gives a smaller loss.">global minimum</Term> is the lowest point *anywhere*. The ball below follows the only rule the walker in the haze can follow: move in the direction that goes downhill.

<LandscapeHiker />

The ball stops wherever the ground is flat, with no way of knowing whether a deeper valley exists elsewhere. So **where you start decides which valley you end in**, unless the landscape is a single bowl (a *convex* function: next lesson).

## The recipe: feel the slope, step the other way

The slope of the loss at the current parameter value is its derivative $L'(w)$, and its **sign** tells you which way is uphill:

- If $L'(w) > 0$, the loss goes *up* as $w$ increases, so downhill is to the left: make $w$ smaller.
- If $L'(w) < 0$, the loss goes *down* as $w$ increases, so downhill is to the right: make $w$ bigger.

Both cases are handled by one rule: **subtract a small multiple of the slope**.

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta\, L'(w)
$$

- $w$ is the current parameter value and $L'(w)$ the slope there.
- $\eta$ (eta) is the <Term def="A small positive number that sets how big each gradient descent step is. You choose it before training.">learning rate</Term>, a <Term def="A setting you choose before training (such as the learning rate or the number of steps), as opposed to a parameter, which training learns.">hyperparameter</Term>: you choose it, training does not learn it.
- The minus sign makes the step go *against* the slope, i.e. downhill.

One application is one <Term def="One update of the parameters: one step of gradient descent.">iteration</Term>, and training repeats it many times. The step is *proportional* to the slope: big where it is steep and shrinking towards 0 near the bottom, so the walker settles (if $\eta$ is sensible).

With many parameters, the slopes form the **gradient** vector $\nabla L$ from the previous lesson, and the rule becomes

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\, \nabla L(\mathbf{w})
$$

where $\leftarrow$ means "replace with". The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, so $-\nabla L$ is the direction of **steepest descent**: hence the name.

<Steps title="Two steps of gradient descent by hand">
  <Step title="Set up">
    Loss $L(w) = w^2$, whose lowest point is at $w = 0$. Start at $w = 3$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 1: slope">
    $L'(w) = 2w$, so at $w = 3$ the slope is $2 \times 3 = 6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 1: update">
    $w_{\text{new}} = 3 - 0.1 \times 6 = 3 - 0.6 = 2.4$. The loss drops from $3^2 = 9$ to $2.4^2 = 5.76$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Step 2">
    Slope $2 \times 2.4 = 4.8$, step $0.1 \times 4.8 = 0.48$, so $w = 2.4 - 0.48 = 1.92$ and the loss is $3.6864$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spot the pattern">
    $2.4 = 0.8 \times 3$ and $1.92 = 0.8 \times 2.4$. Every step multiplies $w$ by the same number, $0.8 = 1 - 2\eta$. Remember this: it explains everything in the next section.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## How big a step? The learning rate

The presets above show four behaviours:

- **Too small** ($\eta = 0.02$): the ball crawls, removing only 4% of the distance to the bottom per step, so training may stop long before the minimum.
- **About right** ($\eta = 0.3$): fast, smooth convergence.
- **Large but below the limit** ($\eta = 0.85$): every step *overshoots* to the other side, but a bit closer each time. The ball zig-zags in.
- **Above the limit** ($\eta = 1.05$): every overshoot is bigger than the last. The walk <Term def="Gets further and further from the minimum instead of settling: the loss grows, often until it becomes infinity or NaN.">diverges</Term>.

Why is the limit exactly 1 for $L(w) = w^2$? Put the slope $2w$ into the update rule:

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \cdot 2w = (1 - 2\eta)\, w
$$

Every step multiplies $w$, the distance from the minimum at 0, by $r = 1 - 2\eta$. If $r$ is between $-1$ and $1$ the distance shrinks; if $r$ is negative, $w$ flips sign every step (the zig-zag); if $r < -1$ it grows. And $1 - 2\eta > -1$ means $\eta < 1$.

The same algebra works for any bowl $L(w) = \tfrac{c}{2}w^2$. Its slope is $c\,w$, and $c = L''(w)$ is its <Term def="How sharply a curve bends: its second derivative. A big curvature means a sharply bending, narrow bowl.">curvature</Term>, how sharply it bends. Then $w_{\text{new}} = (1 - \eta c)\,w$, so gradient descent converges exactly when

$$
0 < \eta < \frac{2}{c}.
$$

Sharper bowls need smaller learning rates. This is about curvature, not steepness: $L = (w - 100)^2$ is very steep at $w = 0$, yet any $\eta < 1$ is safe because its curvature is only 2. And $\eta = 1/c$ gives $r = 0$: the minimum in a single step.

<StepMultiplier />

<KeyIdea>

Each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $1 - \eta c$. Gradient descent converges only if $\eta < 2/c$, where $c$ is the **largest curvature** (the most sharply curved direction). Too small crawls; just below the limit zig-zags; above it diverges.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Is this learning rate safe?">
  <Step title="Find the curvature">
    Loss $L(w) = 3w^2$. Its slope is $L'(w) = 6w$, so its curvature is $c = L''(w) = 6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the limit">
    Safe learning rates satisfy $\eta < 2/c = 2/6 = 1/3 \approx 0.33$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test η = 0.5">
    $r = 1 - 0.5 \times 6 = -2$, so $|r| > 1$. Starting from $w = 1$: $1 \to -2 \to 4 \to -8$. It diverges.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test η = 0.1">
    $r = 1 - 0.1 \times 6 = 0.4$. Starting from $w = 1$: $1 \to 0.4 \to 0.16 \to 0.064$. Smooth convergence.
  </Step>
</Steps>

In real training you watch the loss, not a ball. The chart under the rolling ball shows the four signatures: nearly flat (too small), a quick drop that levels off (good), a wobbly drop (large) and a curve shooting upwards (diverging), often ending with the loss printed as `nan` ("not a number").

<Mistake title="“A bigger learning rate always trains faster”">

Only up to the limit $2/c$. Past it, a bigger $\eta$ makes training fail completely. And if the loss is growing or swinging wildly, training for more <Term def="One full pass through the training data. Training usually runs for many epochs.">epochs</Term> does not help: lower the learning rate first (a common first try is 10 times smaller).

</Mistake>

## Two parameters: zig-zags in long valleys

On a contour map of the loss, $-\nabla L$ crosses the <Term def="A line joining points that have the same loss, like the height lines on a hiking map.">contour lines</Term> at right angles (previous lesson), so in a long, narrow valley it points mostly *across* the valley, down the nearest wall, instead of along it towards the lowest point.

Take the bowl $L(w_1, w_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(0.1\,w_1^2 + w_2^2\right)$. Its gradient is $\nabla L = [\,0.1\,w_1,\; w_2\,]$, so the curvature is $0.1$ along $w_1$ (gently curved) and $1$ along $w_2$ (sharply curved: steep walls). The contours are long, thin ovals: a narrow valley. Now the trouble starts:

- The **sharply curved** direction sets the speed limit: $\eta < 2/1 = 2$.
- At $\eta = 1.8$, the $w_2$ coordinate is multiplied by $1 - 1.8 = -0.8$ each step, so it bounces from wall to wall.
- Meanwhile $w_1$ is multiplied by $1 - 1.8 \times 0.1 = 0.82$, so each step only moves 18% of the remaining way along the valley.
- You cannot raise $\eta$ to hurry $w_1$ along, because $w_2$ would then diverge.

It is a ball rolling down a narrow, sloping drain: steep walls, a gentle slope along the bottom, so it bangs from wall to wall.

<GD2DContours />

With many parameters the largest curvature, $\lambda_{\max}$ (lambda max), caps $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and the most gently curved direction decides how many steps you need (more in the next lesson).

Long valleys often come from features on different scales. For a linear model the curvature along a weight grows with its feature's values (the Grab loss has $L''(w) = 2 \times \text{mean}(x^2)$), so income in dollars (about 50,000) makes its weight's direction far more sharply curved than age in years. The fix changes the data, not the algorithm. <Term def="Rescaling a feature so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1: z = (x − mean) / standard deviation.">Standardising</Term> each feature,

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma},
$$

with mean $\mu$ (mu) and standard deviation $\sigma$ (sigma), gives every feature a similar spread, so the valley becomes a rounder bowl (when the features are not strongly correlated) and one learning rate suits every direction.

## Gradient descent in code

Here is gradient descent from scratch for the Grab model, $\text{fare} = w \times \text{distance}$. The MSE gradient is $L'(w) = \text{mean}\big(-2x\,(y - wx)\big)$ and the curvature is $2 \times \text{mean}(x^2) = 15$, so the limit is $\eta < 2/15 \approx 0.13$. Run it, then try `lr = 0.01` (crawl), `0.12` (zig-zag) and `0.14` (diverge).

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])   # distance (km)
y = np.array([3.0, 4.0, 7.0, 7.0])   # fare ($)

def loss(w):
    return np.mean((y - w * x) ** 2)

def grad(w):
    # dL/dw = mean of -2 * x * (y - w x)
    return np.mean(-2 * x * (y - w * x))

w = 0.0      # starting guess
lr = 0.05    # learning rate (try 0.01, 0.12 and 0.14)
for step in range(8):
    g = grad(w)
    print(f"step {step}: w = {w:.4f}  loss = {loss(w):.4f}  gradient = {g:.4f}")
    w = w - lr * g
print(f"final w = {w:.4f}")`} />

With `lr = 0.05` the multiplier is $1 - 0.05 \times 15 = 0.25$, so the distance to $w = 2$ shrinks fourfold every step: $2 \to 0.5 \to 0.125$. NOAI Final tasks ask for from-scratch NumPy loops like this (the 2025 Final included gradient descent for linear regression).

<Reveal label="The same loop in PyTorch">

PyTorch computes the gradient (`loss.backward()`) and an optimiser applies the update (`optimizer.step()`):

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])   # distance (km)
y = torch.tensor([3.0, 4.0, 7.0, 7.0])   # fare ($)

w = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)   # the parameter to learn
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD([w], lr=0.05)    # plain gradient descent

for step in range(5):
    loss = ((y - w * x) ** 2).mean()   # MSE loss
    optimizer.zero_grad()              # clear the old gradient
    loss.backward()                    # compute dL/dw into w.grad
    print(f"step {step}: w = {w.item():.4f}  loss = {loss.item():.4f}  grad = {w.grad.item():.4f}")
    optimizer.step()                   # w <- w - lr * w.grad

print(f"final w = {w.item():.4f}")
```

```text
step 0: w = 0.0000  loss = 30.7500  grad = -30.0000
step 1: w = 1.5000  loss = 2.6250  grad = -7.5000
step 2: w = 1.8750  loss = 0.8672  grad = -1.8750
step 3: w = 1.9688  loss = 0.7573  grad = -0.4688
step 4: w = 1.9922  loss = 0.7505  grad = -0.1172
final w = 1.9980
```

The numbers match the NumPy version: `torch.optim.SGD` on the whole dataset is the same update $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,L'(w)$. Forgetting `optimizer.zero_grad()` is a classic bug: PyTorch *adds* new gradients to the old ones.

</Reveal>

<ExamTip>

How NOAI asks about this:

- **One step**: $w_{\text{new}} = w - \eta \times \text{gradient}$; a *negative* gradient makes $w$ *increase*.
- **Diagnosis**: loss oscillating, exploding or NaN means the learning rate is **too high**; loss falling very slowly means **too low**.
- **Limit**: $\eta < 2/c$ with $c$ the curvature (not the slope); for $L = a\,w^2$ that is $\eta < 1/a$.
- **Feature scaling** makes gradient descent converge faster and more stably.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Gradient descent repeats $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w})$: feel the slope, then step against it.
- The learning rate $\eta$ scales every step: too small crawls, too large overshoots and zig-zags, and above $2/c$ it diverges.
- For a bowl with curvature $c$, each step multiplies the distance to the minimum by $1 - \eta c$.
- In long, narrow valleys the sharpest direction caps $\eta$, so the path zig-zags and crawls along the gentle one.
- Standardising features makes the bowl rounder, so one learning rate suits every direction.

</Takeaways>
