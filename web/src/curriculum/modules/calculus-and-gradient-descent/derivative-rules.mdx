In the last lesson you found the derivative of $x^2$ the slow way: secants, a limit and some algebra. In the Prelim you have about 36 seconds per question, so nobody does that. Instead, a handful of **rules** give the answer in one line. This lesson covers every rule NOAI expects, including the two that appear in almost every neural network: the derivatives of the **sigmoid** and of **ReLU**.

<Analogy title="Times tables for slopes">

You do not work out $7 \times 8$ by adding seven eights each time; you just know it is 56. Derivative rules work the same way. Each rule is a pattern that the zoom-in method always produces, so once you know the pattern you never need the limit again.

</Analogy>

## The power rule

For any fixed number $n$,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\, x^n = n\, x^{n-1}
$$

- $\frac{d}{dx}$ means "the derivative with respect to $x$ of", so the left side is the slope of the curve $y = x^n$.
- In words: **bring the power down in front, then lower the power by one**.
- $x^3$ becomes $3x^2$; $x^2$ becomes $2x^1 = 2x$, exactly what the limit gave last lesson; $x$ (which is $x^1$) becomes $1 \cdot x^0 = 1$.

It works for negative and fractional powers too. $\frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}$ becomes $-1 \cdot x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}$, and $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ becomes $\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$.

## Constants, multiples and sums

Three helper rules let you differentiate any polynomial term by term:

| Rule | In symbols | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A constant has slope 0 | $\frac{d}{dx}\, c = 0$ | A flat line never rises. |
| Constant multiples stay | $\frac{d}{dx}\big[c\, f(x)\big] = c\, f'(x)$ | Stretching a graph 3 times taller makes every slope 3 times steeper. |
| Sums split up | $\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x) + g(x)\big] = f'(x) + g'(x)$ | The rise of a sum is the sum of the rises. |

<Steps title="Differentiate $f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x^2 + 5x - 7$">
  <Step title="Split into terms">
    Differentiate $3x^4$, $-2x^2$, $5x$ and $-7$ separately, then add the results.
  </Step>
  <Step title="$3x^4$">
    The power rule gives $4x^3$, and the multiple 3 stays: $3 \times 4x^3 = 12x^3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="$-2x^2$ and $5x$">
    $-2 \times 2x = -4x$, and $5 \times 1 = 5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="$-7$">
    A constant: its derivative is $0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add up">
    $f'(x) = 12x^3 - 4x + 5$. At $x = 1$ the slope is $12 - 4 + 5 = 13$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The second derivative

The derivative $f'(x)$ is itself a function, so you can differentiate it again. The result is the <Term def="The derivative of the derivative. It tells you how fast the slope is changing, which is how sharply the graph bends.">second derivative</Term>, written $f''(x)$ or $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. It measures how fast the **slope** changes, which is how sharply the curve bends:

- $f''(x) > 0$: the slope is increasing, so the curve bends **upwards**, like a bowl.
- $f''(x) < 0$: the slope is decreasing, so the curve bends **downwards**, like a dome.

For $f(x) = x^3$: $f'(x) = 3x^2$ and then $f''(x) = 3 \times 2x = 6x$. For a loss, a bowl shape ($L'' > 0$) at a point where $L' = 0$ means a minimum. The curvature lesson at the end of this module builds on this.

## $e^x$: the function that is its own slope

The number $e \approx 2.718$ is special for one reason:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\, e^x = e^x
$$

At every point, the **slope of $e^x$ equals its height**. At $x = 0$ the height is $1$ and so is the slope; at $x = 2$ both are about $7.39$.

Other bases do not have this property. The slope of $2^x$ is only about $0.69$ times its height, and the slope of $3^x$ is about $1.10$ times its height. $e$ is the base, between 2 and 3, where that factor is exactly 1. (In general $\frac{d}{dx} b^x = (\ln b)\, b^x$, and $\ln e = 1$.)

<ExpSelfSlope />

One relative of $e^x$ is needed below: $e^{-x}$, the mirror image of $e^x$ in the $y$-axis. A mirror keeps every height but turns uphill into downhill, so the slope of $e^{-x}$ is **minus** its height:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\, e^{-x} = -e^{-x}
$$

(The next lesson's chain rule proves this in one line.)

<Mistake title="Do not use the power rule on $e^x$">

The power rule needs the **power** to be a fixed number, as in $x^3$. In $e^x$ the variable is in the power, so "bring the power down" gives the wrong answer $x e^{x-1}$. The right answer is simply $e^x$.

</Mistake>

## $\ln x$: slope $1/x$

The natural log $\ln x$ undoes $e^x$: its graph is the graph of $e^x$ reflected in the line $y = x$, which swaps every rise with its run. On $e^x$, at height $x$ the slope is $x$ (height equals slope). Swapping rise and run turns a slope of $x$ into $\frac{1}{x}$, so

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \ln x = \frac{1}{x} \qquad (x > 0).
$$

Switch the visual above to **ln x** to see it. This rule is behind the logs in cross-entropy: the loss $-\ln p$ for a correct class with predicted probability $p$ has slope $-\frac{1}{p}$. If the model gives the right answer only $p = 0.1$, the slope is $-10$, a strong push to raise $p$; at $p = 0.9$ it is only about $-1.1$.

## Products and quotients

For two functions $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ multiplied together, the <Term def="The rule for differentiating a product: (uv)′ = u′v + uv′.">product rule</Term> says

$$
(uv)' = u'v + uv'.
$$

It is **not** $u'v'$. Picture $u$ and $v$ as the sides of a rectangle whose area is $uv$. When both sides grow a little, the area gains a strip along each side, and the tiny corner piece is too small to count.

<ProductRuleRectangle />

For a fraction, the <Term def="The rule for differentiating a fraction: (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v².">quotient rule</Term> is

$$
\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}.
$$

The order matters because of the minus sign: say "bottom times derivative of top, minus top times derivative of bottom, all over bottom squared".

<Steps title="Two quick examples">
  <Step title="Product: $x^2 e^x$">
    $u = x^2$, $u' = 2x$; $v = e^x$, $v' = e^x$. So $(x^2 e^x)' = 2x\,e^x + x^2 e^x = x e^x (2 + x)$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Quotient: $\dfrac{1}{x}$, as a check">
    $u = 1$, $u' = 0$; $v = x$, $v' = 1$. So $\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)' = \dfrac{0 \cdot x - 1 \cdot 1}{x^2} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, matching the power rule.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The sigmoid's famous derivative

The <Term def="The S-shaped function σ(x) = 1/(1 + e^(−x)). It squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, so it is used to turn a score into a probability.">sigmoid</Term> $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$ squashes any score into a probability between 0 and 1. It is a fraction, so the quotient rule finds its slope. Step through the algebra:

<SigmoidDerivation />

<KeyIdea>

$$
\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\,\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big)
$$

The slope of the sigmoid is "output times one minus output". A network has already computed $\sigma(x)$ on its way forward, so the slope costs just one subtraction and one multiplication.

</KeyIdea>

**How big can it get?** $\sigma(x)$ and $1 - \sigma(x)$ are two numbers that add up to 1. The product of two such numbers is largest when they are equal, $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$, which happens at $x = 0$. So the sigmoid's slope is **never more than 0.25**. Far from 0 it is tiny: at $x = 5$, $\sigma \approx 0.9933$ and the slope is about $0.9933 \times 0.0067 \approx 0.0066$. A neuron stuck out there is <Term def="Pushed so far into the flat part of an S-shaped activation that its slope is almost 0, so it hardly learns.">saturated</Term>: changing its input barely changes its output.

<ActivationSlopes />

Its cousin $\tanh(x)$, which squashes to the range $-1$ to $1$, has slope $1 - \tanh^2(x)$. That peaks at $1$ at $x = 0$: four times the sigmoid's best. Remember the number **0.25**: the next lesson shows how multiplying many such slopes together makes deep sigmoid networks stop learning.

## Corners: ReLU and $|x|$

Some functions have a sharp corner, a <Term def="A sharp corner in a graph. Zooming in never makes it look straight, so there is no single tangent there.">kink</Term>. Zoom in on a kink as far as you like and it never turns into a straight line, because the slopes on the two sides disagree. At a kink the derivative is **undefined**.

- **ReLU**, $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$: slope $0$ for $x < 0$ (flat), slope $1$ for $x > 0$, and a kink at $x = 0$. Switch the visual above to ReLU: its slope is a step from 0 to 1.
- **Absolute value** $|x|$: slope $-1$ on the left and $+1$ on the right, so its derivative is the <Term def="sign(x) is −1 for negative x, +1 for positive x, and 0 at x = 0.">sign</Term> of $x$, written $\text{sign}(x)$.

Training code still needs *some* number at exactly 0, so frameworks simply pick one. For $|x|$, any slope between $-1$ and $1$ gives a line through the corner that stays below the graph. Such a value is called a <Term def="At a kink, any slope of a line through the corner that stays on or below the graph everywhere. Optimisers can use it in place of the missing derivative.">subgradient</Term>, and $\text{sign}(0) = 0$ is the usual choice. PyTorch uses 0 for both ReLU and $|x|$ at 0:

```python
import torch

x = torch.tensor([-2.0, 0.0, 3.0], requires_grad=True)
torch.relu(x).sum().backward()
print(x.grad)

x = torch.tensor([-2.0, 0.0, 3.0], requires_grad=True)
torch.abs(x).sum().backward()
print(x.grad)
```

```text
tensor([0., 0., 1.])
tensor([-1.,  0.,  1.])
```

This appeared in the 2026 Final. Gradient boosting (a method you will meet later) trains each new tree on the **negative gradient** of the loss with respect to the current prediction $F$, and a question asked what changes when the loss becomes the absolute error $|y - F|$. Its slope with respect to $F$ is $-\text{sign}(y - F)$, so the negative gradient is $\text{sign}(y - F)$: just $+1$ or $-1$ for each sample, and $0$ when the prediction is exact.

## Check any rule with code

You can always test a rule by zooming in numerically: measure rise over a tiny run on each side of $x$, $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$, and compare with the formula. Run this and change the functions:

<PyRunner packages={['numpy']} code={`import numpy as np

def slope(f, x, h=1e-5):
    """Estimate f'(x) by zooming in: rise over a tiny run either side of x."""
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

for x in [0.0, 2.0, 5.0]:
    s = sigmoid(x)
    print(f"x = {x}:  measured slope {slope(sigmoid, x):.5f},  formula s(1 - s) = {s * (1 - s):.5f}")

print("e^x at x = 1:", round(slope(np.exp, 1.0), 5), "vs", round(np.exp(1.0), 5))
print("ln x at x = 4:", round(slope(np.log, 4.0), 5), "vs 1/4 =", 1 / 4)
print("x^3 at x = 2:", round(slope(lambda x: x**3, 2.0), 5), "vs 3 * 2^2 =", 3 * 2**2)
print("sign:", np.sign(np.array([-3.0, 0.0, 2.5])))`} />

## The cheat sheet

| Function | Derivative | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| $c$ (constant) | $0$ | flat line |
| $x^n$ | $n x^{n-1}$ | power down, power minus one |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | slope = height |
| $e^{-x}$ | $-e^{-x}$ | mirror flips the slope |
| $\ln x$ | $\frac{1}{x}$ | reflection of $e^x$ |
| $uv$ | $u'v + uv'$ | two strips |
| $\frac{u}{v}$ | $\frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ | bottom-dee-top minus top-dee-bottom |
| $\sigma(x)$ | $\sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$ | at most $0.25$, at $x = 0$ |
| $\tanh(x)$ | $1 - \tanh^2(x)$ | at most $1$, at $x = 0$ |
| $\text{ReLU}(x)$ | $0$ if $x < 0$, $1$ if $x > 0$ | undefined at 0 (code uses 0) |
| $\lvert x \rvert$ | $\text{sign}(x)$ | subgradient $0$ at the corner |

<ExamTip>

Typical NOAI calculus items take seconds if you know the table:

- "Derivative of $e^x$?" $e^x$. "Of $\ln x$?" $\frac{1}{x}$. "Second derivative of $x^3$?" $6x$, not $3x^2$.
- "Derivative of the sigmoid?" $\sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$, never just $\sigma(x)$. Its maximum is $0.25$ at $x = 0$.
- "Why is ReLU's derivative a problem at 0?" It is undefined there (a kink); in practice a value of 0 is used.

Traps to expect: answers that apply the power rule to $e^x$, that keep a constant term, or that stop after the first derivative when the question asked for the second.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Power rule: $x^n \to n x^{n-1}$. Constants vanish, multiples stay, sums split. Differentiate twice for $f''$, which measures bending.
- $e^x$ is its own derivative (slope = height); $e^{-x} \to -e^{-x}$; $\ln x \to \frac{1}{x}$.
- Product rule $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$; quotient rule $(u/v)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$.
- $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x)) \le 0.25$, and it is nearly 0 when the sigmoid saturates.
- ReLU has slope 0 or 1 with a kink at 0; $|x|$ has slope $\text{sign}(x)$; code picks 0 at the kink.

</Takeaways>
