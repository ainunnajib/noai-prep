    """a and b are boxes (x1, y1, x2, y2) with x1 < x2 and y1 < y2"""
    w = max(0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0]))   # overlap width, 0 if they miss
    h = max(0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))   # overlap height
    overlap = w * h
    area_a = (a[2] - a[0]) * (a[3] - a[1])
    area_b = (b[2] - b[0]) * (b[3] - b[1])
    return overlap / (area_a + area_b - overlap)

print(box_iou((0, 0, 4, 4), (2, 0, 6, 4)))   # half of each box overlaps: 8 / 24
print(box_iou((0, 0, 4, 4), (2, 2, 6, 6)))   # a quarter of each box: 4 / 28
print(box_iou((2, 2, 6, 6), (0, 0, 8, 8)))   # inside a bigger box: 16 / 64
print(box_iou((0, 0, 2, 2), (5, 5, 7, 7)))   # far apart: 0

Precision, recall and F1 count right and wrong labels. But many models do not output a label. A ride-hailing app predicts a **number** (how many minutes until you arrive), a self-driving car draws a **box** around each pedestrian, and many classifiers choose among **many classes**, some of them rare. Each needs its own ruler: MAE, MSE, RMSE and R² for numbers, IoU for boxes, and macro or micro averaging for many classes.

## How far off? MAE, MSE and RMSE

You met MSE and its square root, RMSE, in [Linear regression and least squares](#/learn/linear-regression/least-squares) and MAE in [L1 and L2 regularisation](#/learn/linear-regression/regularisation), where they scored a line during training. Here they become evaluation metrics: numbers you report about a finished model.

When a model predicts a number, each prediction has an error. As in least squares, the <Term def="The error of one prediction: true value minus predicted value, y − ŷ. Positive means the prediction was too low.">residual</Term> is

$$
r_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i
$$

where $y_i$ is the true value for example $i$ and $\hat{y}_i$ ("y hat") is the model's prediction. ([Regression by gradient descent](#/learn/linear-regression/regression-by-gradient-descent) wrote the error the other way round, $e_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i$; for MAE, MSE and RMSE the sign makes no difference.) A ride that took 23 minutes but was predicted at 20 has residual $+3$. To summarise $n$ residuals in one number we cannot just average them, because $+3$ and $-3$ would cancel to a "perfect" 0.

<Analogy title="Two Grab drivers">
Two drivers quote arrival times for the same four trips. Driver A is always 3 minutes off. Driver B is spot on twice, 1 minute off once, and 11 minutes late once. Both are 3 minutes off on average, but B's one big miss is the one that makes you late for an exam. A good error measure should be able to tell them apart.
</Analogy>

There are two standard fixes for the cancelling signs: drop the signs, or square the errors. Drag the predictions below, then press **Add one big miss**.

<ErrorMetricsLab />

In symbols:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{MAE} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert y_i - \hat{y}_i \rvert \\
\text{MSE} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \\
\text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\text{MSE}}
\end{aligned}
$$

- <Term def="Mean absolute error: the average size of the errors, ignoring their signs. Same units as the target.">MAE</Term> (mean absolute error) drops the signs and averages: the typical size of an error, in minutes. Both drivers score 3.
- <Term def="Mean squared error: the average of the squared errors. Its units are the target's units squared.">MSE</Term> (mean squared error) squares each error first. Squares are never negative, and big errors become *very* big: an error of 10 counts as 100.
- <Term def="Root mean squared error: the square root of the MSE, which brings it back to the target's units.">RMSE</Term> (root mean squared error) takes the square root of the MSE to get back to minutes. Driver B's 11-minute miss gives B the bigger RMSE.

<Steps title="The four rides in the figure: true 11, 17, 23, 29 min; predicted 10, 18, 20, 34 min">
  <Step title="Residuals">
    $11 - 10 = 1$, $\ 17 - 18 = -1$, $\ 23 - 20 = 3$, $\ 29 - 34 = -5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="MAE">
    Drop the signs and average: $\frac{1 + 1 + 3 + 5}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5$ minutes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="MSE">
    Square, then average: $\frac{1 + 1 + 9 + 25}{4} = \frac{36}{4} = 9$ square minutes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="RMSE">
    $\sqrt{9} = 3$ minutes. It is bigger than the MAE because the 5-minute miss counts 25 times as much as a 1-minute one.
  </Step>
</Steps>

What each one is good for:

- **RMSE ≥ MAE, always.** They are equal only when every error has the same size; the further apart they are, the more the errors are dominated by a few big misses.
- Use **RMSE** when big misses are disproportionately bad (a delivery 60 minutes late is far worse than six deliveries 10 minutes late). Use **MAE** when you want a plain "typical error" that a few outliers cannot dominate.
- **MSE** is in squared units (minutes², dollars²), which nobody can picture, so it is rarely reported. It is the standard *training loss* for regression because it is smooth and easy to differentiate, as you saw in [Linear regression and least squares](#/learn/linear-regression/least-squares).

<Mistake>
Three classic traps in multiple-choice options: averaging the residuals with their signs (here $\frac{1 - 1 + 3 - 5}{4} = -0.5$, which hides the errors); forgetting the square root (reporting the MSE, 9, as if it were in minutes); and taking the square root of each squared error before averaging (that just gives the MAE again).
</Mistake>

## R²: better than guessing the average?

Is an RMSE of 3 minutes good? It depends. If every ride takes between 19 and 21 minutes, a 3-minute error is poor; if rides range from 5 to 60 minutes, it is excellent. <Term def="The coefficient of determination: 1 minus (the model's sum of squared errors divided by the sum of squared errors of always predicting the mean). 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the mean, negative is worse.">R²</Term> (said "R squared") answers by comparing your model with the simplest sensible baseline: always predicting the **mean** of the true values.

<Analogy title="The friend who always says 'average'">
Before each test, you predict every classmate's mark. Your friend doesn't bother: they predict the class average for everyone. R² measures how much better your predictions are than your friend's. If you can't beat "everyone gets the average", your predictions aren't worth much, however clever your method.
</Analogy>

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
SS_{res} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \\
SS_{tot} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2
\end{aligned}
$$

$SS_{res}$ ("sum of squares of the residuals") is the total squared error of **your model**. $SS_{tot}$ ("total sum of squares") is the total squared error of the **mean baseline**, where $\bar{y}$ is the average of the true values. Their ratio is the fraction of the baseline's squared error that your model still makes; one minus that is the fraction it removes.

<RSquaredBars />

- $R^2 = 1$: no error left at all.
- $R^2 = 0$: exactly as good as always predicting the mean.
- $R^2 < 0$: **worse** than the mean. This happens with a bad model, and quite often on test data. Despite its name, R² is not literally a square (it equals a squared correlation only for a least-squares line with an intercept, scored on its own training data), so it can be negative.

<KeyIdea>
$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$ is the fraction of the "always predict the mean" squared error that your model removes. RMSE tells you the typical error **in the target's units**; R² tells you how much better than the dumbest sensible baseline you are, **without units**.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="R² for the four rides">
  <Step title="Mean of the true values">
    $\bar{y} = \frac{11 + 17 + 23 + 29}{4} = \frac{80}{4} = 20$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Baseline error, SS_tot">
    Distances from 20: $-9, -3, 3, 9$. Squares: $81 + 9 + 9 + 81 = 180$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Model error, SS_res">
    From the residuals above: $1 + 1 + 9 + 25 = 36$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="R²">
    $1 - \frac{36}{180} = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8$. The model removes 80% of the squared error that "always say 20 minutes" would make.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Because both sums cover the same $n$ rows, you can also divide each by $n$ first and compare the model's MSE with the MSE of always predicting the mean (here $180/4 = 45$). This is often quicker in an exam:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{MSE}_\text{model}}{\text{MSE}_\text{mean}} = 1 - \frac{9}{45} = 0.8
$$

## IoU: how well do two boxes overlap?

An object detector outputs <Term def="An axis-aligned rectangle given by its corners (x1, y1) and (x2, y2), drawn around an object in an image.">bounding boxes</Term>, each written as two corners $(x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)$. To score a predicted box against the true one we need a single number that is 1 when they match exactly and 0 when they don't touch.

<Analogy title="Two highlighters">
You and a friend each highlight the sentence you think answers a comprehension question. Count the words you **both** highlighted, then divide by the words that **either** of you highlighted. If you highlighted the same words, that's 1. If your friend highlighted the whole page "to be safe", the score drops, even though the right words are inside it.
</Analogy>

<Term def="Intersection over union: the size of the overlap of two shapes or sets divided by the size of their union. Also called the Jaccard index.">Intersection over union</Term> (IoU) is exactly that:

$$
\text{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{\lvert A \cap B \rvert}{\lvert A \cup B \rvert} = \frac{\text{overlap}}{\text{union}}
$$

The bars $\lvert \cdot \rvert$ mean "size of" (an area for boxes, a count for sets). $A \cap B$, the **intersection**, is where both boxes are; $A \cup B$, the **union**, is everywhere at least one box is. For sets the same ratio is called the <Term def="The size of the intersection of two sets divided by the size of their union; the same idea as IoU.">Jaccard index</Term>: the CCA sets $\{$chess, robotics, choir$\}$ and $\{$robotics, choir, band, drama$\}$ share 2 items out of 5 different ones, so their Jaccard index is $2/5 = 0.4$.

For two boxes, the overlap is itself a rectangle, so IoU takes five steps:

1. **Overlap width** $= \min(x_2^A, x_2^B) - \max(x_1^A, x_1^B)$: the left edge of the overlap is the further-right of the two left edges, and its right edge is the further-left of the two right edges. If the result is negative, the boxes miss each other: use 0.
2. **Overlap height** $= \min(y_2^A, y_2^B) - \max(y_1^A, y_1^B)$, again 0 if negative.
3. **Overlap area** = width × height.
4. **Union** = area A + area B − overlap. (Adding the two areas counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once.)
5. **IoU** = overlap ÷ union.

Move and resize the boxes, and check each step:

<IoUBoxes />

<Steps title="IoU of A = (0, 0, 4, 4) and B = (2, 0, 6, 4)">
  <Step title="Overlap width">
    $\min(4, 6) - \max(0, 2) = 4 - 2 = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Overlap height">
    $\min(4, 4) - \max(0, 0) = 4 - 0 = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Overlap area">
    $2 \times 4 = 8$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Union">
    Both boxes are $4 \times 4 = 16$, so the union is $16 + 16 - 8 = 24$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="IoU">
    $\frac{8}{24} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.33$. Each box shares **half** of its area with the other, yet IoU is only a third.
  </Step>
</Steps>

IoU punishes **both** kinds of mistake: missing part of the object *and* covering extra space. A predicted box $(0, 0, 8, 8)$ around a true box $(2, 2, 6, 6)$ contains the whole object, but its IoU is only $\frac{16}{64} = 0.25$.

Detectors use box IoU in two places. To **score** a detection, a predicted box usually counts as correct only if its IoU with a true box of the same class is at least a threshold such as 0.5. To **clean up** duplicate boxes on the same object, non-maximum suppression deletes boxes that overlap a better one by more than a threshold (you will run it by hand in [Vision tasks and object detection](#/learn/vision-tasks/vision-tasks-and-detection)).

<Mistake>
Watch for these wrong answers: dividing the overlap by **one** box's area ($8/16 = 0.5$ above); forgetting to subtract the overlap from the union ($8/32 = 0.25$); and, in code, forgetting the `max(0, …)`. For boxes $(0, 0, 2, 2)$ and $(5, 5, 7, 7)$ the "width" is $2 - 5 = -3$ and the "height" is $-3$, and their product is a positive 9: an overlap between boxes that are nowhere near each other.
</Mistake>

## Macro versus micro averaging

With more than two classes, precision, recall and F1 are computed **per class**, each time treating one class as "positive" and all the others as "negative". You then have three F1 scores and need one number. How you average them decides whether rare classes matter.

<Analogy title="CCA survey">
To find out how happy students are with CCAs, you could average each CCA's satisfaction score, so the 8-member chess club counts as much as the 200-member band. Or you could pool every student's answer, so the band dominates. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
</Analogy>

- <Term def="Compute the metric for each class separately, then take the plain average, so every class counts equally however rare it is.">Macro averaging</Term>: compute F1 for each class, then take the plain average. With $C$ classes, $\text{macro-}F_1 = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c} F_1^{(c)}$. Every class counts equally, however rare.
- <Term def="Pool the TP, FP and FN counts of all classes first, then compute the metric once, so every individual prediction counts equally and common classes dominate.">Micro averaging</Term>: add up the true positives, false positives and false negatives of all classes first, then compute one F1 from the totals: $\text{micro-}F_1 = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN}$. Every *prediction* counts equally, so the common classes dominate.
- **Weighted averaging** sits in between: the per-class F1 scores are averaged with weights equal to each class's share of the data.

<MacroMicroAverage />

<Steps title="20 vehicles: 10 cars, 6 buses, 4 motorcycles">
  <Step title="What the model predicted">
    Cars: 9 right, 1 called a bus. Buses: all 6 right. Motorcycles: only 1 right; 1 called a car and 2 called buses.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count TP, FP, FN for each class">
    Car: TP 9, FP 1 (the motorcycle called a car), FN 1. Bus: TP 6, FP 3 (one car and two motorcycles called buses), FN 0. Motorcycle: TP 1, FP 0, FN 3.
  </Step>
  <Step title="F1 for each class">
    Using $F_1 = \frac{2TP}{2TP + FP + FN}$: car $\frac{18}{20} = 0.9$, bus $\frac{12}{15} = 0.8$, motorcycle $\frac{2}{5} = 0.4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Macro-F1">
    $\frac{0.9 + 0.8 + 0.4}{3} = \frac{2.1}{3} = 0.7$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Micro-F1">
    Pool the counts: $TP = 9 + 6 + 1 = 16$, $FP = 1 + 3 + 0 = 4$, $FN = 1 + 0 + 3 = 4$. So $\frac{32}{32 + 4 + 4} = 0.8$, the same as the accuracy $\frac{16}{20}$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Micro-F1 matching accuracy is no coincidence. When each example has exactly one true class and one predicted class, every mistake is one false positive (for the class it was wrongly given) **and** one false negative (for the class it really was). So total FP = total FN = number of mistakes, and micro precision, micro recall and micro-F1 all equal **accuracy**. (Weighted F1 here is $\frac{10(0.9) + 6(0.8) + 4(0.4)}{20} = 0.77$.)

Which one to report? If every class matters equally, especially a rare but important one (a rare disease, a fraud type, motorcycles for a road-safety camera), report **macro**: a weak rare class drags it down where micro would hide it. If what matters is the overall share of correct predictions, micro (which is just accuracy here) is fine. scikit-learn's `classification_report` prints all three.

<PyRunner packages={['scikit-learn']} code={avgCode} />

**Mean IoU is a macro average.** To score a segmentation model, which labels every pixel, you compute one IoU per class (that class's predicted pixels against its true pixels) and average the class IoUs equally. A rare class that the model handles badly therefore pulls mean IoU down. One subtlety: if a class appears in neither the prediction nor the truth of an image, its IoU is $\frac{0}{0}$, undefined, so that class is usually left out of the average rather than counted as 0 or 1.

<ExamTip>
Phrases like "rare classes", "every class equally important" or "imbalanced multi-class" point to **macro** averaging. "Micro-F1 equals accuracy" (for single-label multi-class problems) is a fact worth knowing cold. And "mean IoU" in segmentation is a macro average over classes.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>
- Residual $= y - \hat{y}$. **MAE** averages the sizes of the errors; **MSE** averages their squares (squared units); **RMSE** $= \sqrt{\text{MSE}}$ is back in the target's units and punishes big misses more. RMSE ≥ MAE.
- $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$ compares the model with always predicting the mean: 1 perfect, 0 no better than the mean, negative worse.
- **IoU** = overlap ÷ union, with union = A + B − overlap; for boxes, overlap width $= \min(x_2) - \max(x_1)$, clipped at 0. IoU punishes both missed and extra area.
- **Macro** averages per-class scores equally (rare classes count); **micro** pools the counts first (common classes dominate) and equals accuracy for single-label data. Mean IoU is a macro average.
</Takeaways>
