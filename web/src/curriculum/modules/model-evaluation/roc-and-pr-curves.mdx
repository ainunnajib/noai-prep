Precision, recall and the confusion matrix all depend on one choice: the threshold. Move it and every number changes. So how do you compare two models before anyone has picked a threshold? Draw **every threshold at once**. That picture is the ROC curve, the area under it is the single most quoted number in classification, and its cousin, the precision-recall curve, tells the truth when positives are rare.

<Analogy title="Back to the Changi scanner">

Remember the airport scanner's sensitivity knob from [Confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1](#/learn/model-evaluation/confusion-matrix-and-metrics)? Start with it at zero: it beeps at nothing. Turn it up one notch at a time, and at each notch write down two numbers: the fraction of knives it now beeps at, and the fraction of belt buckles. Plot the pairs and you have traced the scanner's whole personality, from "silent" to "beeps at everything". A good scanner catches most knives while still ignoring most buckles.

</Analogy>

## Two rates, one for each class

The ROC curve uses two rates. Both divide by an **actual** class, that is, by one row of the confusion matrix:

$$
\text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
$$

$$
\text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}
$$

- The <Term def="TP / (TP + FN): the fraction of actual positives that the model flags. The same thing as recall and sensitivity.">true positive rate</Term> (**TPR**) is the fraction of real positives that get flagged. It is exactly **recall**: the knives caught.
- The <Term def="FP / (FP + TN): the fraction of actual negatives that the model wrongly flags. It equals 1 − specificity.">false positive rate</Term> (**FPR**) is the fraction of real negatives that get flagged by mistake: the belt buckles beeped at. It equals $1 - \text{specificity}$.

Because each rate looks inside **one** class only, neither cares how many examples the other class has. Remember this: it is the key to the last part of the lesson.

## Tracing the ROC curve

The <Term def="Receiver operating characteristic curve: a plot of the true positive rate (y) against the false positive rate (x) for every possible threshold.">ROC curve</Term> (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar operators in the Second World War) plots TPR on the vertical axis against FPR on the horizontal axis, one point per threshold.

Take ten examples, five positives (P) and five negatives (N), sorted by score: 0.9 (P), 0.8 (P), 0.7 (N), 0.6 (P), 0.55 (P), 0.5 (N), 0.4 (N), 0.3 (P), 0.2 (N), 0.1 (N). Now lower the threshold one example at a time:

| flag scores ≥ | newly flagged | TP | FP | point (FPR, TPR) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| above 0.9 | none | 0 | 0 | (0, 0) |
| 0.9 | P | 1 | 0 | (0, 0.2) |
| 0.8 | P | 2 | 0 | (0, 0.4) |
| 0.7 | N | 2 | 1 | (0.2, 0.4) |
| 0.6 | P | 3 | 1 | (0.2, 0.6) |
| 0.55 | P | 4 | 1 | (0.2, 0.8) |
| 0.5 | N | 4 | 2 | (0.4, 0.8) |
| 0.4 | N | 4 | 3 | (0.6, 0.8) |
| 0.3 | P | 5 | 3 | (0.6, 1.0) |
| 0.2 | N | 5 | 4 | (0.8, 1.0) |
| 0.1 | N | 5 | 5 | (1.0, 1.0) |

The rule is simple: every positive you pass steps the point **up** by $\frac{1}{5}$ (one more of the five positives caught), and every negative steps it **right** by $\frac{1}{5}$ (one more false alarm). The result is a staircase from (0, 0) to (1, 1). With thousands of examples the steps become tiny and the staircase looks like a smooth curve.

In the figure below, the score panel shows 40 positives (orange, stacked down) and 40 negatives (blue, stacked up); everything right of the threshold is predicted positive, and a red ring marks a mistake. The ROC panel plots that threshold as one point, (FPR, TPR), on the curve.

<ROCTracer />

Four landmarks to know:

- **(0, 0)**: a threshold above every score. Nothing is flagged.
- **(1, 1)**: a threshold below every score. Everything is flagged.
- **(0, 1)**, the top-left corner: every positive caught, no false alarms. A perfect model's curve goes straight up to it.
- **The diagonal** from (0, 0) to (1, 1): random guessing. A model that flags a random 30% of everything catches 30% of the positives *and* 30% of the negatives, so TPR = FPR.

## AUC: one number for the whole curve

The <Term def="Area under the ROC curve, between 0 and 1. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 = random, 1 = perfect.">AUC</Term> (area under the ROC curve) squeezes the whole curve into one number between 0 and 1. The perfect curve fills the whole unit square (AUC = 1); the diagonal covers half of it (AUC = 0.5).

AUC also has a beautiful meaning that needs no calculus:

<KeyIdea>

**AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative.** It measures how well the model *ranks* positives above negatives: 0.5 is a coin toss, 1.0 is a perfect ranking. It does not depend on any threshold.

</KeyIdea>

Why are the two the same? With 5 positives and 5 negatives, the ROC square is cut into $5 \times 5 = 25$ little cells, one for each (positive, negative) pair, and a cell lies under the staircase exactly when that positive outscores that negative. Count the cells and you have the area.

<Steps title="AUC of the ten examples by counting pairs">
  <Step title="List the pairs">
    Each of the 5 positives can be paired with each of the 5 negatives: $5 \times 5 = 25$ pairs.
  </Step>
  <Step title="For each positive, count the negatives below it">
    0.9 beats all 5 negatives. 0.8 beats all 5. 0.6 beats 4 (all except 0.7). 0.55 beats 4. 0.3 beats 2 (0.2 and 0.1).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add up and divide">
    $5 + 5 + 4 + 4 + 2 = 20$ correct pairs, so $\text{AUC} = \frac{20}{25} = 0.8$. (Ties, if any, count as half a pair.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check with the area">
    The staircase has five columns, each $0.2$ wide, with heights 0.4, 0.8, 0.8, 1.0 and 1.0. Area $= 0.2 \times (0.4 + 0.8 + 0.8 + 1.0 + 1.0) = 0.2 \times 4 = 0.8$. Same answer.
  </Step>
</Steps>

In the next figure the same 25 pairs are laid out as the ROC square: the columns are the negatives (highest score on the left) and the rows are the positives (highest score at the bottom). The staircase separates the correctly ranked pairs (✓) from the wrong ones (✗).

<AUCPairs />

Because AUC only looks at the **order** of the scores:

- It ignores the threshold entirely, so it compares models before you choose one.
- Rescaling the scores without changing their order (doubling them, or squaring or taking logs of positive scores such as probabilities) leaves AUC unchanged.
- An AUC **below** 0.5 means the model ranks the classes the wrong way round more often than not. Reverse its ranking (use $1 - \text{score}$ instead of the score) and you get $1 - \text{AUC}$.

## Precision-recall curves for rare positives

ROC curves have a blind spot. FPR divides by **all the negatives**, and when positives are rare there are a huge number of negatives, so a false positive rate that *looks* tiny can still mean an enormous number of false alarms.

<Analogy title="One per cent sounds small">

A bank screens 1,000,000 card payments, of which 100 are fraud. Its model has a false positive rate of just 1%. That 1% is 1% of the 999,900 genuine payments: about 10,000 false alarms, compared with at most 100 real frauds. Even if every fraud is caught, fewer than 1 alert in 100 is real. The ROC point (FPR 0.01, TPR 1.0) looks almost perfect; the investigators drowning in alerts would disagree.

</Analogy>

The fix is to plot the metric that feels the false alarms directly. The <Term def="A plot of precision (y) against recall (x) for every possible threshold. Its baseline is the share of positives.">precision-recall (PR) curve</Term> plots precision on the vertical axis against recall on the horizontal axis, one point per threshold. If a share $\pi$ of the examples are positive (the Greek letter pi, here just a fraction, not 3.14), then out of $N$ examples there are $TP = \pi N \cdot \text{TPR}$ true positives and $FP = (1 - \pi) N \cdot \text{FPR}$ false positives, so

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\pi \cdot \text{TPR}}{\pi \cdot \text{TPR} + (1 - \pi) \cdot \text{FPR}}
$$

The ROC curve stays the same as $\pi$ changes, because TPR and FPR are computed inside each class. Precision mixes the two classes, so when $\pi$ is tiny the $(1 - \pi) \cdot \text{FPR}$ term swamps the top and precision collapses. The PR curve shows it at once.

<ImbalanceROCvsPR />

The area under the PR curve is summarised as <Term def="A summary of the precision-recall curve, often called PR-AUC: the average of the precision values reached each time another true positive is found, as the threshold is lowered.">average precision</Term> (AP, often called **PR-AUC**). Its baseline is not 0.5 but the share of positives $\pi$: a random model's precision is just the fraction of positives in the data. For the ten examples above, walk down the sorted list and note the precision each time you reach a positive: $\frac{1}{1}, \frac{2}{2}, \frac{3}{4}, \frac{4}{5}, \frac{5}{8}$. Their average is $\frac{1 + 1 + 0.75 + 0.8 + 0.625}{5} = 0.835$.

| use | when |
|---|---|
| ROC curve and ROC-AUC | classes are roughly balanced, or you care about ranking both classes well |
| PR curve and PR-AUC | positives are rare and what matters is how trustworthy the positive predictions are (fraud, rare disease, search results) |

<Mistake title="A high ROC-AUC is not a promise of good alarms">

An ROC-AUC of 0.96 says the model ranks well. It does not say that its alarms are mostly real. With 0.5% positives, the same model can have precision below 0.1 at a useful recall. For rare events, always look at precision and recall (or the PR curve) at the threshold you will actually use.

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

Memorise the axes: **ROC = TPR (y) against FPR (x)**; **PR = precision (y) against recall (x)**. "Precision vs recall" is the classic distractor when the question asks about ROC. Diagonal = random = AUC 0.5; top-left corner = perfect. AUC = chance a random positive outscores a random negative. For heavy class imbalance, prefer the PR curve or PR-AUC, whose random baseline is the positive rate, not 0.5.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- **TPR** $= \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$ (recall) and **FPR** $= \frac{FP}{FP + TN}$; each is computed inside one class.
- The **ROC curve** plots TPR against FPR for every threshold, from (0, 0) to (1, 1); each positive steps it up, each negative steps it right. The diagonal is random guessing.
- **AUC** = area under the ROC curve = probability a random positive outscores a random negative: 0.5 random, 1 perfect, threshold-free.
- When positives are rare, a small FPR can still mean many false alarms: use the **precision-recall curve** and **PR-AUC** (average precision), whose baseline is the positive rate.

</Takeaways>
