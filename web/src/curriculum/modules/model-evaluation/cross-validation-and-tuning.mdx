A single train/validation split gives you one score, and that score depends on luck: which rows happened to land in the validation set. Then, as soon as you start tuning, every glance at a score is a chance to fool yourself. This lesson is about getting numbers you can trust: **cross-validation** to steady the score, **grid and random search** to tune settings, and the two classic leaks that make reported scores look better than the model really is.

## Three piles of data, one more time

You met these in the data module. A <Term def="A setting you choose before training, such as k in k-nearest neighbours or the maximum depth of a tree. The model does not learn it from the data; you pick it by trying values and comparing scores.">hyperparameter</Term> is a setting *you* choose, like the depth of a tree or the $k$ in $k$-nearest neighbours. The model's **parameters** (weights, split points) are learned during training; hyperparameters are not.

- **Training set**: the model learns its parameters here.
- <Term def="Data held back from training and used to compare models or hyperparameter settings. Because you make choices based on it, its score becomes slightly optimistic.">Validation set</Term>: you compare hyperparameter settings here and keep the best.
- **Test set**: the final exam. You use it **once**, at the very end, to report how well the chosen model will do on new data.

The rest of the lesson is about doing the middle step well, and about keeping the third step honest.

## One split, one noisy number

Suppose you have 40 students and you hold out 8 of them to validate a model that predicts pass or fail. Each held-out student is worth $\frac{1}{8} = 12.5$ percentage points of the score. If one tricky student happens to land in the validation set instead of the training set, the score drops by 12.5 points, although the model is exactly as good as before. With a small dataset, **which** rows you held out can matter as much as the model itself.

<Analogy title="Everyone takes a turn as the examiner">
A class of 30 wants to rate its own revision notes. If only one group of 6 ever marks the quiz, the result depends on how strict those 6 are. So the class splits into 5 groups and rotates: each group takes one turn as the examiners while the other 24 study, and the final rating averages all five rounds. Everyone gets tested, and everyone's marks count.
</Analogy>

## k-fold cross-validation

<Term def="A way to estimate how well a model will do on new data: split the data into k folds, train k times (each time leaving out a different fold), score each model on the fold it did not see, and average the k scores.">Cross-validation</Term> (CV) turns that rotation into an algorithm. In **k-fold** CV:

1. Shuffle the rows and cut them into $k$ equal parts called <Term def="One of the k equal parts the data is cut into for cross-validation.">folds</Term>.
2. **Round 1**: fold 1 is the validation set. Train a fresh model on the other $k - 1$ folds and score it on fold 1.
3. **Rounds 2 to k**: do the same with fold 2, fold 3, … as the validation fold, so every fold has exactly one turn.
4. Report the **average** of the $k$ scores, and their **spread**.

Press Play and watch the orange validation fold move along the rows. Then change $k$, and switch stratification off and on.

<KFoldAnimator />

Every student is validated exactly once and used for training $k - 1$ times. The dot plot is the point of the whole exercise: a single split would have reported just *one* of those dots, and you would not know whether it was a lucky one or an unlucky one. Five dots show you both the typical score and how much it wobbles.

To summarise the $k$ scores $s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k$, report their mean and their <Term def="A measure of spread: the square root of the average squared distance from the mean. Small means the values sit close together.">standard deviation</Term> (SD):

$$
\bar{s} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i
\qquad\qquad
\text{SD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \left(s_i - \bar{s}\right)^2}
$$

Here $s_i$ is the score on fold $i$, the symbol $\sum_{i=1}^{k}$ means "add up for $i = 1$ to $k$", and $\bar{s}$ ("s bar") is the mean. In words: the mean is the typical score; the SD takes each score's distance from the mean, squares it, averages the squares and takes the square root, giving a typical distance from the mean. You write the result as $\bar{s} \pm \text{SD}$.

<KeyIdea>
k-fold cross-validation trains $k$ models, each validated on a different fold, and reports **mean ± standard deviation** of the $k$ scores. It uses every row for both training and validation and turns one noisy number into an average with error bars, at the cost of $k$ times the training.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Five fold scores: 0.82, 0.78, 0.86, 0.74, 0.80">
  <Step title="Mean">
    $0.82 + 0.78 + 0.86 + 0.74 + 0.80 = 4.00$, and $4.00 / 5 = 0.80$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Distance of each score from the mean">
    Work in hundredths to avoid decimals: $+2,\ -2,\ +6,\ -6,\ 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square, then average">
    $4 + 4 + 36 + 36 + 0 = 80$, and $80 / 5 = 16$ (in hundredths squared).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square root">
    $\sqrt{16} = 4$ hundredths, so SD $= 0.04$. Report **0.80 ± 0.04**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
NumPy's `np.std` divides by $k$, as above. Statistics books often divide by $k - 1$ instead (`np.std(s, ddof=1)` gives $0.045$ here). For a handful of fold scores, either is fine as long as you are consistent; the idea, a typical distance from the mean, is the same.
</Note>

### Choosing k, and what happens afterwards

- **$k = 5$ or $k = 10$** are the usual choices. Each model trains on $\frac{k-1}{k}$ of the data: 80% for $k = 5$, 90% for $k = 10$.
- **Bigger $k$** means more training data per round, but $k$ fits instead of 5, and each validation fold is smaller, so each single score is noisier (try $k = 10$ in the visual).
- **$k = n$**, one row per fold, is called <Term def="Cross-validation where each fold is a single row: n models, each trained on all rows but one and tested on the one left out. Thorough but expensive.">leave-one-out</Term> CV. It is thorough but needs $n$ fits.
- CV **estimates** how good a recipe (model type plus hyperparameters) is. It does not hand you the final model. Once you have chosen, retrain one model on **all** the training data.

### Stratified folds

Random folds can get an uneven mix of classes. In the visual, 10 of the 40 students fail; with $k = 10$, plain random folds may give one fold three failures and another none, and a fold with no failures cannot tell you anything about catching them. <Term def="k-fold cross-validation where each fold keeps the same class proportions as the whole dataset.">Stratified k-fold</Term> deals each class out evenly, so every fold has the same share of each class as the full data. It matters most for small or imbalanced datasets. (scikit-learn's `cross_val_score` already uses stratified folds for classifiers when you pass `cv=5`.)

When rows are not independent, random folds are wrong altogether: for data ordered in time, validate on later rows than you train on; for several rows from the same patient, keep all of that patient's rows in one fold. Otherwise the folds leak, just as the splits did in the data module.

<ExamTip>
NOAI asks *when* to cross-validate. With a **small** dataset, a single validation set is tiny and noisy, so CV is worth the extra training. With a **huge** dataset, one validation set of, say, 100,000 rows is already stable, and training $k$ times may be too slow, so a single split is fine.
</ExamTip>

## Tuning: grid search and random search

<Term def="Choosing hyperparameter values by trying several settings and keeping the one with the best validation or cross-validation score.">Hyperparameter tuning</Term> is a loop: pick a setting, score it with cross-validation, repeat, keep the best. The question is which settings to try.

- <Term def="Tuning by trying every combination of a fixed list of values for each hyperparameter.">Grid search</Term> lists a few values for each hyperparameter and tries **every combination**. With 4 depths and 3 leaf sizes that is $4 \times 3 = 12$ settings; with 5-fold CV, $12 \times 5 = 60$ models. The count multiplies with every hyperparameter you add: 5 hyperparameters with 4 values each is $4^5 = 1024$ settings, or 5,120 models.
- <Term def="Tuning by drawing a fixed number of settings at random from ranges you give for each hyperparameter.">Random search</Term> fixes a budget (say 9 trials) and draws each trial's values **at random** from a range for each hyperparameter.

<Analogy title="Laksa tasting">
You can cook 9 pots of laksa to find the best recipe, varying chilli and coconut milk. A grid tries 3 chilli levels × 3 coconut levels, so each chilli level is cooked 3 times. If chilli is what really decides the taste and coconut hardly matters, you have learnt about only 3 chilli levels. Nine random recipes each use a *different* chilli level, so you learn about 9.
</Analogy>

<GridVsRandomSearch />

In the visual, with 9 trials and "only A matters", the grid's trials sit in 3 columns, so it learns the score at only 3 values of A; random search samples 9 different values of A and usually lands nearer the peak. In real problems it is common that only one or two hyperparameters matter much, and you rarely know in advance which ones. That is why random search is often the better default when there are several hyperparameters.

| | Grid search | Random search |
|---|---|---|
| Tries | every combination | a fixed number, at random |
| Cost | multiplies with each hyperparameter | you set the budget |
| Values of each hyperparameter | only those listed | up to one per trial |
| Best for | 1 or 2 hyperparameters | many, of which few matter |

<Tip>
For learning rates and regularisation strengths, what matters is the order of magnitude. Search them on a **log scale**, e.g. $0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1$, or draw random values of the form $10^u$ with $u$ between $-3$ and $0$.
</Tip>

<Mistake>
"Grid search is more thorough, so it is always better." Only if the grid is fine enough in the directions that matter, and a fine grid in many directions is unaffordable. "Random search is just luck" is wrong too: for the same budget it explores each hyperparameter more thoroughly, and you can keep adding trials for as long as you have time. Smarter searches (for example Bayesian optimisation, which picks the next trial using the scores so far) exist, but grid and random search are the two you must be able to compare.
</Mistake>

## Every fitted step goes inside the folds

Cross-validation is only honest if the validation fold plays no part in training. That includes **every step that learns something from data**, not just the model. Choosing features is the classic trap. If you pick "the 10 most useful columns" using all the rows and *then* cross-validate, the validation rows' labels have already helped to choose the columns. That is <Term def="When information from the validation or test data (or from the future) gets into training, so the reported score is better than the model's real performance.">data leakage</Term>.

<Analogy title="Revising after seeing the paper">
Choosing which topics to revise *after* seeing the exam paper, then saying your mock score shows how well you will do in the real exam. The mock score was real, but it no longer predicts anything.
</Analogy>

The data below are pure noise: random numbers with random labels. No model can genuinely do better than a coin flip, 0.5. Slide the number of columns.

<CVLeakageCompare />

Why does workflow A climb? With 1,000 random columns and only 40 rows, a few columns will match the labels well **by pure chance**, across all 40 rows, including the ones that later serve as validation rows. Selecting those columns bakes the validation labels into the model, so the "lucky" columns keep working on the validation folds. In workflow B, the columns are chosen on the 32 training rows only; their luck does not carry over to the 8 unseen rows, and the score stays at chance. More columns means more chances for luck, which is why A's inflation grows to the right.

The rule covers every fitted step: a scaler's mean and SD, an imputer's fill values, feature selection, PCA, and oversampling methods such as SMOTE (oversample *before* splitting and copies of the same row land in both training and validation folds). The safe way is a <Term def="A scikit-learn object that chains preprocessing steps and a model. Cross-validating the whole pipeline refits every step on each round's training folds only.">Pipeline</Term>: put the steps and the model in one object and cross-validate the whole thing, and scikit-learn refits every step inside each round.

```python
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SelectKBest(f_classif, k=10), LogisticRegression())
cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)     # scaler and selector refitted in every fold
```

## Peeking at the test set

Tuning has a subtler leak. Each time you check a score and then change something because of it, that dataset has influenced your choices. Do that with the **test** set and it quietly becomes a validation set. The 2026 Prelim compared two protocols:

- **Protocol A**: 70% train, 30% test. Train, check the test accuracy, adjust, retrain… 50 times; report the best test accuracy.
- **Protocol B**: 60% train, 20% validation, 20% test. Tune on the validation set; evaluate on the test set once.

<Analogy title="The same practice paper, fifty times">
Retaking the same practice paper 50 times: your score on that paper climbs, partly because you remember its questions. Your result in the real exam does not.
</Analogy>

<ProtocolCompare />

Every measured score is **true skill + luck**, because a test set is a finite sample. One measurement is as likely to be lucky as unlucky, so on average it is right. But "report the best of many" picks out the luckiest measurement. Suppose 5 models are all truly 90% accurate and each is scored on the same 100 test rows: a single score is 90% on average, but the best of the five averages about 93%. With 20 tries the best averages about 95%, with no model any better than before. This is <Term def="The upward bias you get by picking the best of many noisy scores: the winner is partly the luckiest, so its score overstates its true performance. Also called the winner's curse.">selection bias</Term>, and doing it with a test set is sometimes called *adaptive overfitting* to the test set: the model has not memorised the test rows, but *your choices* have.

<KeyIdea>
Tune on a validation set or with cross-validation. Touch the test set **once**, at the end. Any score you used to make a choice is no longer an unbiased estimate.
</KeyIdea>

### Nested cross-validation

The same bias affects tuning by cross-validation, more mildly: `GridSearchCV`'s `best_score_` is the best of many CV scores, so it is a little optimistic. When there is too little data to set aside a test set, <Term def="Cross-validation inside cross-validation: an outer k-fold loop estimates performance, and inside each outer training set an inner CV loop does the tuning. The outer folds never influence any choice.">nested cross-validation</Term> gives an honest estimate of the whole recipe, tuning included:

<Steps title="Nested CV with 5 outer folds">
  <Step title="Outer loop: hold out one fold">
    Set outer fold 1 aside. It plays the role of a test set for this round.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Inner loop: tune on the other 4 folds only">
    Run an ordinary grid or random search with CV (the "inner" CV) using just those 4 folds, and refit the winning setting on them.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Score once">
    Score that tuned model on the held-out outer fold. It played no part in any choice.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Rotate and average">
    Repeat for outer folds 2 to 5 and report the mean ± SD of the 5 outer scores. Cost: with 9 settings and 3 inner folds, $5 \times 9 \times 3 = 135$ fits, plus 5 refits.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Takeaways>
- **k-fold CV**: $k$ rounds, each fold validates once; report mean ± SD of the $k$ scores. Use **stratified** folds for classification, especially with rare classes.
- CV is worth it for **small** datasets; with huge datasets one validation split is already stable.
- **Grid search** tries every combination (cost multiplies); **random search** draws a fixed budget of settings and tries more distinct values of the hyperparameters that matter.
- Every step that **learns from data** (scaling, imputing, feature selection, oversampling) must be fitted inside each training fold: use a **Pipeline**.
- Picking the best of many scores inflates it. Tune on validation data or CV, use **nested CV** for an honest estimate, and touch the test set **once**.
</Takeaways>
