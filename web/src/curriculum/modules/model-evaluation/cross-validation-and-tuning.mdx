export const kfoldCode = `import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=100, n_features=4, flip_y=0.15, random_state=1)
model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)

# k-fold from scratch: shuffle the row numbers, cut them into k folds
k = 5
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
folds = np.array_split(rng.permutation(len(y)), k)
scores = []
for i in range(k):
    val = folds[i]                                   # this round's validation rows
    train = np.concatenate(folds[:i] + folds[i+1:])  # all the other folds
    model.fit(X[train], y[train])
    scores.append(model.score(X[val], y[val]))
    print(f"round {i+1}: train on {len(train)}, validate on {len(val)}, accuracy {scores[-1]:.2f}")
scores = np.array(scores)
print(f"from scratch: {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")

# the same idea in one line (stratified folds keep the class mix in every fold)
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=cv)
print("cross_val_score:", s.round(2), f"-> {s.mean():.3f} ± {s.std():.3f}")`

A single train/validation split gives you one score, and that score depends on luck: which rows happened to land in the validation set. Once you start tuning, every glance at a score is a chance to fool yourself. This lesson is about numbers you can trust: **cross-validation**, **grid and random search**, and the two classic leaks that make a model look better than it is.

<Analogy title="Everyone takes a turn as the examiner">
A class of 30 wants to rate its own revision notes. If only one group of 6 ever marks the quiz, the result depends on how strict those 6 are. So the class splits into 5 groups and rotates: each group takes one turn as the examiners while the other 24 study, and the final rating averages all five rounds. Everyone gets tested, and everyone's marks count.
</Analogy>

## Why one split is not enough

Recall the three piles from [Generalisation and overfitting](#/learn/ml-fundamentals/generalisation-and-overfitting): the **training set** fits the model's parameters, the <Term def="Data held back from training and used to compare models or hyperparameter settings. Because you make choices based on it, its score becomes slightly optimistic.">validation set</Term> compares settings, and the **test set** is used **once**, at the end. The settings you compare are <Term def="A setting you choose before training, such as k in k-nearest neighbours or the maximum depth of a tree. The model does not learn it from the data; you pick it by trying values and comparing scores.">hyperparameters</Term>, such as the depth of a tree or the $k$ in $k$-nearest neighbours: you choose them, the model does not learn them.

With little data, the validation score is noisy. Hold out 8 of 40 students and each is worth $\frac{1}{8} = 12.5$ percentage points: one tricky student landing in the validation set costs 12.5 points, although the model is just as good.

## k-fold cross-validation

<Term def="A way to estimate how well a model will do on new data: split the data into k folds, train k times (each time leaving out a different fold), score each model on the fold it did not see, and average the k scores.">Cross-validation</Term> (CV) turns the analogy's rotation into an algorithm. In **k-fold** CV:

1. Shuffle the rows and cut them into $k$ equal parts called <Term def="One of the k equal parts the data is cut into for cross-validation.">folds</Term>.
2. Train a fresh model on $k - 1$ folds and score it on the fold that was left out.
3. Repeat until every fold has been the validation fold exactly once: $k$ rounds in all.
4. Report the **average** of the $k$ scores, and their **spread**.

<KFoldAnimator />

A single split would report just *one* of the dots in the dot plot, lucky or not; five dots show both the typical score and how much it wobbles. Summarise the $k$ scores by their mean and <Term def="A measure of spread: the square root of the average squared distance from the mean. Small means the values sit close together.">standard deviation</Term> (SD):

$$
\bar{s} = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i
\qquad\qquad
\text{SD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \left(s_i - \bar{s}\right)^2}
$$

Here $s_i$ is the score on fold $i$ and $\bar{s}$ ("s bar") is their mean. The SD is a typical distance from the mean: square the distances, average them, take the square root. Write the result as $\bar{s} \pm \text{SD}$.

<KeyIdea>
k-fold cross-validation trains $k$ models, each validated on a different fold, and reports **mean ± standard deviation** of the $k$ scores. Every row is used for both training and validation, at the cost of $k$ times the training.
</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Five fold scores: 0.82, 0.78, 0.86, 0.74, 0.80">
  <Step title="Mean">
    $0.82 + 0.78 + 0.86 + 0.74 + 0.80 = 4.00$, and $4.00 / 5 = 0.80$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Distance of each score from the mean">
    Work in hundredths to avoid decimals: $+2,\ -2,\ +6,\ -6,\ 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square, then average">
    $4 + 4 + 36 + 36 + 0 = 80$, and $80 / 5 = 16$ (in hundredths squared).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square root">
    $\sqrt{16} = 4$ hundredths, so SD $= 0.04$. Report **0.80 ± 0.04**. (NumPy's `np.std` divides by $k$ like this; `np.std(s, ddof=1)` divides by $k - 1$ and gives 0.045.)
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Choosing k

- **$k = 5$ or $k = 10$** are usual: each model trains on 80% or 90% of the data.
- **Bigger $k$** means more fits, and each smaller validation fold gives a noisier score (try $k = 10$ in the visual).
- **$k = n$**, one row per fold, is <Term def="Cross-validation where each fold is a single row: n models, each trained on all rows but one and tested on the one left out. Thorough but expensive.">leave-one-out</Term> CV: thorough, but $n$ fits.
- CV **estimates** how good a recipe (model type plus hyperparameters) is. Once you have chosen, retrain one model on **all** the training data.

### Stratified folds

Random folds can get an uneven mix of classes: in the visual 10 of the 40 students fail, and with $k = 10$ one fold may get three failures and another none. <Term def="k-fold cross-validation where each fold keeps the same class proportions as the whole dataset.">Stratified k-fold</Term> deals each class out evenly, so every fold keeps the class mix of the full data. scikit-learn's `cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5)` already stratifies for classifiers but does **not** shuffle, so pass `cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)` if the rows might be in any order.

Rows that are not independent need special folds: for time-ordered data, validate on later rows than you train on; keep each patient's rows together in one fold.

## Tuning: grid search and random search

<Term def="Choosing hyperparameter values by trying several settings and keeping the one with the best validation or cross-validation score.">Hyperparameter tuning</Term> is a loop: pick a setting, score it with cross-validation, repeat, keep the best. The question is which settings to try.

- <Term def="Tuning by trying every combination of a fixed list of values for each hyperparameter.">Grid search</Term> lists a few values for each hyperparameter and tries **every combination**: 4 depths × 3 leaf sizes = 12 settings, or 60 models with 5-fold CV. The count multiplies with every hyperparameter: 5 with 4 values each is $4^5 = 1{,}024$ settings.
- <Term def="Tuning by drawing a fixed number of settings at random from ranges you give for each hyperparameter.">Random search</Term> fixes a budget (say 9 trials) and draws each trial's values **at random** from a range for each hyperparameter.

<Analogy title="Laksa tasting">
You can cook 9 pots of laksa, varying chilli and coconut milk. A grid tries 3 chilli levels × 3 coconut levels. If chilli decides the taste and coconut hardly matters, you have learnt about only 3 chilli levels; nine random recipes each use a *different* chilli level, so you learn about 9.
</Analogy>

<GridVsRandomSearch />

With 9 trials and only A mattering, the grid learns the score at just 3 values of A; random search samples 9 and usually lands nearer the peak. In real problems often only one or two hyperparameters matter much, and you rarely know which, so random search is a good default when there are several.

| | Grid search | Random search |
|---|---|---|
| Tries | every combination | a fixed number, at random |
| Cost | multiplies with each hyperparameter | you set the budget |
| Values of each hyperparameter | only those listed | up to one per trial |
| Best for | 1 or 2 hyperparameters | many, of which few matter |

<Tip>
Search learning rates and regularisation strengths on a **log scale**, e.g. $0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1$, or draw random values $10^u$ with $u$ between $-3$ and $0$: what matters is the order of magnitude.
</Tip>

`GridSearchCV` and `RandomizedSearchCV` run the whole loop, cross-validation included, then refit the best setting on all the data. Compare how many different depths each one tries.

## Every fitted step goes inside the folds

Cross-validation is only honest if each round's validation fold plays no part in training, and that includes **every step that learns from data**, not just the model: a scaler's mean and SD, an <Term def="A preprocessing step that fills in missing values, for example with the mean of each column. The fill values are learned from data, so they must come from the training folds only.">imputer</Term>'s fill values for missing entries, feature selection, and oversampling such as SMOTE. You met this rule in [Splitting and leakage](#/learn/data-and-features/splitting-and-leakage), together with the <Term def="A scikit-learn object that chains preprocessing steps and a model. Cross-validating the whole pipeline refits every step on each round's training folds only.">Pipeline</Term> that enforces it: put the steps and the model in one object and pass the whole pipeline to `cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)`, so every step is refitted on each round's training folds only.

The classic trap is to pick "the 10 most useful columns" using all the rows, *then* cross-validate. The validation rows' labels have already helped to choose the columns: that is <Term def="When information from the validation or test data (or from the future) gets into training, so the reported score is better than the model's real performance.">data leakage</Term>. The data below are pure noise, random numbers with random labels, so no model can genuinely beat a coin flip (0.5).

<CVLeakageCompare />

Why does A climb? With hundreds of random columns and only 40 rows, a few columns match the labels well **by pure chance** on all 40 rows, validation rows included. A keeps exactly those columns, so their luck carries over to the validation folds. B chooses columns on each round's 32 training rows, and that luck does not carry over to the 8 unseen rows. More columns means more chances for luck.

Resamplers such as SMOTE need `imblearn.pipeline.Pipeline` from the imbalanced-learn library, which resamples the training folds only; scikit-learn's own Pipeline cannot hold them.

## Peeking at the test set

Tuning has a subtler leak, which you met in [Generalisation and overfitting](#/learn/ml-fundamentals/generalisation-and-overfitting): each time you check a score and change something because of it, that data influences your choices; do it with the **test** set and it quietly becomes a validation set. Here is how big the effect is. The 2026 Prelim compared two protocols:

- **Protocol A**: 70% train, 30% test. Train, check the test accuracy, adjust, retrain… 50 times; report the best test accuracy.
- **Protocol B**: 60% train, 20% validation, 20% test. Tune on the validation set; evaluate on the test set once.

<ProtocolCompare />

Every measured score is **true skill + luck**, because a test set is a finite sample. One measurement is right on average, but "report the best of many" picks out the luckiest. Suppose 5 models are all truly 90% accurate and are scored on the same 100 test rows. If their mistakes are independent, the best of the five averages about 93%, and the best of 20 about 95%. This is <Term def="The upward bias you get by picking the best of many noisy scores: the winner is partly the luckiest, so its score overstates its true performance. Also called the winner's curse.">selection bias</Term>; done with a test set it is called *adaptive overfitting*: the model has not memorised the test rows, but *your choices* have. In the visual, B's best validation score is inflated for the same reason, which is why B reports the untouched test score.

<KeyIdea>
Tune on a validation set or with cross-validation. Touch the test set **once**, at the end. Any score you used to make a choice is no longer an unbiased estimate.
</KeyIdea>

### Nested cross-validation

`GridSearchCV`'s `best_score_` is the best of many CV scores, so it is a little optimistic too. When there is too little data to set aside a test set, <Term def="Cross-validation inside cross-validation: an outer k-fold loop estimates performance, and inside each outer training set an inner CV loop does the tuning. The outer folds never influence any choice.">nested cross-validation</Term> gives an honest estimate of the whole recipe, tuning included. An **outer** k-fold loop holds out one fold at a time. On the remaining folds, an ordinary grid or random search with its own **inner** CV picks the setting, which is then scored once on the held-out outer fold. No outer fold influenced any choice, so the mean ± SD of the outer scores is honest. With 5 outer folds, 9 settings and 3 inner folds that is $5 \times 9 \times 3 = 135$ fits, plus 5 refits.

In scikit-learn, nesting is one line: pass a `GridSearchCV` object to `cross_val_score`. The code below compares three numbers on pure noise: leaky selection, the `best_score_` of a grid search over $k$ (with selection inside a Pipeline), and nested CV of that same search. The leaky score looks impressive and is plain wrong. `best_score_` is the best of three noisy CV scores, so it tends to sit above the nested score, which is the honest one. (With only 60 rows, the honest scores bounce around 0.5: try other seeds.)

<ExamTip>
Look for **"optimistically biased"**, **"selection bias"**, **"data leakage"** and **"test set used once"**. Traps praise the leaky workflow as "more stable" or "using more data", or claim repeated test evaluations give "confidence intervals". A score that helped make a choice, or a CV score after a step fitted on all the data, is biased upwards.
</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>
- **k-fold CV**: $k$ rounds, each fold validates once; report mean ± SD of the $k$ scores. Use **stratified** folds for classification, especially with rare classes.
- CV is worth it for **small** datasets; with huge datasets one validation split is already stable.
- **Grid search** tries every combination (cost multiplies); **random search** draws a fixed budget and tries more distinct values of each hyperparameter.
- Every step that **learns from data** (scaling, imputing, feature selection, oversampling) must be fitted inside each training fold: use a **Pipeline**.
- Picking the best of many scores inflates it. Tune on validation data or CV, use **nested CV** for an honest estimate, and touch the test set **once**.
</Takeaways>
