A model sees only numbers, never units. To a computer, "a difference of 100" looks the same whether it is 100 years of age or 100 dollars of income. So when one feature is measured in big numbers and another in small ones, the big one quietly takes over: it decides who counts as "similar" and it makes training slow. Rescaling the features so they live on comparable scales is one of the cheapest and most effective steps in any machine-learning pipeline.

<Analogy title="Comparing two HDB flats">

Flat A costs \$500,000 and has 4 rooms; flat B costs \$500,100 and has 5 rooms. In raw numbers the price difference is 100 and the room difference is 1, so a naive "distance" says the price matters 100 times more. But an extra room is a big deal, and \$100 is nothing! Before you can compare them fairly, you have to put both differences on a common scale, for example "how unusual is this difference compared with the typical spread of prices, or of room counts?"

</Analogy>

## The problem: the biggest numbers win

Many models measure how close two data points are with the <Term def="The straight-line distance between two points: square each feature's difference, add the squares, and take the square root.">Euclidean distance</Term>. For two features,

$$
d(A, B) = \sqrt{(\Delta\,\text{age})^2 + (\Delta\,\text{income})^2},
$$

where $\Delta$ ("delta") means "the difference between A and B", so $\Delta\,\text{age} = \text{age}_A - \text{age}_B$.

Take three people: A is 25 years old and earns \$3,000 a month, B is 60 and earns \$3,100, and C is 26 and earns \$3,400. Who is more like A?

- $d(A, B) = \sqrt{35^2 + 100^2} = \sqrt{1225 + 10000} \approx 106$
- $d(A, C) = \sqrt{1^2 + 400^2} = \sqrt{1 + 160000} \approx 400$

The distance says B, a person 35 years older, is nearly four times "closer" to A than C, who is almost the same age. Income differences are in hundreds, age differences in tens, and squaring makes the gap even wider: income supplies 89% of the squared distance to B and practically all of it to C. A <Term def="k-nearest neighbours: predicts a new point's label by a vote (or average) of the k training points closest to it.">k-nearest-neighbours</Term> model built on these numbers would effectively ignore age.

<ScaleChangesNeighbours />

The label here, "needs reading glasses", depends mostly on age. Without scaling, the neighbours of a 58-year-old are whoever has a similar income, mostly younger people, so the vote goes wrong. After scaling, age gets a fair say and the neighbours are people in their 60s, so the vote comes out right.

## Two standard fixes

### Standardisation (z-scores)

<Term def="Rescaling a feature as z = (x − mean) / standard deviation, so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Also called z-score normalisation; scikit-learn's StandardScaler.">Standardisation</Term> replaces each value $x$ of a feature by its z-score:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

- $\mu$ (mu) is the feature's mean, so $x - \mu$ says how far $x$ is above (+) or below (−) average.
- $\sigma$ (sigma) is the feature's standard deviation, its typical distance from the mean. Dividing by it measures that distance **in standard deviations**, a unit that is the same for every feature.
- After standardising, every feature has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. A z-score of $+2$ means "two standard deviations above average", whether the feature was age, income or exam marks.

<Steps title="Standardise the values 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9">
  <Step title="Mean">
    The sum is $40$ and there are 8 values, so $\mu = 40 \div 8 = 5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Standard deviation">
    The deviations from 5 are $-3, -1, -1, -1, 0, 0, 2, 4$. Their squares add up to $9 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 32$. The variance is $32 \div 8 = 4$, so $\sigma = \sqrt{4} = 2$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="z-scores">
    $z = (x - 5) / 2$: the value 9 becomes $4 \div 2 = 2$, the value 2 becomes $-3 \div 2 = -1.5$, and each 4 becomes $-0.5$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check">
    The new values $-1.5, -0.5, -0.5, -0.5, 0, 0, 1, 2$ add up to 0, so their mean is 0, as promised.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Min-max scaling

<Term def="Rescaling a feature as (x − min) / (max − min), so the smallest value becomes 0 and the largest becomes 1. scikit-learn's MinMaxScaler.">Min-max scaling</Term> squeezes each feature into the range 0 to 1:

$$
x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}
$$

- $x - x_{\min}$ shifts the feature so that its smallest value becomes 0.
- $x_{\max} - x_{\min}$ is the feature's range; dividing by it makes the largest value exactly 1.

For temperatures 24, 26, 29 and 34 °C, the range is $34 - 24 = 10$, so 26 becomes $2 \div 10 = 0.2$, 29 becomes $0.5$, and 34 becomes $1$. Pixel values are a special case you will meet often: they always run from 0 to 255, so dividing by 255 is min-max scaling.

<KeyIdea>

**Standardise**: $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1. **Min-max**: $x' = (x - x_{\min})/(x_{\max} - x_{\min})$ gives the range 0 to 1. Either way, every feature ends up on a comparable scale, so no feature wins just because of its units.

</KeyIdea>

### Which one should you use?

- **Standardisation** is the default for most models (linear and logistic regression, SVMs, k-NN, neural networks, PCA).
- **Min-max** suits features with natural limits (pixels, percentages) and models that expect inputs in $[0, 1]$.
- **Outliers** hurt min-max badly: one extreme value becomes 1 and squashes every other value towards 0. Standardisation is affected too, since the mean and standard deviation are pulled by extremes. A <Term def="Scaling with the median and the interquartile range, z = (x − median) / IQR, so a few extreme values barely change the scale. scikit-learn's RobustScaler.">robust scaler</Term> uses the median and the IQR from the previous lesson instead.

<Note title="“Normalisation” means different things">

People use "normalise" loosely. In an exam, "normalise to $[0, 1]$" means min-max scaling and "z-score normalisation" means standardisation. scikit-learn's `Normalizer` is something else again: it rescales each **row** (one data point) to length 1, which is useful for comparing text vectors by direction.

</Note>

## Scaling makes gradient descent behave

Distances are not the only thing that units distort. Models trained by gradient descent (linear and logistic regression, neural networks) suffer too. The loss surface over the weights is a bowl, and the steepness of the bowl along each weight grows with the size of that weight's feature: for squared error, the curvature along a weight is proportional to the average of its feature's squared values. A feature in large units makes its weight's direction very steep; a feature in small units makes its direction gentle. The bowl becomes a long, narrow valley, and the learning rate faces an impossible choice:

- it must be small enough for the **steep** direction, or the steps overshoot and diverge;
- but then it is far too small for the **gentle** direction, so training crawls along the valley floor.

Standardising gives every feature the same spread, so the valley becomes a round bowl (as long as the features are not strongly correlated), every direction tolerates the same learning rate, and the gradient points almost straight at the minimum.

<ScalingContours />

Even with the best learning rate each run can use, the raw features need 97 steps to get within 1% of the minimum, while the standardised features need just 2. And that is with income measured in thousands of dollars. Measure it in plain dollars and the valley becomes so narrow that even the best learning rate needs about 56,000 steps.

<Mistake title="“Scaling changes what the model can learn”">

For an unregularised linear model, scaling does not change the best possible predictions: a weight on income in dollars is simply 1,000 times smaller than the weight on income in thousands. What scaling changes is how easily **gradient descent** finds those weights, and what **distance-based** and **regularised** models (where the penalty treats all weights alike) consider "big".

</Mistake>

## Which models need scaling, and why trees do not

| Model | Needs scaling? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| k-NN, k-means, SVMs | Yes | They compare points by distance |
| Linear and logistic regression, neural networks (trained by gradient descent) | Yes | Unscaled features make a long, narrow valley |
| Ridge and Lasso (L2 and L1 penalties), PCA | Yes | The penalty, or the "largest variance", depends on units |
| Decision trees, random forests, gradient boosting (e.g. XGBoost) | No | Each split looks at one feature at a time |

Why are trees immune? A tree asks questions like "is income below \$5,000?". Standardising income turns that into "is the income z-score below 0.37?", which sends exactly the same people left and right. Scaling stretches the number line but never changes the **order** of the values, and the order is all a split depends on.

## Fit the scaler on the training data only

The mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum are **learned from data**, just like the fill values of an imputer. The rule is the same: compute them from the training set, then use those same numbers to transform the validation and test sets (and any new data in production).

<Steps title="Scaling a test value with training statistics">
  <Step title="Learn from the training set">
    A feature has training mean $\mu = 50$ and training standard deviation $\sigma = 10$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Transform a test value">
    A test value of 70 becomes $z = (70 - 50)/10 = 2$, using the **training** $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Do not refit on the test set">
    If you recomputed $\mu$ and $\sigma$ on the test set, the same raw value would map to a different number than it would in training, and a test set of one sample would have $\sigma = 0$ and divide by zero.
  </Step>
</Steps>

In scikit-learn, call `fit` (or `fit_transform`) on the training data and only `transform` on everything else:

<PyRunner packages={['numpy', 'scikit-learn']} code={`import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler

# columns: age (years), monthly income ($)
X_train = np.array([[25, 3000], [60, 3100], [26, 3400], [45, 5000]], dtype=float)
X_test = np.array([[30, 4000]], dtype=float)

scaler = StandardScaler()
scaler.fit(X_train)                 # learn each column's mean and std from TRAIN only
print("means:", scaler.mean_)
print("stds: ", scaler.scale_.round(2))
print(scaler.transform(X_train).round(2))
print("test:", scaler.transform(X_test).round(2))   # uses the TRAIN statistics

mm = MinMaxScaler().fit(X_train)
print("min-max test:", mm.transform(X_test).round(2))

# the same standardisation by hand with NumPy
mu = X_train.mean(axis=0)           # one mean per column
sigma = X_train.std(axis=0)         # one std per column (divides by n)
print(((X_test - mu) / sigma).round(2))`} />

Notice `axis=0`: it computes one mean and one standard deviation **per column** (per feature). Writing `X_train.mean()` without it averages ages and incomes together, a classic bug in from-scratch Final tasks.

In PyTorch the same idea uses `dim=0`. Watch one detail: `torch.std` divides by $n - 1$ by default, while `StandardScaler` and `np.std` divide by $n$. Pass `correction=0` to match them.

```python
import torch

# 3 samples, 2 features (age, income in $)
x = torch.tensor([[20., 2000.], [40., 4000.], [60., 9000.]])

mu = x.mean(dim=0)                  # mean of each column
sd = x.std(dim=0, correction=0)     # divide by n, like StandardScaler
z = (x - mu) / sd
print(z)
print(z.mean(dim=0), z.std(dim=0, correction=0))
print(x.std(dim=0))                 # default divides by n - 1: a bit bigger
```

<Mistake title="Calling fit_transform on the test set">

`scaler.fit_transform(X_test)` refits the scaler on the test data, so training and test values are measured on different rulers, and the test set's statistics leak into your evaluation. Use `scaler.transform(X_test)`. A scikit-learn `Pipeline` does this for you automatically (see the lesson on splitting and leakage).

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

How NOAI asks about this:

- **z-score arithmetic**: $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. "Mean 10, standard deviation 2, value 14" gives $z = 4/2 = 2$. Do not forget to divide.
- **Min-max arithmetic**: $(x - \min)/(\max - \min)$. Subtract the minimum first.
- **Which models need scaling**: distance-based (k-NN, k-means, SVM), gradient-descent-trained (linear and logistic regression, neural networks), regularised models and PCA do; tree-based models do not.
- **Why scaling helps training**: features with large values dominate the gradient and stretch the loss into a narrow valley; scaling lets one learning rate work for every weight, so gradient descent converges faster.
- **Leakage**: fit the scaler on the training set only and transform the test set with it.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Distances and gradients see only numbers, so features in large units dominate unless you rescale.
- Standardisation $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1; min-max $(x - x_{\min})/(x_{\max} - x_{\min})$ gives the range 0 to 1, but outliers squash it.
- Scaling turns a long, narrow loss valley into a round bowl, so gradient descent can use a larger learning rate and converge in far fewer steps.
- k-NN, SVMs, k-means, PCA, regularised and gradient-trained models need scaling; trees do not, because splits depend only on the order of values.
- Learn the scaling statistics on the training set (`fit`), then apply them everywhere else (`transform`).

</Takeaways>
