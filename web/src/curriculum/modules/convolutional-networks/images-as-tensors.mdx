A phone photo looks like a picture to you. To a neural network it is a block of numbers, and before you can understand convolutional networks you need to see that block clearly: what shape it has, how many numbers it holds, why the "obvious" network wastes millions of weights on it, and how to scale the numbers so that training goes well.

<Analogy title="The mosaic mural">

Some MRT stations have big mosaic murals. From the platform you see a picture. Walk up close and it is just small square tiles, and each tile's colour is a mix of three paints: some red, some green, some blue. Write down three numbers per tile and you could rebuild the whole mural. That list of numbers is exactly how a computer stores a photo.

</Analogy>

## A greyscale image is a grid of numbers

A digital image is a grid of tiny squares called <Term def="Short for 'picture element': one tiny square of a digital image, holding one colour.">pixels</Term>. In a **greyscale** (black-and-white) image each pixel holds one number, its brightness: usually a whole number from **0 (black)** to **255 (white)**, with greys in between.

Why 255? Each pixel is stored in one <Term def="A group of 8 bits. With 8 bits you can write 2⁸ = 256 different patterns, so one byte stores a whole number from 0 to 255.">byte</Term>, and 8 <Term def="A bit is a single 0 or 1, the smallest unit of computer memory.">bits</Term> give $2^8 = 256$ possible values: 0, 1, …, 255.

<GreyImage />

That is the whole secret: the picture *is* the grid. Height $H$ = 5 rows, width $W$ = 5 columns, 25 numbers.

## Colour images: three channels

A colour pixel needs three numbers: how much **red**, **green** and **blue** light to mix. $(255, 0, 0)$ is pure red, $(255, 255, 0)$ is yellow (red light plus green light), $(255, 255, 255)$ is white and $(0, 0, 0)$ is black.

Collect all the red numbers and you get one grid; the same for green and blue. Each grid is called a <Term def="One grid of numbers in an image or feature map. An RGB image has 3 channels (red, green, blue); a greyscale image has 1.">channel</Term>. A colour image is three channel grids stacked on top of each other. Zoom in and see:

<PixelZoom />

A block of numbers with several axes like this is called a <Term def="A multi-dimensional array of numbers. A vector is a 1-D tensor, a matrix is 2-D, a colour image is 3-D (channels, height, width) and a batch of images is 4-D.">tensor</Term>. A vector is a 1-D tensor, a matrix is 2-D, and a colour image is 3-D. Its **shape** lists the length of each axis. PyTorch puts channels first:

$$
\text{one image: } (C, H, W) \qquad \text{a batch of images: } (N, C, H, W)
$$

- $C$ is the number of channels: 3 for RGB, 1 for greyscale.
- $H$ and $W$ are the height and width in pixels.
- $N$ is the <Term def="How many examples are processed together in one step of training or prediction.">batch size</Term>, the number of images processed together. Networks almost always take a batch, even a batch of one.

So a $224 \times 224$ colour photo is a tensor of shape $(3, 224, 224)$, and 32 of them together have shape $(32, 3, 224, 224)$.

<Note title="Channels first or channels last?">

Image files, NumPy, Pillow and matplotlib usually store images **channels last**: $(H, W, C)$. PyTorch's convolution layers expect **channels first**: $(N, C, H, W)$. Converting between the two is a `transpose` in NumPy or a `permute` in PyTorch, and forgetting it is one of the most common bugs in vision code.

</Note>

A **video** just adds a time axis: $T$ frames gives $(T, C, H, W)$, and a batch of videos is $(N, T, C, H, W)$. Each frame is an ordinary image. (3-D convolution layers such as `nn.Conv3d` expect the channels before time instead: $(N, C, T, H, W)$.)

## Counting the numbers

The number of values in a tensor is the product of its shape. This is a favourite no-calculator question.

<Steps title="How many numbers in a batch of photos?">
  <Step title="Pixels in one image">
    A $224 \times 224$ image has $224 \times 224 = 50{,}176$ pixels. (Trick: $224^2 = (200 + 24)^2 = 40{,}000 + 9{,}600 + 576$.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="Three channels">
    Each pixel has 3 numbers: $3 \times 50{,}176 = 150{,}528$ numbers for one colour image.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A batch of 32">
    $32 \times 150{,}528 = 4{,}816{,}896$, almost 5 million numbers in one training step.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Memory">

  </Step>

Smaller benchmark images are worth knowing too: an <Term def="A classic dataset of 70,000 small greyscale images of handwritten digits 0 to 9, each 28 × 28 pixels.">MNIST</Term> handwritten digit is $1 \times 28 \times 28 = 784$ numbers, and a <Term def="A classic dataset of 60,000 small colour photos, 32 × 32 pixels, in 10 classes such as cat, ship and truck.">CIFAR-10</Term> photo is $3 \times 32 \times 32 = 3{,}072$.

## Why not just flatten the image into an MLP?

You already know one network that takes a list of numbers: the multilayer perceptron (MLP). Its layers are <Term def="A layer in which every input is connected to every neuron by its own weight: output = Wx + b. Also called a fully connected or linear layer (nn.Linear).">dense layers</Term>: every input is connected to every neuron by its own weight. To use one on an image you **flatten** it, laying all $3 \times 224 \times 224$ numbers end to end in one long list of 150,528 inputs. It works, in the sense that it runs. But it is a terrible fit, for three reasons.

**1. Far too many weights.** A dense layer needs one weight per (input, neuron) pair. With 1,000 neurons that is $150{,}528 \times 1{,}000 = 150{,}528{,}000$ weights, plus 1,000 biases: about 150 million <Term def="The numbers inside a model that training adjusts, such as weights and biases.">parameters</Term> in the very first layer, around 600 MB of memory. So many parameters need huge amounts of data to train without <Term def="Fitting the training data so closely, noise included, that the model does worse on new data.">overfitting</Term>.

<ParameterExplosion />

**2. It ignores which pixels are neighbours.** After flattening, pixel 0 and pixel 1 are just "input 0" and "input 1". A dense layer treats the inputs as an unordered set: if you shuffled every image's pixels in the same fixed way, it would learn exactly as well. But in a picture, neighbours matter: an edge is a bright pixel *next to* a dark one.

**3. A small shift looks like a brand-new input.** Move the object one pixel to the right and many of the 150,528 inputs change, hitting completely different weights. The network has to learn "a cat at the left" and "a cat in the middle" separately.

<FlattenShuffle />

<KeyIdea>

A dense layer sees an image as one long list where the order means nothing. It needs a weight for every (pixel, neuron) pair and has to relearn each pattern at every position. Convolutional layers fix both problems with **small filters that look at neighbouring pixels and are reused everywhere**: that is the next lesson.

</KeyIdea>

## Normalising image inputs

Raw pixels are whole numbers from 0 to 255. Networks train badly on big, all-positive inputs, so images are rescaled before training, in two steps.

**Step 1: divide by 255.** Every value now lies between 0 and 1. This is <Term def="Rescaling values to [0, 1] with x' = (x − min) / (max − min). For 8-bit images min = 0 and max = 255, so it is just x / 255.">min-max scaling</Term>, $x' = (x - \min)/(\max - \min)$, with $\min = 0$ and $\max = 255$.

That fixes the size, but every input is still **positive**, and that slows learning. Take one neuron of the first layer. It computes $z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n + b$. By the chain rule, the gradient of the loss $L$ for weight $w_i$ is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} = \delta \cdot x_i, \qquad \text{where } \delta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}.
$$

In words: each weight's gradient is the neuron's error signal $\delta$ (one number, shared by all its weights) times the input that weight multiplies. If every $x_i$ is positive, then for a single training example every gradient has **the same sign as $\delta$**, so one update can only move all the weights up or all of them down. (With mini-batches the signs can partly mix, but the loss surface stays stretched along the "all weights together" direction, so training still zig-zags.) If the best weights need some to rise and others to fall, the neuron must zig-zag there:

<SignLockedSteps />

The two settings differ only in centring. A real first-layer neuron has far more than two positive inputs (150,528 in a dense layer on a $224 \times 224$ photo), and the zig-zag gets much worse.

**Step 2: standardise (z-score).** Subtract a mean and divide by a standard deviation, separately for each channel:

$$
x' = \frac{x - \mu_c}{\sigma_c}
$$

- $x$ is a pixel value (after dividing by 255) in channel $c$.
- $\mu_c$ ("mu") is the **mean** of channel $c$ over all pixels of all **training** images.
- $\sigma_c$ ("sigma") is the **standard deviation** of channel $c$ over the same pixels, a measure of their typical spread.
- $x'$ is the standardised value: how many standard deviations the pixel is above (positive) or below (negative) the average.

After this, each channel has mean 0 and standard deviation 1 across the training set, so inputs sit on both sides of zero and gradient signs are mixed.

<Steps title="Standardising three pixels">
  <Step title="The statistics">
    Suppose a greyscale training set, after dividing by 255, has mean $\mu = 0.5$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 0.25$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A dark pixel, 0.25">
    $(0.25 - 0.5) / 0.25 = -0.25 / 0.25 = -1$: one standard deviation below average.
  </Step>
  <Step title="An average pixel, 0.5">
    $(0.5 - 0.5) / 0.25 = 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A white pixel, 1.0 (byte 255)">
    $(1.0 - 0.5) / 0.25 = 2$: two standard deviations above average.
  </Step>
</Steps>

For models pretrained on <Term def="A huge collection of labelled photos. Its best-known subset, about 1.2 million images in 1,000 classes, is used to pretrain most standard vision models.">ImageNet</Term> you reuse the statistics they were trained with: means $(0.485, 0.456, 0.406)$ and standard deviations $(0.229, 0.224, 0.225)$ for red, green and blue. In PyTorch, `transforms.ToTensor()` turns an $(H, W, C)$ image of bytes into a $(C, H, W)$ tensor of values in $[0, 1]$, and `transforms.Normalize(mean, std)` then does the subtract-and-divide per channel.

### Dataset-wide, not per image

There is a tempting alternative: standardise **each image with its own** mean and standard deviation. Every image then has mean 0 and spread 1 by itself. That sounds tidy, but it throws information away. In a chest X-ray, overall brightness carries meaning: fluid or dense tissue shows up whiter. Per-image scaling makes a dense, hazy X-ray look just like a clear one.

It is like an exam board marking every script with the same mark scheme (dataset-wide statistics) versus rescaling each script so its average is always 50 (per-image statistics): the second makes it impossible to tell who actually did better.

<ImageNormaliser />

<KeyIdea>

Normalise image inputs with **one fixed transform for every image**: divide by 255, then subtract the per-channel mean and divide by the per-channel standard deviation **of the training set**. Zero-centring helps optimisation; fixed statistics keep real brightness differences that per-image scaling would erase.

</KeyIdea>

<Mistake title="Computing statistics on the wrong data">

The mean and standard deviation come from the **training set** only, and then the very same numbers are used on validation and test images (and in deployment). Recomputing them on the test set leaks information about it; recomputing them per image erases brightness differences, as above. For the same reason, random brightness changes as data augmentation are risky when brightness is a real signal, as in X-rays.

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

The 2026 Prelim had a long scenario on 512 × 512 greyscale X-rays comparing min-max scaling, dataset-wide z-scoring and per-image standardisation, with validation accuracy best for dataset z-score, then min-max, then per-image. Know both reasons: **zero-centring** explains why z-scoring beats min-max (both apply one fixed transform to every image; apart from a rescaling, they differ only in centring), and **fixed dataset-wide statistics** explain why both beat per-image scaling. Check which two methods a question compares: a reason that also holds for the loser cannot explain the gap.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- A greyscale image is a grid of brightness numbers (0 to 255); a colour image stacks three channel grids, red, green and blue.
- PyTorch shapes: one image $(C, H, W)$, a batch $(N, C, H, W)$, a video $(T, C, H, W)$. The count of numbers is the product, e.g. $3 \times 224 \times 224 = 150{,}528$.
- Flattening an image into a dense layer needs a weight per (pixel, neuron) pair (150 million for 1,000 neurons on a 224 × 224 photo) and throws away which pixels are neighbours.
- Normalise with one fixed transform: divide by 255, then subtract the training set's per-channel mean and divide by its standard deviation. Zero-centred inputs train faster than all-positive ones; per-image scaling erases real brightness differences.

</Takeaways>
