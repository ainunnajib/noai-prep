### Why a zero eigenvalue means "singular" (Q15)

Recall that an <Term def="A non-zero vector v that the matrix A only stretches or shrinks, without turning it: Av = λv.">eigenvector</Term> $v$ of a matrix $A$ satisfies

$$
A v = \lambda v
$$

- $A$ is the matrix, $v$ is a non-zero vector, and $\lambda$ (the Greek letter lambda) is a plain number called the **eigenvalue**.
- In words: $A$ does not turn $v$; it only scales it by $\lambda$.

Now suppose one eigenvalue is $0$. Then $Av = 0 \cdot v = 0$: the matrix squashes the whole direction of $v$ flat onto the origin. Two different inputs, $v$ and $0$, both give the output $0$, so no matrix can "undo" $A$ and tell them apart. A matrix with no inverse is called <Term def="A square matrix with no inverse. Equivalently, its determinant is 0, or it squashes some non-zero vector to zero.">singular</Term>.

The **determinant** measures how much $A$ scales areas (in 2-D) or volumes (in 3-D), and it equals the product of the eigenvalues. One factor of 0 makes the product 0, which is the fast route through Q15.

<Steps title="Check it on a small matrix">
  <Step title="Pick a matrix">
    $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Its second row is twice its first, which is suspicious.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Determinant">
    $ad - bc = 2 \times 2 - 1 \times 4 = 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the squashed direction">
    Try $v = [1, -2]$: $Av = [2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-2),\; 4 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-2)] = [0, 0]$. So $v$ is an eigenvector with $\lambda = 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The other eigenvalue">
    The eigenvalues add up to the **trace** (sum of the diagonal), $2 + 2 = 4$, and multiply to the determinant, 0. So they are $4$ and $0$, and $4 \times 0 = 0$ agrees with step 2.
  </Step>
</Steps>

A squashed direction also costs **rank**: the number of independent directions the matrix's outputs can fill. A $3 \times 3$ matrix with eigenvalues 4, 2, 0 has rank below 3. And no matrix can have more independent directions than it has rows or columns, so $\text{rank} \le \min(\text{rows}, \text{columns})$.

### The sigmoid derivative in four lines (Q34)

The <Term def="The S-shaped function σ(x) = 1/(1 + e^(−x)). It squeezes any number into the range 0 to 1.">sigmoid</Term> is $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$. Its derivative has a famous short form.

<Steps title="Derive σ′(x) = σ(x)(1 − σ(x))">
  <Step title="Rewrite as a power">
    $\sigma(x) = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Chain rule: outer × inner">
    The outer function is $u^{-1}$, with derivative $-u^{-2}$. The inner function is $u = 1 + e^{-x}$, with derivative $-e^{-x}$. Multiply: $\sigma'(x) = -(1 + e^{-x})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-x}) = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Split the fraction">
    $\dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}$. The first factor is $\sigma(x)$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spot 1 − σ">
    $1 - \sigma(x) = \dfrac{1 + e^{-x} - 1}{1 + e^{-x}} = \dfrac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}$, which is the second factor. So $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\,(1 - \sigma(x))$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

At $x = 0$, $\sigma(0) = \tfrac12$, so the slope is $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. That is the steepest the sigmoid ever gets. In a deep network, backpropagation multiplies one such factor per sigmoid layer, so ten layers shrink the gradient by at least $(\tfrac14)^{10} = \tfrac{1}{1\,048\,576}$, less than a millionth. That is the **vanishing gradient** problem behind Q44.

<Mistake title="σ′ = σ is the rule for eˣ">

The function that is its own derivative is $e^x$, not the sigmoid. The option "σ(x)" in Q34 is there to catch that mix-up. If in doubt, plug in $x = 0$: the true slope is 0.25, and "σ(x)" gives 0.5.

</Mistake>

<ExamTip title="Plug in a number">

When the options are formulas and you have forgotten the rule, evaluate every option at an easy point (here $x = 0$) and compare with what you know must be true there (the slope is positive and at most $\tfrac14$). Usually only one option survives, in about 10 seconds.

</ExamTip>

The same outer-times-inner move from step 2 decides this backpropagation question. Try it before you open the answer.

### Newton's method: count before you guess (Q74)

Newton's method takes the step $w \leftarrow w - H^{-1} \nabla L$.

- $w$ is the vector of all $n$ parameters, and $\nabla L$ is the gradient (one slope per parameter).
- $H$ is the <Term def="The square table of all second derivatives of the loss, one row and one column per parameter. It describes the curvature of the loss surface.">Hessian</Term>: all the second derivatives, an $n \times n$ table.
- $H^{-1}$ is its inverse, which Newton uses to scale the step to the curvature.

For the network in Q74, $n = 10{,}000 = 10^4$. The Hessian has $n^2 = 10^8$ entries, about 400 MB in 32-bit floats, and inverting an $n \times n$ matrix takes on the order of $n^3 = 10^{12}$ operations, **at every step**. A forward pass costs roughly one multiply per weight, about $10^4$ per example, and backpropagation a small multiple of that. Adam needs only those. So the one Newton-only operation is the one eating 95% of the time.

<Mistake title="Blaming something both optimisers do">

A 100× gap between two methods must come from something one of them does and the other does not. The forward pass, backpropagation and data loading are shared, so they are eliminated without any arithmetic.

</Mistake>

### Two computing traps, run for real (Q99 and Q115)

Q99 is about how pandas reads square brackets. Run this and watch three of the four options fail, each with a different error:

print(df["total price"].tolist())         # square brackets: works

The second line shows why the paper's word "ONLY" is a little too strong: `df.loc[:, 'total price']` also works. Among the four options offered, though, square brackets are the only one that runs.

Q115 is about where PyTorch keeps gradients. Every tensor created with `requires_grad=True` has a `.grad` slot, and `loss.backward()` **adds** the new gradient into it. Here $\text{loss} = w^2$, so $\frac{d\,\text{loss}}{dw} = 2w = 4$ at $w = 2$:

```python
import torch

w = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)

for step in range(2):
    loss = w ** 2          # a fresh loss each time
    loss.backward()        # d(loss)/dw = 2w = 4
    print(f"after backward {step + 1}: w.grad = {w.grad.item()}")

w.grad.zero_()             # what optimiser.zero_grad() does for every parameter
loss = w ** 2
loss.backward()
print("after zero_grad and one backward:", w.grad.item())
```

```text
after backward 1: w.grad = 4.0
after backward 2: w.grad = 8.0
after zero_grad and one backward: 4.0
```

Read literally, though, "call `loss.backward()` twice" could mean twice on the *same* loss tensor. PyTorch frees the graph after the first backward pass to save memory, so that version fails:

```python
w = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
loss = w ** 2
loss.backward()
loss.backward()        # the SAME loss tensor again
```

```text
RuntimeError: Trying to backward through the graph a second time (or directly access saved tensors after they have already been freed)
```

The key accepts "Gradients accumulate", the intended reading (two training steps without zeroing). Knowing both behaviours means neither reading can surprise you.

<ExamTip title="Twin options cancel">

In Q115, "Second call overwrites first" and "Only second call's gradients are kept" say the same thing. A question has one right answer, so two options that mean the same must both be wrong. Look for twins whenever you are stuck.

</ExamTip>

## The time you bank

Why care whether Q1 takes 5 seconds or 30? Because the Prelim gives one clock for the whole paper, and in 2026 the last block, Problem Solving, was by far the slowest to read. Every second saved on a one-fact question is a second you can spend on a long scenario.
