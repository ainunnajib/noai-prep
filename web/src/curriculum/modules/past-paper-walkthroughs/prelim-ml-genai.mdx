Questions 120 to 237 of the 2026 Prelim were the AI/ML/DL and Generative AI blocks: 118 questions, and not one of them needed a calculation. 87 were one-line definitions such as "Boosting reduces:". What they really test is whether you can tell apart ideas that sound alike: bagging and boosting, BERT and GPT, BatchNorm and LayerNorm. This lesson maps where those questions came from, solves one from each group in full, and then takes the wrong options apart so you can see exactly how they are built.

## Seven model solutions across the clusters

Inside every cluster the same thing happens: a question names one idea, and the most tempting wrong option is the right answer about its neighbour. Five of these pairs come up so often that they are worth a closer look.

### Bagging against boosting (Q155)

Two words first. A model's <Term def="Error that comes from the model being too simple to capture the pattern. It stays the same however much data you collect. High bias = underfitting.">bias</Term> is the error it makes because it is too simple: a straight line through a curved pattern is wrong in the same way every time. Its <Term def="Error that comes from the model being too sensitive to its particular training sample: train it on a slightly different sample and its predictions change a lot. High variance = overfitting.">variance</Term> is how much its predictions jump about when you train it on a different sample of data.

- **Bagging** trains many deep trees, each on a different random sample of the rows, and averages them. Each tree overreacts to its own sample, but in different directions, so the average is steadier: **variance falls**. Think of five friends each guessing how long the chicken-rice queue will take; the average of their guesses is usually better than any single guess.
- **Boosting** trains small, simple trees one after another. Each new tree focuses on the examples the team still gets wrong, so the combined model gets less and less simple: **bias falls**.

The same pair decides the question just before Q155 in the paper. Try it now.

### Why stacked 3×3 kernels win (Q204)

The pair here is one big kernel against a stack of small ones. You counted this in the [conv and parameter drills](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/drill-conv-and-params): with $C$ channels in and out, two $3 \times 3$ layers cost $18C^2$ weights against $25C^2$, see the same $5 \times 5$ patch of the input, and add one extra ReLU in between.

### LoRA by the numbers (Q235)

<Term def="Low-Rank Adaptation: fine-tune a big model by freezing its weights and training a small low-rank correction BA for chosen weight matrices (often the attention projections).">LoRA</Term> leaves a big weight matrix $W$ (size $d \times k$) frozen and learns a correction

$$
W_{\text{new}} = W + BA
$$

- $B$ is a tall, thin $d \times r$ matrix and $A$ is a short, wide $r \times k$ matrix.
- $r$ is the **rank**, a small number such as 8, so $BA$ is a full $d \times k$ matrix built from very few numbers.

The [conv and parameter drills](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/drill-conv-and-params) counted this too: for a $4096 \times 4096$ layer with $r = 8$, LoRA trains $4096 \times 8 + 8 \times 4096 = 65{,}536$ numbers instead of 16.8 million (0.39%). That makes fine-tuning cheap in memory and storage, but running the model afterwards is not faster: merging $BA$ into $W$ at best keeps it the same speed.

### Diffusion as a chain of small noisy steps (Q245)

A <Term def="A sequence of random steps where each state depends only on the state just before it.">Markov chain</Term> is a sequence of steps where each state depends only on the previous one. DDPM's forward chain takes a clean image and adds a little Gaussian noise at each of many steps until only noise is left; nothing is learnt there. A network learns the reverse chain, removing a little noise per step. Generating an image therefore means running the network once per step, often dozens to a thousand times, while a GAN's generator runs once.

<Mistake title="Learning definitions one at a time">

If you learn "boosting reduces bias" on its own, the option "Variance" still looks reasonable in the exam, because you also half-remember a sentence about ensembles and variance. Learn the pair as one fact ("bagging ↔ variance, boosting ↔ bias") and the twin option becomes an obvious trap.

</Mistake>

## Distractor anatomy

Setters do not make up distractors at random; each one is built to catch a particular mistake. Seven kinds of trap cover every wrong option in the 13 questions below:

- **Confusable twin:** the right answer about the neighbouring idea.
- **Opposite effect:** the right topic with the direction flipped.
- **Wrong reason:** a plausible cause or benefit that is not the real one.
- **True, but not the point:** a correct fact that does not answer the question asked.
- **Overclaim:** an absolute word such as *only*, *always* or *never*.
- **Half-done working:** in number questions, the answer with one step skipped or swapped (no square root, no division, the wrong operation).
- **Filler:** an unrelated or empty option that you can cross out at once.

These refine the five strike rules from the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination): overclaim is its *absolute words*, confusable twin its *swapped terms*, true but not the point its *answers a different question*, and filler its *vague or empty*. Opposite effect, wrong reason and half-done working are new. Its fifth rule, *clashes with the stem*, needs a scenario, so it belongs to the next lesson.

<ExamTip title="Eliminate by trap type, then decide">

On a recall question you do not know cold: cross out fillers and overclaims first (5 seconds), then look for a twin pair or a pair of options that say the same thing. You are often left with two options, and the pair you learnt decides between them.

</ExamTip>

<ExamTip title="Last-resort tells, measured on the 2026 paper">

Use these only to break a tie when real elimination has failed. In Q120–237, whenever one option was longer than each of the other three, it was the right answer in 102 of 113 questions. Across the whole paper, 68 options started with *always*, *never*, *only*, *cannot*, *all*, *no* or *none* (the 50 in the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination) counted a slightly different word list), and just one of them was right. Setters can change this habit in 2027, so it is a tie-breaker, not a method. (The checkpoint questions on this site deliberately avoid it.)

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- The AI and GenAI questions of 2026 fall into ten concept clusters; four of them held 79 of 136 questions.
- Almost every recall question is decided by one **confusable pair**: bagging/boosting, Xavier/He, BERT/GPT, fine-tuning/prompting, forward/reverse diffusion, self-/cross-attention. Learn pairs, not isolated definitions.
- Wrong options are built from a few traps: twin, opposite, wrong reason, true-but-irrelevant, overclaim, half-done working and filler. Name the trap and the option loses its pull.
- Cross out fillers and overclaims first, then let the pair decide.

</Takeaways>
