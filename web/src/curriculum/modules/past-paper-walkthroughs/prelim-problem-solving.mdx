## Stem-first reading

Scenario stems are long, but the question they ask is usually short. Four moves:

1. **Read the question line first.** It is the last sentence of the stem. It tells you what kind of answer you need: a diagnosis, a metric, a fix, or a mechanism.
2. **Scan for the numbers that question needs.** Training versus validation scores, the class ratio, the learning rate, the memory, the epoch, the dates.
3. **Sort the rest** into "useful" (rules an option out) and "noise" (the story around the numbers).
4. **Compare the options on the one phrase that differs.** Long options usually share most of their words. Find the part that is different and judge only that.

Watch an expert do it on a real question, then take the highlighter yourself.

### Turning numbers into one deciding number

Step 2 is faster when you know which number to compute. Four quantities decide most of the 2026 scenarios.

- **Generalisation gap**, $g = \text{acc}_{\text{train}} - \text{acc}_{\text{val}}$: training accuracy minus validation accuracy, so how much worse the model does on data it has not seen. A gap of tens of points means <Term def="The model learns its training data too closely, noise included, so it does much worse on new data. Also called high variance.">overfitting</Term>. Both scores low with a tiny gap means <Term def="The model is too simple to capture the pattern, so it does badly even on its training data. Also called high bias.">underfitting</Term>.
- **Chance level**, $1/K$, where $K$ is the number of balanced classes: guessing among 10 classes is right $1/10 = 10\%$ of the time. Validation accuracy sitting at this <Term def="The accuracy you would get by guessing at random. For K balanced classes it is 1/K.">chance level</Term> while training clearly improves points to a **bug**, not to the model.
- **Positive share**, $r = N_{+} / N$: the number of positive examples $N_{+}$ divided by the total $N$. When $r$ is small, predicting "negative" for everything already scores $1 - r$, so **accuracy misleads**.
- **Size ratio**, features $p$ against samples $n$. When $p > n$ (or there are hundreds of parameters per example), the model can **memorise**.

### Case 2 under the microscope: arithmetic without a calculator

Scenarios hide the paper's hardest arithmetic. Here is the fraud case done by hand, the way you would on scrap paper. The terms: a <Term def="A positive case the model correctly flags (here: a fraud it caught).">true positive</Term> (TP) is a fraud caught, a false positive (FP) is a legitimate transaction flagged, a false negative (FN) is a fraud missed, and a true negative (TN) is a legitimate transaction passed. **Precision** $P$ asks "of the alerts, how many are real?", **recall** $R$ asks "of the frauds, how many did we catch?", and $F_1 = \dfrac{2PR}{P + R}$ is their <Term def="2 divided by (1/P + 1/R). It is pulled towards the smaller of the two numbers, so it is only high when both are high.">harmonic mean</Term>.

<Steps title="Q265: 9,800 legitimate, 200 fraud; TP 180, FP 294, TN 9,506, FN 20">
  <Step title="Find the do-nothing baseline">
    A model that calls every transaction legitimate is right $9{,}800$ times out of $10{,}000$: **98%** accuracy, while catching zero fraud. Any accuracy below 98% is worse than doing nothing, by this measure.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the stem's accuracy">
    The stem's Practitioner A claims 98.7% accuracy. Check it: $\text{accuracy} = \dfrac{TP + TN}{N} = \dfrac{180 + 9{,}506}{10{,}000} = \dfrac{9{,}686}{10{,}000} = 96.86\%$. The claim is simply wrong. Never trust arithmetic printed in a stem without a quick check.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Precision: of the alerts, how many are real?">
    $P = \dfrac{TP}{TP + FP} = \dfrac{180}{474}$. No calculator: $474 \times 0.4 = 189.6$, a bit more than 180, so the answer is a bit under 40%. Since $474 \times 0.38 \approx 180$, precision $\approx 38\%$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Recall: of the frauds, how many did we catch?">
    $R = \dfrac{TP}{TP + FN} = \dfrac{180}{200} = 90\%$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Which error costs more?">
    A missed fraud (FN) loses money; a false alarm (FP) costs a few minutes of review. So recall is the primary metric. For reference, $F_1$ simplifies to $\dfrac{2\,TP}{2\,TP + FP + FN} = \dfrac{360}{674} \approx 0.53$, a number that treats both errors as equally bad, which is exactly what this stem argues against.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The simplified form of $F_1$ is handy without a calculator because it only needs the raw counts.

<Mistake title="Picking the 'shotgun' option">
Options that apply three fixes at once ("reduce the learning rate by 10×, add gradient clipping, switch to mixed precision") sound thorough. They are usually wrong when the question asks how to **diagnose**, because changing three things at once tells you nothing about the cause, and one of the three may even make things worse. The same goes for the reflex "it's overfitting": when validation sits exactly at chance, or the test data comes from a different year, the gap is not the story.
</Mistake>

The out-of-memory case needs one idea in particular. <Term def="Running several small batches one after another and adding up their gradients before a single optimiser step, so the update matches a bigger batch.">Gradient accumulation</Term> splits a batch that does not fit into $k$ <Term def="A slice of a batch small enough to fit in GPU memory.">micro-batches</Term> of size $b$, runs them one after another, adds up their gradients (dividing each micro-batch loss by $k$), and only then calls the optimiser. The update behaves like one batch of

$$
B_{\text{eff}} = b \times k,
$$

where $B_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective batch size. The gradient matches a batch of $b \times k$ exactly, except in BatchNorm layers, whose batch statistics are still computed over each micro-batch of $b$. Only one micro-batch's activations are in memory at a time, so memory drops; training does not get faster, because the micro-batches run in sequence. In the out-of-memory case (Q274), four micro-batches of 32 give $B_{\text{eff}} = 32 \times 4 = 128$. Q285 asks the same thing with smaller numbers: work out $B_{\text{eff}}$ before you read the options.

## Keyword triggers

You met a first trigger table in the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination); here are the triggers the 2026 scenarios actually used, grouped by diagnosis. A doctor who hears "chest pain and sweating" immediately thinks of a short list of causes. Long scenario questions usually hinge on one or two phrases in the same way:

- **"Oscillates wildly"** points to the learning rate.
- **"CUDA out of memory"** points to batch size and precision.
- **"1% positives"** points to precision and recall.
- **"p > n"** points to overfitting.
- **Validation at 10% on 10 classes** points to a label or pipeline bug.
- **"Trained on 2023 data, tested on 2025"** points to <Term def="The data the model meets after deployment differs from the data it was trained on (new customers, new prices, a new economy). Also called data drift.">distribution shift</Term> (see [data bugs and shift](#/learn/debugging-and-diagnosis/data-bugs-and-shift)).

## Pacing the scenario block

<ExamTip title="One plan for the whole paper">
If 2027 keeps the 2026 shape, roughly 18 of the {PRELIM.questions} questions (about 12%) will be long scenarios, most of them in the last fifth of the paper. Plan about 25 seconds for each short question and about 90 seconds for each scenario: $132 \times 25\,\text{s} + 18 \times 90\,\text{s} = 3{,}300\,\text{s} + 1{,}620\,\text{s} = 4{,}920\,\text{s} = 82$ minutes, which leaves 8 minutes spare. On this plan you pass Q100 well before 60:00, so the checkpoints from the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination) still hold; the scenarios simply use up about half of its 15-minute reserve. It is the same 25 s / 90 s plan as the time bank in [2026 Prelim: Mathematics and Computing](#/learn/past-paper-walkthroughs/prelim-maths-computing), which models 15 scenarios and so shows a little more spare time: quick one-fact answers pay for the slow scenarios. Scoring is {PRELIM.marking}, so never leave a scenario blank: if the trigger does not appear within about 30 seconds, eliminate what you can, pick one, note the number and move on.
</ExamTip>

<Note title="About guessing by option length">
The "longest option is right" tell from the [pacing lesson](#/learn/prelim-speed-toolkit/pacing-and-elimination) is weakest exactly here. In Q120–237, as measured in [2026 Prelim: AI/ML/DL and Generative AI](#/learn/past-paper-walkthroughs/prelim-ml-genai), the uniquely longest option was right in 102 of 113 questions; in Q271–300 it was right only 18 of 29 times. Use it only to break a blind guess; the reading method is what earns the marks.
</Note>

<Takeaways>
- Read the **question line first**, then scan for the numbers it needs; most of a scenario stem is scenery.
- Turn the numbers into one deciding quantity: the train–validation gap, chance level $1/K$, the positive share, or features versus samples.
- Long options differ in one phrase: compare them on that phrase only, and distrust options that apply several fixes at once.
- A small set of diagnoses repeats: learning rate, warm-up and schedules, numerical blow-ups, memory, over- and underfitting, label bugs, distribution shift and imbalanced metrics.
- Spot the trigger phrase ("oscillates wildly", "CUDA out of memory", "1% positives", "$p > n$", "10% on 10 classes", "2023 data, tested on 2025") and the answer usually follows.
</Takeaways>
