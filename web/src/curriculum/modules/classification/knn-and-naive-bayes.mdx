Logistic regression learns a few weights and then throws its training data away. The two classifiers in this lesson are even simpler. **k-nearest neighbours** keeps every training example and, when a new one arrives, asks the most similar ones what they are. **Naive Bayes** counts how often each clue appears in each class and multiplies the counts together. Neither needs gradient descent, both fit in a few lines of NumPy, and NOAI papers ask about both: what $k$ does, why distances need scaled features, what is "naive" about Naive Bayes, and why we add 1 to every count.

## k-nearest neighbours: ask the neighbours

<Analogy title="The hawker centre queue">

You are at a hawker centre you have never visited, wondering whether the stall in front of you is any good. You ask the five people standing nearest to you in the queue. Four say "shiok", one says "skip it", so you stay. You did not work out a theory of good food; you trusted the people closest to you. That is k-nearest neighbours with $k = 5$.

</Analogy>

You met k-NN briefly at the start of the course. Here is the full recipe. The training data are labelled examples $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$: each $x_i$ is a list of feature values (a point) and $y_i$ is its class. To classify a new point $x$:

1. **Measure** the distance $d(x, x_i)$ from $x$ to every one of the $n$ training points.
2. **Pick** the $k$ training points with the smallest distances: the $k$ nearest neighbours of $x$.
3. **Vote**: predict the class that appears most often among those $k$ labels. (To predict a number instead of a class, k-NN *regression* averages the neighbours' values.)

That is the whole algorithm. Training is nothing more than storing the data, so k-NN is called a <Term def="A model that does no work when it is trained (it only stores the examples) and does all its work when asked for a prediction. k-NN is the standard example.">lazy learner</Term>, or an <Term def="A method that predicts by comparing a new example directly with stored training examples, instead of learning a formula with weights.">instance-based</Term> method. The number $k$ is a <Term def="A setting you choose before training, such as k in k-NN, instead of a value the model learns from the data.">hyperparameter</Term>: you choose it; the data do not.

### What k does

Play with the map before reading on. Three things stand out.

- **$k = 1$:** every training point claims the patch of the map closest to it, so each unusual point grows a little island of its own colour. Training accuracy is a perfect 100%, because each training point is its own nearest neighbour at distance 0. Yet on 400 new points it scores only 79.3%, its worst result: it has memorised the noise.
- **$k = 9$:** each prediction is a vote of 9 points, so one odd point is outvoted. The islands vanish, the boundary follows the wave, and test accuracy rises to 88.5%.
- **$k = 25$:** the vote now reaches so far that the dips of the wave are averaged away, and test accuracy falls back to 83.8%. In the extreme $k = n$, every training point votes every time and k-NN predicts the majority class everywhere.

Small $k$ has high <Term def="How much a model's predictions change when it is trained on a different sample of data. Move or relabel one training point and a 1-NN map changes a lot: high variance.">variance</Term>: nudge one training point and the map changes. Large $k$ has high <Term def="Error that comes from a model being too simple or too smooth to follow the real pattern, so it misses in the same way whatever sample it is trained on.">bias</Term>: it is too smooth to follow the real pattern. This is the same overfitting-versus-underfitting trade-off you met with polynomial degree in regression, with $k$ playing the part of (inverse) flexibility.

<KeyIdea>

**Small $k$** gives a jagged, flexible boundary: low bias, high variance, overfitting. **Large $k$** gives a smooth boundary: low variance, high bias, underfitting. Choose $k$ by accuracy on held-out validation data (or cross-validation), never on the training data, where $k = 1$ always looks perfect.

</KeyIdea>

Two practical details. With two classes, use an **odd** $k$ so the vote can never tie. And a popular variant, **distance-weighted k-NN**, gives each neighbour a vote of $1/d$, so closer neighbours count more.

### The price of being lazy

k-NN skips training, but it pays at prediction time. To classify **one** new point it must compute the distance to all $n$ stored points, each with $d$ features: roughly $n \times d$ subtractions, squarings and additions. With a million stored examples and 100 features that is about 100 million operations per prediction, and the whole training set must stay in memory. A trained linear model needs only about 100 multiplications.

| | k-NN | a weight-based model (such as logistic regression) |
|---|---|---|
| Training | store the data: instant | fit the weights: slow |
| One prediction | compare with all $n$ points: slow | one weighted sum: fast |
| Memory | the whole training set | just the weights |

Large systems avoid checking every point with clever indexes (such as k-d trees and approximate nearest-neighbour search). The same nearest-neighbour search is what a vector database does when a chatbot looks up documents, which you will meet later in the course.

## What counts as "near"?

<Analogy title="As the crow flies, or by road">

A crow flying between two HDB blocks goes in a straight line. A Grab driver must follow the roads: along one street, turn, then along another. The two distances between the same pair of places differ, and they can even disagree about which of two places is closer.

</Analogy>

k-NN needs a <Term def="A rule for measuring how far apart two points are. Different metrics can disagree about which points are nearest.">distance metric</Term>. For two points $a = (a_1, \dots, a_d)$ and $b = (b_1, \dots, b_d)$ with $d$ features, the three you need to know are

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\text{Euclidean}}(a, b) &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d} (a_j - b_j)^2} \\
d_{\text{Manhattan}}(a, b) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d} |a_j - b_j| \\
d_{\text{cosine}}(a, b) &= 1 - \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert}
\end{aligned}
$$

- $a_j - b_j$ is how far apart the two points are in feature $j$, and $\sum_{j=1}^{d}$ adds over all $d$ features.
- **Euclidean** (the L2 distance) squares each difference, adds them and takes the square root: Pythagoras in $d$ dimensions, the crow's straight line. It is the default.
- **Manhattan** (the L1 or "taxicab" distance) adds the absolute differences ($|{-3}| = 3$): the distance by road on a grid of streets.
- **Cosine** looks only at direction. $a \cdot b = \sum_j a_j b_j$ is the dot product and $\lVert a \rVert$ is the length of $a$, so the fraction is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows from the origin. Pointing the same way gives distance 0, whatever the lengths.

<Steps title="The metric can flip a 1-NN prediction">
  <Step title="The set-up">
    The new point is $Q = (0, 0)$. Training point $A = (3, 3)$ is orange and $B = (0, 5)$ is blue. Use $k = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Euclidean">
    $d(Q, A) = \sqrt{3^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} \approx 4.24$ and $d(Q, B) = \sqrt{0^2 + 5^2} = 5$. $A$ is nearer, so predict **orange**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Manhattan">
    $d(Q, A) = 3 + 3 = 6$ and $d(Q, B) = 0 + 5 = 5$. Now $B$ is nearer, so predict **blue**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Why they disagree">
    A diagonal trip is short for the crow but long by road. Same data, same $k$, opposite predictions: the metric is part of the model.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Cosine distance shines for text. Count the words "free" and "lunch" in three messages: $a = [2, 1]$, $b = [4, 2]$ and $c = [1, 2]$. Euclidean distance says $c$ is closer to $a$ ($\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ against $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$). But $b$ is just $a$ written twice as long, with the same mix of words, and cosine sees that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(a, b) &= \frac{2 \times 4 + 1 \times 2}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{20}} = \frac{10}{10} = 1 \\
\cos(a, c) &= \frac{2 \times 1 + 1 \times 2}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}} = \frac{4}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the cosine distance from $a$ is $1 - 1 = 0$ to $b$ but $1 - 0.8 = 0.2$ to $c$: by direction, $b$ is the nearest.

### The units decide who is near

The distance formula sees only numbers, not meanings. Suppose one feature is a flat's price in dollars and another is its floor level. Two flats \$50,000 apart differ by 50,000 in the first feature, while even a flat on floor 1 and one on floor 40 differ by only 39 in the second, so the price decides the distance almost on its own. The **units** of measurement, not the importance of the features, have chosen the neighbours. That is what the "units of $x_2$" control in the figure does: multiply a feature's numbers by 4 and its differences count 4 times as much.

The fix is to put every feature on a common scale before running k-NN, usually by **standardising**:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

where $\mu$ (mu) is the feature's mean and $\sigma$ (sigma) its standard deviation, both computed on the training data. Every feature then measures "how many standard deviations from average", a unit shared by all features.

<Mistake title="Running k-NN on raw units">

Forgetting to scale is the classic k-NN bug: the feature with the biggest numbers silently decides everything. A second slip is computing $\mu$ and $\sigma$ on all the data, test set included. Compute them on the training data only, then apply the same $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to every new point.

</Mistake>

<PastPaperQuestion paper="prelim2026" n={156} />

<Note title="Too many features: the curse of dimensionality">

With hundreds of features, almost all points end up roughly the same distance from each other, so "nearest" stops meaning much. Distance-based methods such as k-NN suffer first. Fewer, better features (or a reduction such as PCA) help.

</Note>

## Naive Bayes: count, multiply, compare

<Analogy title="Whose message is this?">

A preview pops up on your phone, cut off: "…free … lunch … now". Is it from your CCA group chat or a spam bot? You weigh each word ("lunch" sounds like your CCA, "free" sounds a little spammy) and you also remember that most of your messages come from real people. Naive Bayes does exactly this, with numbers.

</Analogy>

Naive Bayes is <Term def="P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B): it turns how likely the evidence is under each hypothesis into how likely each hypothesis is given the evidence.">Bayes' theorem</Term> turned into a classifier. For a class $c$ and features $x_1, \dots, x_m$ (here, the words of a message):

$$
P(c \mid x_1, \dots, x_m) = \frac{P(c)\; P(x_1, \dots, x_m \mid c)}{P(x_1, \dots, x_m)}
$$

- $P(c)$ is the **prior**: how common class $c$ is before you read the message.
- $P(x_1, \dots, x_m \mid c)$ is the **likelihood**: how probable these features are in a message of class $c$.
- $P(x_1, \dots, x_m)$, the evidence, is the same for every class, so to *choose* a class you can ignore it. Divide by it only at the end, if you want probabilities that add up to 1.

The likelihood is the hard part. A vocabulary of 10,000 words allows $2^{10000}$ different sets of words, so no training set could ever count how often each exact combination appears in spam. Naive Bayes makes one bold simplification, called <Term def="Two features are conditionally independent given the class if, once you know the class, learning one of them tells you nothing more about the other.">conditional independence</Term>: once the class is known, it treats the features as independent of each other, so the likelihood becomes a product of one-feature probabilities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P(x_1, \dots, x_m \mid c) \\
&\quad = P(x_1 \mid c)\, P(x_2 \mid c) \cdots P(x_m \mid c) \\
&\quad = \prod_{i=1}^{m} P(x_i \mid c)
\end{aligned}
$$

The symbol $\prod$ means "multiply all of these", just as $\sum$ means "add all of these". The classifier picks

$$
\hat{c} = \underset{c}{\arg\max}\; P(c) \prod_{i=1}^{m} P(x_i \mid c)
$$

where $\arg\max_c$ means "the class $c$ that makes this score largest", and $\hat{c}$ (c-hat) is the predicted class.

### Training is just counting

Our running example has 10 short training messages, 4 spam and 6 normal, each cut down to the words of a 6-word vocabulary. Counting every word in each class gives:

| word | count in spam | count in normal |
|---|---|---|
| free | 4 | 1 |
| win | 2 | 1 |
| prize | 2 | 0 |
| now | 1 | 2 |
| lunch | 1 | 3 |
| homework | 0 | 3 |
| **total** | **10** | **10** |

<Reveal label="Show the 10 training messages">

Spam: "win free prize now", "free prize", "win free lunch", "free". Normal: "homework now", "lunch now", "free lunch", "win", "lunch homework", "homework". Count the words in each group and you get the table above.

</Reveal>

Every probability the model needs is a fraction read off this table:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(c) &= \frac{\text{messages in class } c}{\text{all messages}} \\[4pt]
P(w \mid c) &= \frac{\text{count of word } w \text{ in class } c}{\text{total words in class } c}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first is the **prior**: $P(\text{spam}) = 4/10 = 0.4$ and $P(\text{normal}) = 6/10 = 0.6$. The second is the **word likelihood**: for example $P(\text{free} \mid \text{spam}) = 4/10$ but $P(\text{free} \mid \text{normal}) = 1/10$.

Counting words like this is called *multinomial* Naive Bayes, the standard version for text.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Normal score">
    $0.6 \times \frac{1}{10} \times \frac{3}{10} \times \frac{2}{10} = 0.6 \times 0.006 = 0.0036$
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare">
    $0.0036 > 0.0016$, so predict **normal**. Exam shortcut: every word fraction has the same denominator 10, so just compare $0.4 \times 4 \times 1 \times 1 = 1.6$ with $0.6 \times 1 \times 3 \times 2 = 3.6$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Normalise (only if you want a probability)">
    $P(\text{normal} \mid \text{message}) = \frac{0.0036}{0.0036 + 0.0016} = \frac{36}{52} \approx 0.69$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<KeyIdea>

Naive Bayes scores each class as **prior × product of $P(\text{feature} \mid \text{class})$** and picks the largest score. Training is just counting. The "naive" part is assuming the features are independent once the class is known.

</KeyIdea>

### Why does such a naive model work?

The independence assumption is plainly false: "free" and "prize" love to turn up together. Yet Naive Bayes was the classic spam filter, and it still makes a strong, quick baseline for text. Three reasons:

1. To classify, it only needs the **ranking** of the class scores to be right, not the exact probabilities. The double-counted evidence often pushes the scores further apart without swapping their order. (Its probabilities do tend to be over-confident.)
2. It has very few parameters, one probability per word per class, so it learns from little data and barely overfits: high bias, low variance, the opposite end from 1-NN.
3. Training is one pass of counting and prediction is a handful of multiplications, so it is fast even with 100,000 words.

## The zero problem and Laplace smoothing

In the figure above, adding the word "prize" sent the normal score straight to 0. "Prize" never appeared in the six normal training messages, so $P(\text{prize} \mid \text{normal}) = \frac{0}{10} = 0$, and a single factor of 0 makes the whole product 0, however normal the other words are. Worse, a message such as "free prize homework" contains a word never seen in spam ("homework") *and* one never seen in normal messages ("prize"), so both scores are 0 and the classifier cannot choose at all. Ten messages are far too few to conclude that a word is impossible in a class.

<Analogy title="One free star for every dish">

A food guide lists a stall only if every dish on its menu has at least one good review. A great stall that has just added a new dish, which nobody has reviewed yet, disappears from the guide. A fairer rule: pretend every dish starts with one good review, so a missing review can lower a stall's standing but never wipe it out.

</Analogy>

<Term def="Adding 1 to every count before turning counts into probabilities, so that nothing seen zero times gets probability 0. Also called add-one smoothing.">Laplace smoothing</Term> does exactly that:

$$
P(w \mid c) = \frac{\text{count}(w, c) + 1}{\text{total words in } c + |V|}
$$

- $+1$ gives every word one imaginary extra appearance in every class, so no count is 0.
- $|V|$ is the **vocabulary size**, the number of different words (6 here). We added 1 to each of the $|V|$ counts, so the total grows by $|V|$, and putting that in the denominator keeps each class's probabilities adding up to exactly 1.

In our table every class has 10 words and $|V| = 6$, so every smoothed denominator is $10 + 6 = 16$. For example $P(\text{free} \mid \text{spam})$ becomes $(4 + 1)/16 = 5/16$, and the troublesome $P(\text{homework} \mid \text{spam})$ becomes $(0 + 1)/16 = 1/16$: small, but no longer 0. The figure shows the whole table both ways.

<Steps title="“free prize homework” with Laplace smoothing">
  <Step title="Without smoothing">
    Spam needs $P(\text{homework} \mid \text{spam}) = \frac{0}{10}$ and normal needs $P(\text{prize} \mid \text{normal}) = \frac{0}{10}$, so both scores are 0: no decision.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Smoothed spam score">
    $0.4 \times \frac{5}{16} \times \frac{3}{16} \times \frac{1}{16}$
  </Step>
  <Step title="Smoothed normal score">
    $0.6 \times \frac{2}{16} \times \frac{1}{16} \times \frac{4}{16}$
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare">
    Both have the factor $\frac{1}{16^3}$, so compare $0.4 \times 5 \times 3 \times 1 = 6$ with $0.6 \times 2 \times 1 \times 4 = 4.8$. Predict **spam**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Normalise">
    $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{message}) = \frac{6}{6 + 4.8} = \frac{6}{10.8} \approx 0.56$: a cautious verdict, two spammy words against one normal one.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The general version adds a number $\alpha$ (alpha) instead of 1:

$$
P(w \mid c) = \frac{\text{count}(w, c) + \alpha}{\text{total words in } c + \alpha |V|}
$$

Laplace smoothing is $\alpha = 1$, which is also scikit-learn's default (`MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0)`); a smaller $\alpha$ such as 0.1 trusts the counts more.

<Mistake title="Two smoothing slips">

Adding 1 only to the zero counts is wrong: add 1 to **every** count, or the unseen words are unfairly favoured. And remember to add $|V|$, not 1, to the denominator.

</Mistake>

<Note title="Two details real implementations add">

- **Logs instead of products.** Multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows: 300 words with likelihoods near $0.001$ give about $10^{-900}$, far below the smallest positive number a standard 64-bit float can hold (about $5 \times 10^{-324}$), so the product comes out as exactly 0.0. Implementations add logarithms instead: $\log P(c) + \sum_i \log P(x_i \mid c)$. Since $\log$ always increases, the class with the largest log score is still the winner.
- **Numeric features.** For measurements such as height or temperature, *Gaussian* Naive Bayes replaces counting with a bell curve: $P(x_i \mid c)$ comes from a normal distribution with that class's mean and variance for feature $i$.

</Note>

## Both models in NumPy

Final-round tasks often ask you to implement a simple model from scratch. Here is k-NN, using the worked example above plus three more points, checked against scikit-learn. `np.argsort(d)[:k]` gives the indices of the $k$ smallest distances, and `np.bincount` counts the votes for each label.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy', 'scikit-learn']} code={`import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

def knn_predict(X_train, y_train, x, k=3, metric="euclidean"):
    diff = X_train - x                          # one row of differences per training point
    if metric == "euclidean":
        d = np.sqrt((diff ** 2).sum(axis=1))
    else:                                       # "manhattan"
        d = np.abs(diff).sum(axis=1)
    nearest = np.argsort(d)[:k]                 # indices of the k smallest distances
    votes = np.bincount(y_train[nearest])       # how many neighbours have label 0, 1, ...
    return votes.argmax(), nearest

X = np.array([[3, 3], [0, 5], [6, 1], [2, 6], [5, 5]])
y = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 0])                   # 0 = orange, 1 = blue
q = np.array([0, 0])

for k in [1, 3]:
    for metric in ["euclidean", "manhattan"]:
        label, nn = knn_predict(X, y, q, k, metric)
        print(f"k={k} {metric:9s} -> class {label}, neighbours {nn}")

# the same with scikit-learn
model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=1, metric="manhattan").fit(X, y)
print("scikit-learn, k=1 manhattan:", model.predict([[0, 0]]))`} />

<PyRunner packages={['numpy', 'scikit-learn']} code={`import numpy as np

vocab = ["free", "win", "prize", "now", "lunch", "homework"]
counts = np.array([[4, 2, 2, 1, 1, 0],          # spam:   word counts
                   [1, 1, 0, 2, 3, 3]])         # normal: word counts
priors = np.array([4, 6]) / 10                  # 4 spam and 6 normal messages

def naive_bayes(message, alpha=1.0):
    idx = [vocab.index(w) for w in message.split()]
    probs = (counts + alpha) / (counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) + alpha * len(vocab))
    log_score = np.log(priors) + np.log(probs[:, idx]).sum(axis=1)   # add logs, don't multiply
    score = np.exp(log_score)
    return score, score / score.sum()

for msg in ["free lunch now", "free prize homework"]:
    score, post = naive_bayes(msg)
    print(f"{msg!r}: scores {score.round(5)}, P(spam) = {post[0]:.3f}")

# check with scikit-learn

<ExamTip title="How NOAI asks about these two">

- "k-NN predictions are based on…" the **k nearest training examples**. Not k clusters (that is k-means), not k features, not k trees.
- $k = 1$ means 100% training accuracy, a jagged boundary and overfitting; large $k$ means a smooth boundary and underfitting; $k = n$ predicts the majority class everywhere.
- k-NN, k-means and SVMs need **feature scaling**; decision trees do not.
- Lazy learner: instant training, slow prediction, stores all the data.
- "Naive" = features **conditionally independent given the class**. It works anyway because it only needs the ranking of the classes right.
- Laplace: $P(w \mid c) = (\text{count} + 1) / (\text{total} + |V|)$. Without it, one unseen word zeroes a whole class.
- No calculator? When the fractions share a denominator, compare the numerators only.

</ExamTip>

## Side by side

| | k-nearest neighbours | Naive Bayes |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | vote of the $k$ most similar training points | prior × product of per-feature likelihoods |
| Training | store the data | count |
| Prediction cost | distance to all $n$ points | one product per class |
| Key assumption | nearby points share a label | features independent given the class |
| Main knob | $k$ and the distance metric | smoothing $\alpha$ |
| Watch out for | unscaled features, many features | zero counts, underflow |
| Typical use | small, low-dimensional data | text, quick baselines |

<Takeaways>

- **k-NN** stores the training data and predicts by a majority vote of the $k$ nearest points (an average, for regression). Small $k$ overfits with a jagged boundary, large $k$ underfits; pick $k$ on validation data, and use an odd $k$ for two classes.
- "Near" depends on the **metric** (Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine) and on the **units**: standardise features before k-NN.
- k-NN is a **lazy learner**: no training, but every prediction compares against all $n$ stored points.
- **Naive Bayes** picks the class with the largest $P(c) \prod_i P(x_i \mid c)$, assuming features are independent once the class is known. Training is counting.
- **Laplace smoothing**, $(\text{count} + 1) / (\text{total} + |V|)$, stops one unseen word from vetoing a class; real implementations add log probabilities to avoid underflow.

</Takeaways>
