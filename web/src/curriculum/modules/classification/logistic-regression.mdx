Will this student pass? Is this email spam? Is this X-ray showing pneumonia? Questions with a yes/no answer are <Term def="Predicting which category (class) an example belongs to, such as spam or not spam, rather than predicting a number.">classification</Term> problems, and the simplest model for them is **logistic regression**. Despite its name, it is a *classifier*: it outputs the **probability** that the answer is "yes". Its two ingredients, the sigmoid and the log loss, reappear in every neural network you will meet, so this lesson is worth getting rock solid.

<Analogy title="A dimmer switch">

A normal light switch is either off or on. A dimmer switch turns a knob position into a brightness *between* off and full. Logistic regression is a dimmer for evidence: it first adds up the evidence into one score (the knob position, any number at all), then smoothly turns that score into "how likely is yes", a number between 0 and 1. Weak evidence gives a dim 50-50; overwhelming evidence pushes it close to fully on or fully off, but never quite there.

</Analogy>

## The problem: predicting a yes/no label

Twelve students recorded how many hours they studied and whether they passed a test:

| hours *x* | 0.5 | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 2.5 | 2.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| passed *y* | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 |

| hours *x* | 3.5 | 3.5 | 4 | 4.5 | 5 | 5.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| passed *y* | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

The <Term def="The true answer for an example. In binary classification it is 1 for the 'yes' class and 0 for the 'no' class.">label</Term> $y$ is 1 for "passed" (the **positive class**) and 0 for "failed" (the **negative class**). Two classes means this is **binary classification**.

Why not fit a straight line through these 0s and 1s, as in linear regression? The least-squares line here is $\hat{y} = 0.25x - 0.25$, and a line keeps going: it predicts $1.75$ for 8 hours of study and $-0.25$ for none at all. Neither is a sensible answer to "how likely is a pass?". A probability must stay between 0 and 1. So logistic regression keeps the straight-line part but bends its output into that range.

## Step 1: a score. Step 2: squash it.

First, compute a **score** exactly like linear regression:

$$
z = w\,x + b
$$

$w$ (the **weight**) says how much each extra hour adds to the score, and $b$ (the **bias**) shifts the score up or down. The score $z$ can be any number, negative or positive. In ML the raw score before squashing is often called a <Term def="The raw, unsquashed score a classifier computes before turning it into a probability. For logistic regression, z = wx + b.">logit</Term>.

Second, squash the score with the <Term def="The S-shaped function σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^(−z)). It turns any number into a value strictly between 0 and 1.">sigmoid</Term> function $\sigma$ (the Greek letter sigma):

$$
p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

Read it piece by piece:

- $e \approx 2.718$ is Euler's number, and $e^{-z}$ is always positive.
- If $z$ is large and positive, $e^{-z}$ is tiny, so $p \approx \frac{1}{1 + 0} = 1$.
- If $z$ is large and negative, $e^{-z}$ is huge, so $p \approx \frac{1}{\text{huge}} \approx 0$.
- If $z = 0$, then $e^{0} = 1$ and $p = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = 0.5$.

The output $p$ is the model's probability that $y = 1$, written $P(y = 1 \mid x)$ ("the probability of a pass, given $x$ hours"). The probability of the other class is simply $1 - p$.

<SigmoidSquash />

These anchor values are worth memorising for the no-calculator Prelim:

| *z* | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| σ(*z*) | **0.5** | 0.73 | 0.88 | 0.95 | 0.98 |

You only need the positive half, because of the symmetry $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$. So $\sigma(-1) = 1 - 0.73 = 0.27$, $\sigma(-2) = 1 - 0.88 = 0.12$, $\sigma(-3) = 0.05$ and $\sigma(-4) = 0.02$.

<KeyIdea>

Logistic regression computes a straight-line score $z = wx + b$ and squashes it with the sigmoid, $p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, into a probability between 0 and 1. $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, big positive scores give nearly 1 and big negative scores nearly 0. The weight sets **how sharp** the switch from "fail" to "pass" is; the 50-50 point sits where $z = 0$.

</KeyIdea>

## Making predictions

Training on the twelve students (you will see how below) gives $w \approx 2.03$ and $b \approx -6.09$. Round them to $w = 2$, $b = -6$ to predict by hand.

<Steps title="Will they pass? (w = 2, b = −6)">
  <Step title="A student who studied 4 hours">
    Score: $z = 2 \times 4 - 6 = 2$. Probability: $\sigma(2) \approx 0.88$. Predict **pass**, with 88% confidence.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A student who studied 3 hours">
    $z = 2 \times 3 - 6 = 0$, so $p = \sigma(0) = 0.5$. The model cannot decide: 3 hours is the 50-50 point.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A student who studied 1 hour">
    $z = 2 \times 1 - 6 = -4$, so $p = \sigma(-4) \approx 0.018$. Predict **fail**: only a 1.8% chance of passing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Turn probabilities into a decision">
    The usual rule is: predict class 1 when $p \ge 0.5$. Since $\sigma(z) \ge 0.5$ exactly when $z \ge 0$, this is the same as "predict class 1 when the score is positive". You never need to compute the sigmoid just to decide!
  </Step>
</Steps>

The cut-off 0.5 is called the <Term def="The probability above which a classifier predicts the positive class. 0.5 is the default, but it can be moved.">threshold</Term>. It is a choice, not a law: a cancer screening test might flag anyone with $p \ge 0.1$, because missing a sick patient is far worse than a false alarm. The module on evaluating models explores this trade-off.

<Note title="Reading the score as log-odds">

Gamblers talk about <Term def="The probability that something happens divided by the probability that it does not: p / (1 − p). A probability of 0.75 is odds of 3 (3 to 1).">odds</Term>: a 75% chance is odds of $0.75 / 0.25 = 3$, "3 to 1". Solve $p = \sigma(z)$ for $z$ and you get $z = \ln\frac{p}{1-p}$, the **log of the odds**. So logistic regression says: the *log-odds* of a pass is a straight-line function of the hours. Each extra hour adds $w = 2$ to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds of passing by $e^2 \approx 7.4$. This is why statisticians call it *logistic* regression: it is linear regression on the log-odds.

</Note>

## Two features: a straight decision boundary

Real problems have many features. With two features $x_1$ and $x_2$, each with its own weight, the score is

$$
z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b
$$

where $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2)$ is the **weight vector** and $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}$ is the dot product of the weights with the features (from the vectors module: multiply matching entries and add). With 100 features it is the same formula, just a longer dot product.

The <Term def="The set of points where a classifier is exactly undecided (p = 0.5). On one side it predicts class 1, on the other class 0.">decision boundary</Term> is where the model is exactly undecided: $p = 0.5$, which means $z = 0$:

$$
w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b = 0
$$

That is the equation of a **straight line**. On the side where $z > 0$ the model predicts class 1; on the other side, class 0. Two more geometric facts:

- The weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is **perpendicular** to the boundary and points towards the class-1 side, because moving along $\mathbf{w}$ raises the score fastest.
- The **length** of $\mathbf{w}$ sets how quickly the probability changes as you move away from the line. A long $\mathbf{w}$ means a sharp, confident switch; a short one means a gentle, unsure one.

<LogisticBoundary2D />

<KeyIdea>

Logistic regression is a **linear classifier**: its decision boundary $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is a straight line (a flat plane, or "hyperplane", with more features), with a smooth confidence map on either side. The direction of $\mathbf{w}$ turns the line, $b$ slides it, and the length of $\mathbf{w}$ sets how confident the model is away from it.

</KeyIdea>

<Steps title="Which side? (w = (2, −1), b = 4)">
  <Step title="The boundary">
    $2x_1 - x_2 + 4 = 0$, which rearranges to $x_2 = 2x_1 + 4$: a straight line with gradient 2 crossing the $x_2$-axis at 4.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The point (1, 3)">
    $z = 2(1) - 3 + 4 = 3 > 0$, so class 1, with $p = \sigma(3) \approx 0.95$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The point (−3, 1)">
    $z = 2(-3) - 1 + 4 = -3 < 0$, so class 0. Its probability of class 1 is $\sigma(-3) \approx 0.05$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Mistake title="A straight line cannot draw a circle">

Because the boundary is always straight, plain logistic regression fails when the classes are arranged in rings or in a checkerboard (the XOR pattern). The fix is to add new features such as $x_1^2$, $x_2^2$ or $x_1 x_2$: the boundary is still a flat plane in the bigger feature space, but it looks curved when drawn in the original $(x_1, x_2)$ space. Neural networks and the kernel trick (in the SVM lesson) automate this idea.

</Mistake>

## Scoring probabilities: the log loss

To train the model we need a loss: one number that says how bad its probabilities are. For a single example with true label $y$ and predicted probability $p$:

- if $y = 1$, the cost is $-\ln p$ (small when $p$ is near 1);
- if $y = 0$, the cost is $-\ln(1 - p)$ (small when $p$ is near 0).

In both cases it is "minus the log of the probability the model gave to what *actually* happened". One formula covers both, because one of the two terms is always multiplied by 0:

$$
L = -\big[\, y \ln p + (1 - y) \ln(1 - p) \,\big]
$$

This is the <Term def="The standard loss for binary classification: L = −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)], averaged over the examples. Also called log loss.">binary cross-entropy</Term> (BCE), also called **log loss**. For a whole dataset, average it over the $m$ examples. (The next lesson explains where the name comes from.)

  </Step>
  <Step title="Student B failed (y = 0); the model said p = 0.2">
    The model gave the true outcome (fail) probability $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$. Cost $= -\ln 0.8 \approx 0.223$.
  </Step>

    Cost $= -\ln 0.1 = \ln 10 \approx 2.303$. Confident and wrong: very expensive.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Average">
    $(0.105 + 0.223 + 2.303) / 3 \approx 0.877$. One confident mistake costs more than 20 times a confident correct answer, and it dominates the total.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Mistake title="Use the probability of what happened">

For a $y = 0$ example the cost is $-\ln(1 - p)$, not $-\ln p$. Student B's cost is $-\ln 0.8$, not $-\ln 0.2 \approx 1.61$. A quick sanity check: a good prediction must have a small loss.

</Mistake>

## Why not mean squared error?

Squared error $(y - p)^2$ also gets smaller as $p$ approaches $y$, so why not use it? The visual above shows the reason: look at how hard each loss **pushes** when the model is wrong.

Training adjusts $w$ and $b$ by following the gradient, and every gradient passes through the score $z$. For log loss with a sigmoid, the slope with respect to the score is beautifully simple:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = p - y
$$

<Reveal label="Show the derivation">

From the calculus module, $\frac{dp}{dz} = \sigma'(z) = p(1 - p)$. Differentiate the loss with respect to $p$: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial p} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p}$. The chain rule multiplies the two:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \left(-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1-y}{1-p}\right) p(1-p) = -y(1-p) + (1-y)p = p - y.
$$

The $p(1 - p)$ from the sigmoid cancels perfectly against the fractions from the log.

</Reveal>

So the push is just "prediction minus truth": student C ($y = 1$, $p = 0.1$) gets a strong push of $-0.9$. With squared error, the sigmoid's slope $p(1-p)$ does **not** cancel:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(p - y)^2 = 2(p - y)\,p(1 - p)
$$

For student C that is $2(-0.9)(0.1)(0.9) = -0.162$, more than five times weaker. If the model were even more wrong, $p = 0.01$, log loss pushes with $-0.99$ but squared error with only about $-0.02$. A model that is confidently wrong barely learns: the gradient **vanishes** exactly when it is needed most. Squared error with a sigmoid is also not convex in $w$ and $b$, so it can have flat plateaus, while log loss for logistic regression is convex, a single bowl.

## Training: gradient descent

There is no formula that solves for the best $w$ and $b$ in one step (unlike the least-squares line). Instead, gradient descent walks downhill on the mean log loss. Averaging $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = p_i - y_i$ over the $m$ examples, and using $\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = x_i$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial b} = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} &= \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (p_i - y_i)\,x_i \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} &= \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} (p_i - y_i)
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step updates $w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ and $b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}$, where $\eta$ is the learning rate. This has *exactly* the same shape as linear regression's gradient ("error times input"), except that the prediction is now $p = \sigma(wx + b)$. With many features in a matrix $X$ (one row per example), the weight gradient is $\frac{1}{m} X^\top (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$. Run it:

The loss starts at $0.6931 = \ln 2$ (with $w = b = 0$, every prediction is a 50-50 guess) and settles at about $0.3045$ with $w \approx 2.03$ and $b \approx -6.09$: the 50-50 point is 3 hours.

In scikit-learn the same model is `LogisticRegression`. One trap: by default it adds L2 regularisation with strength set by `C` (the *inverse* of the regularisation strength, default `C=1.0`), which shrinks the weights. A huge `C` switches the penalty almost off and reproduces our fit:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X = np.array([0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 2.5, 3.5, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5]).reshape(-1, 1)
y = np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1])

model = LogisticRegression(C=1e6).fit(X, y)
print("w =", model.coef_[0, 0].round(3), " b =", model.intercept_[0].round(3))
print("P(fail), P(pass) at 4 h:", model.predict_proba([[4]]).round(3))
print("predictions for 2, 3.2, 4 h:", model.predict([[2], [3.2], [4]]))
```

```text
w = 2.029  b = -6.088
P(fail), P(pass) at 4 h: [[0.116 0.884]]
predictions for 2, 3.2, 4 h: [0 1 1]
```

With the default `C=1.0` you would get $w \approx 1.19$ and $b \approx -3.57$ instead: the same 50-50 point near 3 hours, but a flatter, less confident curve.

<ExamTip>

How NOAI asks about logistic regression:

- **Name trap**: it is used for (binary) **classification**, not for predicting a continuous value.
- **Sigmoid facts**: $\sigma(0) = 0.5$; the output range is the open interval $(0, 1)$; $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$; and $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, at most $0.25$.
- **Decision rule**: $p \ge 0.5 \iff z \ge 0$, so the boundary $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is linear. "Can logistic regression separate a ring?" Not without extra features.
- **Loss**: binary cross-entropy $-[y\ln p + (1-y)\ln(1-p)]$. Anchor costs: $-\ln 0.5 \approx 0.693$, $-\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$, $-\ln 0.1 \approx 2.303$.
- **Why not MSE**: with a sigmoid, its gradient carries an extra $p(1-p)$ factor that vanishes for confident mistakes, and the loss is not convex. Cross-entropy's gradient is simply $p - y$.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Logistic regression is a **classifier**: score $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$, then probability $p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, which lies strictly between 0 and 1, with $\sigma(0) = 0.5$.
- Predict class 1 when $p \ge 0.5$, i.e. when $z \ge 0$. The decision boundary $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is a straight line (hyperplane); $\mathbf{w}$ is perpendicular to it, and its length sets the confidence.
- Train with **binary cross-entropy** $L = -[y\ln p + (1-y)\ln(1-p)]$: about 0.105 for $p = 0.9$ on a positive, about 4.6 for $p = 0.01$. Confident mistakes are punished hard.
- Its gradient with respect to the score is just $p - y$, so learning is strongest when the model is most wrong. Squared error with a sigmoid loses that push.
- In scikit-learn, `LogisticRegression` is L2-regularised by default; `C` is the *inverse* regularisation strength.

</Takeaways>
