Logistic regression learns a few weights and then throws its training data away. **k-nearest neighbours** (k-NN) does the opposite: it keeps every training example and, when a new one arrives, asks the most similar ones what they are. It needs no gradient descent, fits in a few lines of NumPy, and NOAI papers ask about it often: what $k$ does, which distance to use, and why the features need scaling first.

## k-nearest neighbours: ask the neighbours

<Analogy title="The hawker centre queue">

You are at a hawker centre you have never visited, wondering whether the stall in front of you is any good. You ask the five people standing nearest to you in the queue. Four say "shiok", one says "skip it", so you stay. You did not work out a theory of good food; you trusted the people closest to you. That is k-nearest neighbours with $k = 5$.

</Analogy>

You met k-NN briefly at the start of the course. Here is the full recipe. The training data are labelled examples $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$: each $x_i$ is a list of feature values (a point) and $y_i$ is its class. To classify a new point $x$:

1. **Measure** the distance $d(x, x_i)$ from $x$ to every one of the $n$ training points.
2. **Pick** the $k$ training points with the smallest distances: the $k$ nearest neighbours of $x$.
3. **Vote**: predict the class that appears most often among those $k$ labels. (To predict a number instead of a class, k-NN *regression* averages the neighbours' values.)

That is the whole algorithm. Training is nothing more than storing the data, so k-NN is called a <Term def="A model that does no work when it is trained (it only stores the examples) and does all its work when asked for a prediction. k-NN is the standard example.">lazy learner</Term>, or an <Term def="A method that predicts by comparing a new example directly with stored training examples, instead of learning a formula with weights.">instance-based</Term> method. The number $k$ is a <Term def="A setting you choose before training, such as k in k-NN, instead of a value the model learns from the data.">hyperparameter</Term>: you choose it; the data do not.

### What k does

Play with the map before reading on. Three things stand out.

- **$k = 1$:** every training point claims the patch of the map closest to it, so each unusual point grows a little island of its own colour. Training accuracy is a perfect 100%, because each training point is its own nearest neighbour at distance 0. Yet on 400 new points it scores only 79.3%, its worst result: it has memorised the noise.
- **$k = 9$:** each prediction is a vote of 9 points, so one odd point is outvoted. The islands vanish, the boundary follows the wave, and test accuracy rises to 88.5%.
- **$k = 25$:** the vote now reaches so far that the dips of the wave are averaged away, and test accuracy falls back to 83.8%. In the extreme $k = n$, every training point votes every time and k-NN predicts the majority class everywhere.

Small $k$ has high <Term def="How much a model's predictions change when it is trained on a different sample of data. Move or relabel one training point and a 1-NN map changes a lot: high variance.">variance</Term>: nudge one training point and the map changes. Large $k$ has high <Term def="Error that comes from a model being too simple or too smooth to follow the real pattern, so it misses in the same way whatever sample it is trained on.">bias</Term>: it is too smooth to follow the real pattern. This is the same overfitting-versus-underfitting trade-off you met with polynomial degree in regression, with $k$ playing the part of (inverse) flexibility.

<KeyIdea>

**Small $k$** gives a jagged, flexible boundary: low bias, high variance, overfitting. **Large $k$** gives a smooth boundary: low variance, high bias, underfitting. Choose $k$ by accuracy on held-out validation data (or cross-validation), never on the training data, where $k = 1$ always looks perfect.

</KeyIdea>

Two practical details. With two classes, use an **odd** $k$ so the vote can never tie. And a popular variant, **distance-weighted k-NN**, gives each neighbour a vote of $1/d$, so closer neighbours count more.

### The price of being lazy

k-NN skips training, but it pays at prediction time. To classify **one** new point it must compute the distance to all $n$ stored points, each with $d$ features: roughly $n \times d$ subtractions, squarings and additions. With a million stored examples and 100 features that is about 100 million operations per prediction, and the whole training set must stay in memory. A trained linear model needs only about 100 multiplications.

| | k-NN | a weight-based model (such as logistic regression) |
|---|---|---|
| Training | store the data: instant | fit the weights: slow |
| One prediction | compare with all $n$ points: slow | one weighted sum: fast |
| Memory | the whole training set | just the weights |

Large systems avoid checking every point with clever indexes (such as k-d trees and approximate nearest-neighbour search). The same nearest-neighbour search is what a vector database does when a chatbot looks up documents, which you will meet later in the course.

## What counts as "near"?

<Analogy title="As the crow flies, or by road">

A crow flying between two HDB blocks goes in a straight line. A Grab driver must follow the roads: along one street, turn, then along another. The two distances between the same pair of places differ, and they can even disagree about which of two places is closer.

</Analogy>

k-NN needs a <Term def="A rule for measuring how far apart two points are. Different metrics can disagree about which points are nearest.">distance metric</Term>. For two points $a = (a_1, \dots, a_d)$ and $b = (b_1, \dots, b_d)$ with $d$ features, the three you need to know are

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{\text{Euclidean}}(a, b) &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d} (a_j - b_j)^2} \\
d_{\text{Manhattan}}(a, b) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d} |a_j - b_j| \\
d_{\text{cosine}}(a, b) &= 1 - \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert}
\end{aligned}
$$

- $a_j - b_j$ is how far apart the two points are in feature $j$, and $\sum_{j=1}^{d}$ adds over all $d$ features.
- **Euclidean** (the L2 distance) squares each difference, adds them and takes the square root: Pythagoras in $d$ dimensions, the crow's straight line. It is the default.
- **Manhattan** (the L1 or "taxicab" distance) adds the absolute differences ($|{-3}| = 3$): the distance by road on a grid of streets.
- **Cosine** looks only at direction. $a \cdot b = \sum_j a_j b_j$ is the dot product and $\lVert a \rVert$ is the length of $a$, so the fraction is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows from the origin. Pointing the same way gives distance 0, whatever the lengths.

<Steps title="The metric can flip a 1-NN prediction">
  <Step title="The set-up">
    The new point is $Q = (0, 0)$. Training point $A = (3, 3)$ is orange and $B = (0, 5)$ is blue. Use $k = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Euclidean">
    $d(Q, A) = \sqrt{3^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} \approx 4.24$ and $d(Q, B) = \sqrt{0^2 + 5^2} = 5$. $A$ is nearer, so predict **orange**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Manhattan">
    $d(Q, A) = 3 + 3 = 6$ and $d(Q, B) = 0 + 5 = 5$. Now $B$ is nearer, so predict **blue**.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Why they disagree">
    A diagonal trip is short for the crow but long by road. Same data, same $k$, opposite predictions: the metric is part of the model.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Cosine distance shines for text. Count the words "free" and "lunch" in three messages: $a = [2, 1]$, $b = [4, 2]$ and $c = [1, 2]$. Euclidean distance says $c$ is closer to $a$ ($\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ against $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.24$). But $b$ is just $a$ written twice as long, with the same mix of words, and cosine sees that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(a, b) &= \frac{2 \times 4 + 1 \times 2}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{20}} = \frac{10}{10} = 1 \\
\cos(a, c) &= \frac{2 \times 1 + 1 \times 2}{\sqrt{5} \times \sqrt{5}} = \frac{4}{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the cosine distance from $a$ is $1 - 1 = 0$ to $b$ but $1 - 0.8 = 0.2$ to $c$: by direction, $b$ is the nearest.

### The units decide who is near

The distance formula sees only numbers, not meanings. Suppose one feature is a flat's price in dollars and another is its floor level. Two flats \$50,000 apart differ by 50,000 in the first feature, while even a flat on floor 1 and one on floor 40 differ by only 39 in the second, so the price decides the distance almost on its own. The **units** of measurement, not the importance of the features, have chosen the neighbours. That is what the "units of $x_2$" control in the figure does: multiply a feature's numbers by 4 and its differences count 4 times as much.

The fix is to put every feature on a common scale before running k-NN, usually by **standardising**:

$$
z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

where $\mu$ (mu) is the feature's mean and $\sigma$ (sigma) its standard deviation, both computed on the training data. Every feature then measures "how many standard deviations from average", a unit shared by all features.

<Mistake title="Running k-NN on raw units">

Forgetting to scale is the classic k-NN bug: the feature with the biggest numbers silently decides everything. A second slip is computing $\mu$ and $\sigma$ on all the data, test set included. Compute them on the training data only, then apply the same $\mu$ and $\sigma$ to every new point.

</Mistake>

<Note title="Too many features: the curse of dimensionality">

With hundreds of features, almost all points end up roughly the same distance from each other, so "nearest" stops meaning much. Distance-based methods such as k-NN suffer first. Fewer, better features (or a reduction such as PCA) help.

</Note>

## k-NN in NumPy

Final-round tasks often ask you to implement a simple model from scratch. Here is k-NN, using the worked example above plus three more points, checked against scikit-learn. `np.argsort(d)[:k]` gives the indices of the $k$ smallest distances, and `np.bincount` counts the votes for each label.

<PyRunner packages={['numpy', 'scikit-learn']} code={`import numpy as np
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

def knn_predict(X_train, y_train, x, k=3, metric="euclidean"):
    diff = X_train - x                          # one row of differences per training point
    if metric == "euclidean":
        d = np.sqrt((diff ** 2).sum(axis=1))
    else:                                       # "manhattan"
        d = np.abs(diff).sum(axis=1)
    nearest = np.argsort(d)[:k]                 # indices of the k smallest distances
    votes = np.bincount(y_train[nearest])       # how many neighbours have label 0, 1, ...
    return votes.argmax(), nearest

X = np.array([[3, 3], [0, 5], [6, 1], [2, 6], [5, 5]])
y = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 0])                   # 0 = orange, 1 = blue
q = np.array([0, 0])

for k in [1, 3]:
    for metric in ["euclidean", "manhattan"]:
        label, nn = knn_predict(X, y, q, k, metric)
        print(f"k={k} {metric:9s} -> class {label}, neighbours {nn}")

# the same with scikit-learn
model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=1, metric="manhattan").fit(X, y)
print("scikit-learn, k=1 manhattan:", model.predict([[0, 0]]))`} />

```text
k=1 euclidean -> class 0, neighbours [0]
k=1 manhattan -> class 1, neighbours [1]
k=3 euclidean -> class 0, neighbours [0 1 2]
k=3 manhattan -> class 0, neighbours [1 0 2]
scikit-learn, k=1 manhattan: [1]
```

<ExamTip title="How NOAI asks about k-NN">

- "k-NN predictions are based on…" the **k nearest training examples**. Not k clusters (that is k-means), not k features, not k trees.
- $k = 1$ means 100% training accuracy, a jagged boundary and overfitting; large $k$ means a smooth boundary and underfitting; $k = n$ predicts the majority class everywhere.
- Euclidean, Manhattan and cosine distance can disagree about which point is nearest, so the metric is part of the model.
- k-NN, k-means and SVMs need **feature scaling**; decision trees do not.
- Lazy learner: instant training, slow prediction, stores all the data.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- **k-NN** stores the training data and predicts by a majority vote of the $k$ nearest points (an average, for regression). Small $k$ overfits with a jagged boundary, large $k$ underfits; pick $k$ on validation data, and use an odd $k$ for two classes.
- "Near" depends on the **metric** (Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine) and on the **units**: standardise features before k-NN.
- k-NN is a **lazy learner**: no training, but every prediction compares against all $n$ stored points.

</Takeaways>
