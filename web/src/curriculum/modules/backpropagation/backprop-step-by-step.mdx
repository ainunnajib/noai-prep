Last lesson you broke formulas into graphs and learned each node's local derivative. Now comes the payoff. **Backpropagation** (backprop for short) runs the graph in reverse and, in one sweep, finds how the loss depends on **every** weight and bias. Every neural network is trained this way, from a two-neuron toy to a large language model. NOAI asks about it in both rounds: what it computes in the Prelim, and how to code it in the Final.

<Analogy title="Tracing a delay through a group project">

Your Project Work group's report is late, and the leader traces the delay backwards. Each day the slides slipped pushed the report back by $\tfrac{2}{3}$ of a day (some of it was made up at the end). Each day the survey data slipped pushed the slides back by half a day. So each day of survey delay cost the report $\tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}$ of a day. Nobody needs to see the whole project: each person only needs the rate handed back to them and how much their own part held up the next person. Backprop works the same way. A gradient is a rate like these, passed backwards and **multiplied** at every hand-over. Unlike blame, though, a gradient is never shared out: every input of an add node gets the full amount.

</Analogy>

## One rule, applied over and over

For every node $v$ in the graph, backprop answers one question: if $v$ changed a little, how much would the loss change? That rate is $\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}$, called the <Term def="∂L/∂v: how much the loss L changes per unit change in v. Its sign says which way to move v to lower the loss, and its size says how strongly v matters right now.">gradient</Term> of the loss with respect to $v$. Every gradient comes from the rule you met in the last lesson:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial\, \text{input}}}_{\text{wanted}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial\, \text{output}}}_{\text{upstream}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\partial\, \text{output}}{\partial\, \text{input}}}_{\text{local}}
$$

The upstream gradient is already known, because the node after this one was processed first; the local derivative comes straight from the gate table. The whole algorithm is four steps:

1. Run the **forward pass** and keep every node's value.
2. **Seed** the loss node with $\frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$ (nudging $L$ by 1 changes $L$ by 1).
3. Visit the nodes in **reverse** order. At each node, multiply its upstream gradient by each local derivative and hand the result to that input.
4. If an input receives gradients from more than one node, **add** them.

Why reverse order? A node's gradient depends on the gradients of every node that *used* its output, and those all come after it in the forward pass. Going backwards guarantees that each upstream gradient is complete before anyone needs it.

## A full backward pass by hand

Here is the stepper's first graph worked by hand, $L = (wx + b - y)^2$ with $w = 2$, $x = 3$, $b = 1$ and $y = 5$. The forward pass gave $p = 6$, $\hat y = 7$, $e = 2$ and $L = 4$.

<Steps title="Backward pass for $L = (wx + b - y)^2$">
  <Step title="Seed the loss">
    $\frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Square node $L = e^2$">
    Local derivative $2e = 4$. So $\frac{\partial L}{\partial e} = 1 \times 4 = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Subtract node $e = \hat y - y$">
    Local derivatives $1$ and $-1$. So $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} = 4 \times 1 = 4$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = 4 \times (-1) = -4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add node $\hat y = p + b$">
    Add distributes: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial p} = 4 \times 1 = 4$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = 4 \times 1 = 4$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Multiply node $p = w \times x$">
    Multiply swaps: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 4 \times x = 4 \times 3 = 12$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 4 \times w = 4 \times 2 = 8$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Use the gradients">
    Gradient descent with learning rate $0.01$: $w \leftarrow 2 - 0.01 \times 12 = 1.88$ and $b \leftarrow 1 - 0.01 \times 4 = 0.96$. The new prediction is $1.88 \times 3 + 0.96 = 6.6$, so the loss drops from $4$ to $1.6^2 = 2.56$.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Written in one line, the gradient for $w$ is a product of local derivatives along the path from $L$ to $w$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial e} \cdot \frac{\partial e}{\partial \hat y} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial p} \cdot \frac{\partial p}{\partial w} = 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 = 12 .
$$

That is the **chain rule** for a chain of four functions. Backprop computes this product starting from the loss end and **stores** each partial product on the way ($4$ at $e$, $4$ at $\hat y$, $4$ at $p$). The gradient for $b$ then reuses the $4$ already sitting at $\hat y$ instead of starting again from $L$.

<KeyIdea>

Backprop is the chain rule applied mechanically, one edge at a time: gradient into an input = upstream gradient × local derivative, visiting nodes from the loss backwards.

</KeyIdea>

## When a value is used twice, gradients add

In the stepper's **x used twice** graph, $x$ feeds two multiply nodes, $u_1 = w_1 x$ and $u_2 = w_2 x$. Each one sends a gradient back to $x$, and backprop **adds** them. Why add rather than, say, keep the bigger one?

Nudge $x$ a little. Both $u_1$ and $u_2$ move, and the loss feels both moves. For a small nudge, the total effect on $L$ is the effect through the first path plus the effect through the second path. This is the <Term def="When x affects L through several intermediate values, ∂L/∂x is the sum over those values of (∂L/∂value × ∂value/∂x): one chain-rule product per path, added up.">multivariable chain rule</Term>:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_1}\,\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x}}_{\text{path through } u_1} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial u_2}\,\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial x}}_{\text{path through } u_2}
$$

So a gradient is a chain-rule product for each path, summed over all paths. The next figure uses a simpler pair of paths, $u = 3x$ and $v = x^2$, which meet again in $f = u + v$, so you can check every slope in your head.

<BranchSum />

A neat sanity check is to treat $x^2$ as a multiply node whose two inputs are both $x$. Multiply swaps, so each input slot receives $g \times x$. The two slots are the same variable, so the gradient is $gx + gx = 2gx$. With $g = 1$ that is $2x$: the power rule, recovered just by adding branches.

Branches are everywhere in real networks:

- an input feeds every neuron in the first layer;
- a hidden neuron feeds every neuron in the next layer;
- in a <Term def="Convolutional neural network: an image model that slides one small grid of weights (a filter) across the picture, reusing the same weights at every position. You build them in the convolutional-networks module.">CNN</Term> (convolutional neural network), the same filter weights are reused at every position of the image, so a shared weight's gradient is the sum over all positions;
- a residual connection $y = x + F(x)$ sends $x$ along two paths (more on this in the next lesson).

<Mistake title="Overwriting instead of adding">

In hand-written backprop code, `grad_x = ...` inside a loop over paths keeps only the last path. It must be `grad_x += ...`. Within one backward pass, PyTorch's autograd adds the contributions from every path for you. Separately, each call to `.backward()` adds its result into `.grad` instead of overwriting it, so a training loop asks its <Term def="The object that updates the weights from their gradients, such as torch.optim.SGD. You build one in the PyTorch module.">optimiser</Term> to clear the old values with `optimiser.zero_grad()` before each backward pass; otherwise the previous batch's gradients are still sitting there and the new ones are added on top.

</Mistake>

## Gradients through a whole network

A layer of a neural network is just many multiply and add nodes followed by an activation node. Apply the gate rules to it and two patterns fall out.

**1. A weight's gradient is its input activation times the delta at its far end.** Weight $w_{jk}$ connects neuron $j$ (activation $h_j$) to neuron $k$, adding $h_j\, w_{jk}$ to $k$'s weighted sum $z_k$. Multiply swaps, so

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{jk}} = h_j \times \delta_k, \qquad \text{where } \delta_k = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k}.
$$

Note the index order: here $w_{jk}$ runs *from* neuron $j$ *to* neuron $k$. In [the multilayer-perceptrons lesson](#/learn/neural-networks/multilayer-perceptrons), $W$ had one row per receiving neuron, so this same weight sits at row $k$, column $j$ of that $W$.

The number $\delta_k$ is the gradient at neuron $k$'s weighted sum, called that neuron's <Term def="δ = ∂L/∂z, the gradient of the loss with respect to a neuron's weighted sum z (before the activation). Weight gradients are built from it. Some books call it the error signal.">delta</Term>. Careful: a delta is not the error $e = \hat y - y$ from the last lesson. For squared error at an output with no activation, $\delta = 2e$. The bias $b_k$ is simply added into $z_k$, so add distributes and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_k} = \delta_k$.

**2. Deltas flow back along the same weights.** Hidden neuron $j$ feeds every neuron $k$ of the next layer: a branch. Its gradients therefore add, and its activation node then scales the total by the activation's slope:

$$
\delta_j = \Big(\sum_k w_{jk}\, \delta_k\Big) \times f'(z_j)
$$

Here $\sum_k$ means "add up over every neuron $k$ that $j$ feeds", and $f'(z_j)$ is the activation's local derivative: $1$ or $0$ for ReLU, $\sigma(1 - \sigma) \le 0.25$ for sigmoid and $1 - \tanh^2 \le 1$ for tanh.

<NetGradientFlow />

Three things to notice in the visual:

- A weight whose input activation is $0$ gets **zero** gradient: it had no effect on this example, so there is nothing to correct.
- A ReLU neuron that is "off" ($z \le 0$) has slope $0$ and **blocks** the gradient to every weight feeding into it.
- With sigmoid, the activation's slope contributes a factor of **at most** $0.25$ per layer. Over ten layers the slopes alone multiply to at most $0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$, and usually to much less (a saturated neuron's slope is close to 0), unless large weights make up for it. That is the **vanishing-gradient** problem, the subject of the next lesson.

<Reveal label="Preview: the same rules as matrices (optional)">

In matrix form we now switch to the row-vector convention NumPy code uses: one example is a row $a$, and a layer computes $z = aW + b$ with $W$ of shape (inputs, outputs), the transpose of the $W$ in $\mathbf{h} = f(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$. The rules then read

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = a^T\delta, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \delta, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = \delta\, W^T.
$$

Entry $(j, k)$ of $a^T\delta$ is $a_j \delta_k$, which is pattern 1, and $\delta W^T$ adds up $w_{jk}\delta_k$ over $k$, which is pattern 2. Each gradient has the **same shape** as the thing it is the gradient of, a handy check. The network-from-scratch lesson uses exactly these lines for a whole batch.

</Reveal>

<Mistake title="Which activation goes into a weight's gradient?">

$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{jk}}$ uses the activation flowing **into** the weight ($h_j$, at its start) and the delta at the neuron it **feeds** ($\delta_k$, at its end). Using neuron $k$'s own output instead of $h_j$ is the most common slip.

</Mistake>

## A two-layer network by hand

Now a real (if tiny) network: one input, two ReLU hidden neurons and one output, with squared-error loss.

- **Hidden neuron 1:** $z_1 = w_1 x + b_1$ with $w_1 = 1$ and $b_1 = -1$, then $h_1 = \text{ReLU}(z_1)$.
- **Hidden neuron 2:** $z_2 = w_2 x + b_2$ with $w_2 = -1$ and $b_2 = 1$, then $h_2 = \text{ReLU}(z_2)$.
- **Output:** $\hat y = v_1 h_1 + v_2 h_2 + c$ with $v_1 = 2$, $v_2 = 3$ and $c = 0.5$ (no activation).
- **Data:** input $x = 2$, target $y = 1$, loss $L = (\hat y - y)^2$.

<OneTwoOneNet />

<Steps title="Forward, backward and one update">
  <Step title="Forward: hidden layer">
    $z_1 = 1 \times 2 - 1 = 1$, so $h_1 = \text{ReLU}(1) = 1$. $z_2 = -1 \times 2 + 1 = -1$, so $h_2 = \text{ReLU}(-1) = 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Forward: output and loss">
    $\hat y = 2 \times 1 + 3 \times 0 + 0.5 = 2.5$ and $L = (2.5 - 1)^2 = 2.25$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Output delta">
    $\delta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} = 2(\hat y - y) = 2 \times 1.5 = 3$: twice the error $\hat y - y = 1.5$. (The output has no activation, so this is also $\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}$ for the output neuron.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="Output-layer gradients">
    Input activation × delta: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_1} = h_1 \times 3 = 3$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial v_2} = h_2 \times 3 = 0$. Bias: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial c} = 3$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Back into the hidden layer">
    Along the weights: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} = v_1 \times 3 = 6$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_2} = v_2 \times 3 = 9$. Through ReLU: $\delta_1 = 6 \times 1 = 6$ (because $z_1 > 0$) but $\delta_2 = 9 \times 0 = 0$ (because $z_2 \le 0$, blocked).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Hidden-layer gradients">
    $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = x \times \delta_1 = 2 \times 6 = 12$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_1} = 6$. $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = 2 \times 0 = 0$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_2} = 0$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="One gradient-descent step">
    With learning rate $0.01$: $w_1 = 1 - 0.12 = 0.88$, $b_1 = -1 - 0.06 = -1.06$, $v_1 = 2 - 0.03 = 1.97$ and $c = 0.5 - 0.03 = 0.47$ (the parameters with zero gradient stay put). New forward pass: $z_1 = 0.88 \times 2 - 1.06 = 0.70$, $\hat y = 1.97 \times 0.70 + 0.47 = 1.849$, and $L = 0.849^2 \approx 0.72$. The loss fell from $2.25$ to $0.72$ in one step.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The same thing in code

Here is the hand calculation in NumPy. Each backward line is one "upstream × local" step. Run it, then change `x` to `-2` and see which hidden neuron switches on.

<NoWrapRunner code={`import numpy as np

x, y = 2.0, 1.0
W1 = np.array([1.0, -1.0])   # hidden weights (2 neurons)
b1 = np.array([-1.0, 1.0])   # hidden biases
v  = np.array([2.0, 3.0])    # output weights
c  = 0.5                     # output bias

# forward pass: keep every intermediate value
z1 = W1 * x + b1           # weighted sums of the 2 hidden neurons
h  = np.maximum(0, z1)     # ReLU
y_hat = h @ v + c          # output
L = (y_hat - y) ** 2       # squared error

# backward pass: upstream x local, from the loss back
d_yhat = 2 * (y_hat - y)   # square node: local 2(y_hat - y)
d_v  = h * d_yhat          # weight grad = input activation x delta
d_c  = d_yhat              # bias: add node passes it straight through
d_h  = v * d_yhat          # back along the output weights
d_z1 = d_h * (z1 > 0)      # ReLU: pass where z1 > 0, block elsewhere
d_W1 = x * d_z1            # weight grad = input x delta
d_b1 = d_z1

print("L =", L)
print("dL/dv =", d_v, "  dL/dc =", d_c)
print("dL/dW1 =", d_W1, "  dL/db1 =", d_b1)`} />

With `x = -2` one entry prints as `-0.`: that is just zero with a minus sign, because NumPy keeps the sign of $-2 \times 0$. It equals 0.

<Reveal label="Optional: the same in PyTorch">

PyTorch's autograd records the same graph during the forward pass, and `L.backward()` runs the same backward sweep:

```python
import torch

x, y = torch.tensor(2.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
W1 = torch.tensor([1.0, -1.0], requires_grad=True)
b1 = torch.tensor([-1.0, 1.0], requires_grad=True)
v  = torch.tensor([2.0, 3.0], requires_grad=True)
c  = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)

h = torch.relu(W1 * x + b1)
L = (h @ v + c - y) ** 2
L.backward()               # backprop through the recorded graph

print(L.item())
print(v.grad, c.grad)
print(W1.grad, b1.grad)
```

```text
2.25
tensor([3., 0.]) tensor(3.)
tensor([12.,  0.]) tensor([6., 0.])
```

The numbers match the hand calculation exactly. Note that `L.backward()` only **computes** the gradients and stores them in `.grad`; changing the weights is the optimiser's job (`optimiser.step()`).

</Reveal>

## Why go backwards at all?

You could find each gradient by nudging each weight separately and re-running the forward pass, as in the nudge check in the last lesson. For a network with a million weights, that is about a million extra forward passes (two million with the more accurate central difference) for a single training step. Backprop gets **all** the gradients in one backward sweep, which costs only a small multiple (roughly two to three times) of one forward pass, because each upstream gradient is computed once and then shared by everything below it. This trick is called <Term def="Computing derivatives by recording the computation as a graph and sweeping it once from the output back to the inputs, reusing each upstream gradient. Backpropagation is reverse-mode automatic differentiation applied to a neural network's loss.">reverse-mode automatic differentiation</Term>, and it is what makes training large networks possible.

Automatic differentiation also has a **forward mode**, which carries derivatives along with the values from the inputs to the output and needs one sweep per input. Both modes give exact derivatives; only the cost differs. Reverse mode needs one sweep per output, and training has one output (the loss) but millions of inputs (the weights), so reverse mode wins by a factor of millions.

<Reveal label="Three ways a computer can find derivatives (exam options mix them up)">

- **Numerical** differentiation nudges and divides, as in the check above: easy, but approximate and slow (one or two extra forward passes per weight).
- **Symbolic** differentiation rewrites the formula, as you do in school calculus. For a big network the formula grows enormous.
- **Automatic** differentiation applies the chain rule node by node to the recorded graph, giving exact values without ever writing out the whole formula. It comes in the forward and reverse modes above.

</Reveal>

<ExamTip>

Keep the roles straight, because Prelim options love to swap them. The **forward pass** makes predictions. **Backpropagation** computes gradients with the chain rule (`loss.backward()`). The **optimiser** uses them to update the weights (`optimiser.step()`). Backprop does not initialise weights, update weights or measure accuracy.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Backprop: run the forward pass and keep the values, seed $\frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$, then visit nodes in reverse; gradient into an input = upstream × local.
- It is the chain rule applied one edge at a time, storing each partial product so every gradient comes out of a single backward sweep.
- Where a value feeds several nodes, its gradients **add** (the multivariable chain rule; autograd does this within one backward pass). Separately, PyTorch accumulates `.grad` across `backward()` calls, so call `zero_grad()` every step.
- In a network: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{jk}} = h_j\,\delta_k$ (input activation × delta at the far end), $\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_k} = \delta_k$, and $\delta_j = \big(\sum_k w_{jk}\delta_k\big) f'(z_j)$.
- A zero input or a switched-off ReLU gives zero gradient to the weights involved, and sigmoid slopes of at most 0.25 shrink gradients layer by layer.

</Takeaways>
