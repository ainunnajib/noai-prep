Backpropagation hands every weight a gradient, but nothing promises that the gradient arrives at a useful size. In a deep network the signal from the loss has to travel back through every layer, and each layer multiplies it by some number. For years this made networks with more than a handful of layers almost impossible to train. This lesson shows exactly why, and the five fixes that make 100-layer networks routine today.

<Analogy title="Passing a message down the line">

Twenty people stand in a line at an NDP rehearsal and pass a message from the back to the front by whispering. If each person repeats it at a quarter of the volume they heard, ten people later it is down to $(1/4)^{10}$, about one millionth: the front of the line hears nothing. If instead each person repeats it 1.5 times *louder*, after twenty people it is $1.5^{20} \approx 3300$ times louder, a roar that drowns everything else. The message only arrives intact if everyone passes it on at the same volume.

</Analogy>

## Why the gradient is a product

Start with the simplest deep network: one neuron per layer. The input $x = a_0$ passes through $D$ layers, and layer $k$ computes

$$
z_k = w_k \, a_{k-1}, \qquad a_k = \varphi(z_k)
$$

- $a_{k-1}$ is the output of the layer before, and $w_k$ is layer $k$'s weight.
- $z_k$ is the <Term def="The weighted input of a neuron, before the activation function is applied.">pre-activation</Term>, and $\varphi$ is the activation function (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU). $\varphi'(z_k)$ is its slope at $z_k$.

Backpropagation walks back one edge at a time, multiplying by each local derivative. Stepping back through layer $k$ means multiplying by $\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial a_{k-1}} = w_k \, \varphi'(z_k)$. So the gradient that reaches the first layer is

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_D} \times w_2\varphi'(z_2) \times \cdots \times w_D\varphi'(z_D)
$$

In words: **the gradient at layer 1 is the gradient the loss sends back, times one factor (weight × slope) for every layer in between.** The first weight's own gradient is built from it, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1} \, \varphi'(z_1) \, x$, so when $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_1}$ is tiny, $w_1$ hardly moves. If every factor is about the same number $f$, the product is $f^{D-1}$, and powers change fast: $0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}$ while $1.5^{20} \approx 3300$.

<KeyIdea>

The gradient reaching an early layer is a **product of one factor per later layer**. Factors below 1 make it shrink exponentially with depth, a <Term def="When gradients get smaller and smaller as they travel back towards the input, so the early layers barely learn.">vanishing gradient</Term>; factors above 1 make it grow exponentially, an <Term def="When gradients get larger and larger as they travel back, giving huge, unstable weight updates.">exploding gradient</Term>.

</KeyIdea>

## Why sigmoid is the usual culprit

The sigmoid $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ has the slope $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)$. Write $p = \sigma(z)$, a number between 0 and 1. The product $p(1-p)$ is largest when $p = 0.5$, which happens at $z = 0$, and there it equals $0.25$. So **a sigmoid never passes back more than a quarter of the gradient it receives** (times its weight). For large $|z|$ the curve goes flat, the neuron <Term def="A sigmoid or tanh neuron saturates when its input is so large or so negative that the curve is flat there, so its slope is almost 0.">saturates</Term>, and the slope is close to 0: even worse.

<Steps title="How small does it get? (no calculator)">
  <Step title="The best case: $z = 0$">
    $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, so $\sigma'(0) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="A saturating neuron: $z = 2$ and $z = 5$">
    $\sigma(2) \approx 0.88$, so $\sigma'(2) \approx 0.88 \times 0.12 \approx 0.105$, about $0.1$. At $z = 5$, $\sigma \approx 0.993$ and $\sigma' \approx 0.007$.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Ten layers, best case, every weight 1">
    The factors multiply: $0.25^{10} = \frac{1}{4^{10}}$. Speed trick: $4^5 = 1024 \approx 10^3$, so $4^{10} \approx 10^6$. The gradient is about **one millionth** of what the loss sent back.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What that means for training">
    Each weight moves by (learning rate) × (gradient). If the last layer's weights move by about 0.01 per step, the first layer's move by about $10^{-8}$: they would need a million times as many steps to learn the same amount. In practice they stay near their random starting values while the later layers do all the work.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Exploding gradients and `nan` losses

Factors above 1 cause the opposite problem. A ReLU has slope 1 for positive inputs, so with weights around 1.5 each layer multiplies the gradient by 1.5, and twenty layers give $1.5^{20} \approx 3300$. Gradient descent then takes a giant step: the update $w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.01$ and a gradient of 10 000 moves the weight by 100.

After a step like that the outputs become enormous and the numbers <Term def="Grow beyond the largest number the computer's number format can store, which turns them into infinity.">overflow</Term>. A `float32` can hold numbers up to about $3.4 \times 10^{38}$; anything bigger becomes `inf`, and `inf - inf` or `0 * inf` gives `nan` ("not a number"). Any sum or product that touches a `nan` is also `nan`, so once one appears the loss prints `nan` for ever after. A typical training log reads 2.31, 2.28, 2.26, 845.2, then `nan`, `nan`, `nan`.

## Activation, initialisation and depth, together

Real layers have many neurons, so each factor becomes a matrix (the weights) combined with the slopes, but the story is the same: each layer stretches or shrinks the gradient by some typical factor, and depth turns that factor into a power. Two things set the factor: **the activation's slopes** and **the size of the weights**.

Take a square layer, with $n$ inputs (its <Term def="The number of inputs feeding into each neuron of a layer.">fan-in</Term>) and $n$ outputs, as in the chart below. In the backward pass, each neuron's gradient is a sum of $n$ contributions, one from each neuron it feeds. A sum of $n$ random $\pm 1$ terms has a typical size of about $\sqrt{n}$, not $n$, because the pluses and minuses partly cancel: 4 terms give about 2, and 100 terms about 10. So weights of size about $1/\sqrt{n}$ cancel that growth. Write the weights' standard deviation as $k/\sqrt{n}$; then $k$ is whatever stretching is left over, and the layer scales the gradient's size by roughly $k$ times the typical (<Term def="Root-mean-square: square the values, take their mean, then the square root. For ReLU slopes (half 1s, half 0s) it is the square root of 1/2, about 0.71.">root-mean-square</Term>) slope of its activation:

- **tanh** near 0 has slope about 1, so $k = 1$ keeps the factor near 1. This is <Term def="Random weights with variance 2/(n_in + n_out), which is 1/n when a layer has n inputs and n outputs. Designed for sigmoid and tanh.">Xavier (Glorot) initialisation</Term>.
- **ReLU** has slope 1 on the half of its neurons that are active and 0 on the rest, so its root-mean-square slope is $\sqrt{1/2} \approx 0.71$. Choosing $k = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ makes up for it, since $1.41 \times 0.71 \approx 1$. This is <Term def="Random weights with variance 2/n_in, where n_in is the fan-in. Designed for ReLU layers.">He (Kaiming) initialisation</Term>.
- **sigmoid** has slope at most 0.25 (about 0.2 on average), so with sensible weights its factor stays well below 1.

<Reveal label="The exact variances (optional)">

Xavier draws the weights with variance $2/(n_{in} + n_{out})$, which is $1/n$ when a layer has $n$ inputs and $n$ outputs: that is $k = 1$. He uses variance $2/n_{in}$, that is $k = \sqrt{2}$. ReLU passes only its active half, which halves the signal's squared size, and doubling the variance puts it back.

</Reveal>

<GradientDepthBars />

Each of the standard fixes attacks one cause:

1. **ReLU in the hidden layers.** Its slope is exactly 1 for every positive input and it never saturates there, so active neurons pass the gradient back undiminished. The catch: its slope is 0 for negative inputs, and a neuron whose input is negative for every example never learns again (the "dying ReLU"). Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope such as 0.01 for negative inputs.
2. **He or Xavier initialisation.** Choose the starting weight scale so each layer's factor begins near 1.
3. **<Term def="Batch normalisation: for each neuron, subtract the mini-batch mean and divide by the mini-batch standard deviation of its pre-activations, then apply a learned scale and shift.">BatchNorm</Term>.** Rescales every neuron's pre-activations over the mini-batch to mean 0 and variance 1, so the weights' scale stops mattering. In the chart, switch BatchNorm on and drag $k$: the dots hardly move.
4. **Residual connections**, next.
5. **Gradient clipping**, for the explosions that still happen.

Initialisation and normalisation get a full lesson in the module on training deep networks; here the point is *why* they work. Good initialisation starts the per-layer factor near 1, and normalisation stops it depending on the size of the weights.

<Reveal label="Going further: BatchNorm's limits, and recurrent networks">

BatchNorm alone does not force the factor to exactly 1. Try ReLU + BatchNorm (residual off) at depth 30 in the chart: the gradient still grows about 1.2 times per layer, roughly 560 times in all. That is why ResNets combine it with residual connections (setting 7).

A recurrent network (RNN) applies the *same* weights at every time step, so a 100-word sentence behaves like a 100-layer network, and the same factor gets multiplied a hundred times. RNNs suffer from both problems. LSTMs add a gated memory path that works much like the residual highway below, and gradient clipping is standard when training them.

</Reveal>

## Residual connections: a gradient highway

<Analogy title="The expressway">

Driving from Jurong to Changi on local roads means stopping at every traffic light; one jammed junction and nothing gets through. The PIE bypasses all of them. A residual connection gives the gradient its own expressway past every layer.

</Analogy>

A <Term def="A shortcut that adds a block's input to its output, y = x + F(x), so the block only has to learn the change F(x).">residual (skip) connection</Term> adds a block's input to its output:

$$
y = x + F(x)
$$

Here $F$ is the block's ordinary layers (often two layers with an activation between them) and $x$ is the block's input. Differentiate both sides:

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = 1 + F'(x)
$$

so the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}}_{\text{skip path}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\,F'(x)}_{\text{through } F}
$$

The first term is the gradient from above, **passed back unchanged**, however small $F'(x)$ is. Stack $D$ blocks and a plain network multiplies $F'_1 F'_2 \cdots F'_D$, which collapses when the factors are small. A residual network multiplies $(1 + F'_1)(1 + F'_2)\cdots(1 + F'_D)$; multiply it out and one of the terms is always $1 \times 1 \times \cdots \times 1 = 1$: the path straight down the skip connections. So the gradient cannot be wiped out just because every $F'$ is small. (In a real network $F'$ can also be negative, so the skip path guarantees a direct route for the gradient, not that it can never shrink.)

<Steps title="Three blocks, each with $F' = 0.1$">
  <Step title="Plain stack">
    $0.1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.001$. The gradient is a thousand times smaller after just three blocks.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Residual stack">
    $(1 + 0.1)^3 = 1.1 \times 1.1 \times 1.1 = 1.331$. The gradient arrives at full strength.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<ResidualHighway />

This is the idea behind **ResNet** (2015), which trained networks with 152 layers at a time when a plain 34-layer network trained *worse* than a plain 18-layer one. Every Transformer block uses residual connections too. Notice in the gradient-size chart above (setting 6) and in the highway with a large $s$ that residual connections alone can let the gradient *grow*, because every block adds its own contribution; that is why ResNets also put BatchNorm inside every block.

## Gradient clipping: a cap for explosions

Even with good initialisation, an unlucky batch can produce one enormous gradient, and one bad step can wreck weights that took hours to train. <Term def="Shrinking the gradient before the weight update whenever it is larger than a chosen threshold.">Gradient clipping</Term> caps the gradient's size before the update. The usual version, **clipping by norm** with threshold $c$ (PyTorch calls it `max_norm`):

$$
g \leftarrow \begin{cases} g & \text{if } \lVert g \rVert \le c \\[4pt] g \cdot \dfrac{c}{\lVert g \rVert} & \text{if } \lVert g \rVert > c \end{cases}
$$

- $g$ is the gradient of *all* the parameters, written as one long vector, and $\lVert g \rVert$ is its length (the square root of the sum of squares).
- When $g$ is too long, multiplying by $\frac{c}{\lVert g \rVert}$ (a number below 1) makes its length exactly $c$ **without changing its direction**.

For example, $g = (6, 8)$ has $\lVert g \rVert = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$. With $c = 5$, multiply by $5/10$: the clipped gradient is $(3, 4)$, still pointing the same way but half as long.

<ClipByNorm />

PyTorch also has `clip_grad_value_`, which cuts each component to $[-c, c]$ separately. That can turn the gradient's direction, which is why clipping by norm is the usual choice; try **clip by value** in the figure to see the turn.

In PyTorch the clip goes after the gradients exist and before the optimiser uses them:

```python
loss.backward()     # 1. compute the gradients
# 2. cap their total length at max_norm
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
optimiser.step()    # 3. update with the capped gradients
```

<Mistake title="Clipping does not cure vanishing gradients">

Clipping only ever makes gradients *shorter*. A gradient of $10^{-9}$ is left exactly as it is. Vanishing gradients need ReLU, good initialisation, normalisation or residual connections; clipping is the tool for **exploding** gradients.

</Mistake>

## Diagnose and fix

| What you see | Likely problem | First things to try |
|---|---|---|
| Gradient norms shrink layer by layer towards the input; early layers barely change; the loss plateaus | Vanishing gradients | ReLU, He initialisation, BatchNorm, residual connections |
| Loss spikes, then turns into `nan`; gradient norms in the thousands or more | Exploding gradients | Gradient clipping, a lower learning rate, better initialisation, normalisation |
| Many ReLU neurons output 0 for every input and their gradients are 0 | Dying ReLU | Leaky ReLU, a lower learning rate |

<ExamTip>

Prelim questions on this topic are mostly matching: **sigmoid or tanh derivative below 1 → vanishing**; **residual / skip connections → gradient flow in very deep networks (ResNet)**; **gradient clipping → exploding gradients**; **He → ReLU, Xavier → sigmoid/tanh**; **LSTM → vanishing gradients in RNNs**; **`nan` loss → learning rate too high or exploding gradients**. For quick sums, remember $0.25^n = 1/4^n$, $4^5 \approx 10^3$ and $2^{10} \approx 10^3$. If you are given a table of gradient norms per layer, compare the input end with the output end: tiny at the input means vanishing, huge means exploding.

</ExamTip>

<Takeaways>

- Backprop multiplies **one factor per layer** (weight × activation slope), so gradient size changes **exponentially** with depth: below 1 it vanishes, above 1 it explodes.
- Sigmoid's slope is at most **0.25**, so deep sigmoid networks vanish ($0.25^{10} \approx 10^{-6}$); exploding gradients cause huge updates, overflow and `nan` losses.
- Fixes: **ReLU** (slope 1), **He/Xavier initialisation** (factor near 1 at the start), **BatchNorm** (the weights' scale stops mattering), **residual connections** ($y = x + F(x)$ gives $\partial y / \partial x = 1 + F'(x)$, a gradient highway).
- **Gradient clipping** rescales $g$ to length $c$ when $\lVert g \rVert > c$, keeping its direction; it only fixes *exploding* gradients and goes between `loss.backward()` and `optimiser.step()`.

</Takeaways>
