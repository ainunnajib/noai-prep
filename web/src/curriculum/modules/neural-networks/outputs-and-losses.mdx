The hidden layers build useful features; the **output layer** and the loss must turn them into an answer of the right *kind*: a price, a yes or no, one digit out of ten, or a set of tags. Get that pairing wrong and nothing crashes: the network just trains slowly, badly or not at all, one of the sneakiest bugs in deep learning.

<Analogy title="The right container for the takeaway">

At a hawker centre, soup goes in a bowl with a lid, satay in a paper bag and a drink in a cup with a straw. Put the soup in the paper bag and the food is fine, but you get a mess. A network's output layer is the container for its answer (a number, a probability, a choice among options, or several yes/no answers), and each kind of answer has its own container and its own way of being checked.

</Analogy>

## Logits: what the last layer really outputs

The output layer is an ordinary fully connected layer: it computes $\mathbf{z} = W\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}$ from the last hidden layer $\mathbf{h}$. Its raw outputs are called <Term def="The raw, unsquashed scores produced by a network's last layer, before any sigmoid or softmax. They can be any real number.">logits</Term>: any real numbers, positive or negative, with no particular range. The output layer together with whatever we do to its logits is often called the network's <Term def="The final part of a network that turns features into the answer for a particular task: the output layer and its activation (trained with a matching loss).">head</Term>.

To turn logits into an answer we may squash them with one of two functions you met in the classification module, from [Logistic regression](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression) onwards:

- The **sigmoid** $\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ turns **one** logit into a probability between 0 and 1. Big positive $z$ gives nearly 1, big negative $z$ nearly 0, and $\sigma(0) = 0.5$.
- The **softmax** turns **K** logits $z_1, \dots, z_K$ into $K$ probabilities that are all positive and add up to 1:

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2} + \dots + e^{z_K}}
$$

Each logit is exponentiated (made positive), then divided by the total so they share 100%; the biggest logit always gets the biggest probability.

## Four kinds of task, four heads

You met the three classification heads in [softmax and multi-class](#/learn/classification/softmax-and-multiclass); regression adds a fourth.

| Task | Output layer | Loss |
|---|---|---|
| **Regression**, e.g. an HDB flat's resale price | 1 output per target, used as it is (no activation) | mean squared error (or mean absolute error) |
| **Binary** classification, e.g. spam or not | 1 logit → sigmoid | binary cross-entropy |
| **Multi-class** classification, e.g. which digit, 0 to 9 | $K$ logits → softmax | cross-entropy |
| **Multi-label** classification, e.g. which dishes are in a photo | $K$ logits → a separate sigmoid on each | binary cross-entropy on each label |

In PyTorch the four losses are `nn.MSELoss` (or `nn.L1Loss` for MAE) for regression, `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` for binary **and** multi-label, and `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` for multi-class.

### Regression: a raw number

The output can be any size, so the last layer has **no activation**: squashing it with a sigmoid (always between 0 and 1) would make a price such as 500,000 impossible to predict. For $N$ examples with true values $y_i$ and predictions $\hat{y}_i$, the two standard losses are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \\[6pt]
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
\end{gathered}
$$

The <Term def="Mean squared error: the average of the squared differences between predictions and true values. The standard regression loss.">MSE</Term> averages the squared errors; the <Term def="Mean absolute error: the average of the absolute (unsigned) differences between predictions and true values.">MAE</Term> averages the plain sizes of the errors. Predict $3$ and $5$ when the truth is $2$ and $9$: the errors are $1$ and $4$, so $\text{MSE} = (1 + 16)/2 = 8.5$ but $\text{MAE} = (1 + 4)/2 = 2.5$. The single big miss supplies 16 of the 17 squared units: MSE punishes large errors much harder, while MAE is less thrown by a few outliers.

### Binary classification: one logit and a sigmoid

One yes/no question needs one logit $z$. The probability of "yes" is $p = \sigma(z)$, and the loss is <Term def="The loss for a yes/no prediction: L = −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)], where y is 1 or 0 and p is the predicted probability of 1.">binary cross-entropy</Term>:

$$
L = -\big[\, y \ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p) \,\big]
$$

where $y$ is 1 for "yes" and 0 for "no". Only one term is ever switched on: the loss is $-\ln$ of the probability the model gave to what actually happened.

<Steps title="Binary cross-entropy by hand: the model says p = 0.8">
  <Step title="The truth is yes (y = 1)">
    Only the first term is on: $L = -\ln 0.8 = \ln\frac{5}{4} = \ln 5 - 2\ln 2 \approx 1.609 - 1.386 = 0.22$. Small: the model gave 80% to what happened.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The truth is no (y = 0)">
    Only the second term is on: $L = -\ln(1 - 0.8) = -\ln 0.2 = \ln 5 \approx 1.61$, about seven times bigger: the model gave only 20% to what happened.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The pattern">
    $L = -\ln(\text{probability given to the truth})$: 0 for a perfect 100%, and it shoots up as that probability heads to 0. (You only need $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ and $\ln 10 \approx 2.303$; then $\ln 5 = \ln 10 - \ln 2$.)
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Multi-class classification: K logits and a softmax

Exactly one of $K$ classes is correct, so the head has $K$ logits and a softmax shares 100% probability between them. You met softmax and <Term def="For one example, minus the natural log of the probability the model gave to the true class: L = −ln p(true class).">cross-entropy</Term> in [softmax and multi-class](#/learn/classification/softmax-and-multiclass): $L = -\ln p_{\text{true class}}$.

### Multi-label classification: K logits, K separate sigmoids

As in [Softmax, cross-entropy and multi-class problems](#/learn/classification/softmax-and-multiclass), when several labels can be true at once (a food photo can show chicken rice **and** satay), each of the $K$ logits gets its **own** sigmoid and its own binary cross-entropy, and the loss is their average. The target is a list of 0s and 1s, such as $(1, 1, 0, 0, 0)$, and the probabilities need **not** add up to 1: a softmax would force chicken rice and satay to share one 100%.

<KeyIdea>

Match the head to the answer: a **number** gets no activation and MSE (or MAE); **one yes/no** gets one logit, a sigmoid and binary cross-entropy; **one of K** gets K logits, a softmax and cross-entropy; **several yes/nos** get K logits, a sigmoid on each and binary cross-entropy on each.

</KeyIdea>

## Why PyTorch wants raw logits

The two PyTorch classification losses, `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` and `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss`, take the **logits** and apply the softmax or sigmoid **inside** the loss, so the model should end with a plain `nn.Linear` layer. There are two good reasons.

**1. Numerical stability.** In PyTorch's default 32-bit floats, $\sigma(20)$ rounds to exactly $1.0$, so a hand-made loss that needs $\ln(1 - p)$ ends up taking $\ln 0$. Computing the loss straight from the logit avoids that subtraction, using the identities $-\ln \sigma(z) = \ln(1 + e^{-z})$ and, since $1 - \sigma(z) = \sigma(-z)$, $-\ln(1 - \sigma(z)) = \ln(1 + e^{z})$:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

z = torch.tensor([20.0])    # a very confident logit...
y = torch.tensor([0.0])     # ...but the true label is 0

p = torch.sigmoid(z)
print(p)                             # exactly 1.0 in float32
print(nn.BCELoss()(p, y))            # needs log(1 - p) = log(0)
print(nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()(z, y))  # works from the logit
```

```text
tensor([1.])
tensor(100.)
tensor(20.)
```

`nn.BCELoss` cannot take $\ln 0 = -\infty$, so PyTorch clamps the log at $-100$ and reports a meaningless 100. `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` never forms $1 - p$ and gets the true loss, $\ln(1 + e^{20}) \approx 20$.

`CrossEntropyLoss` uses a similar trick for the softmax, so even logits like 1,000 cannot overflow.

<Reveal label="How? (the log-sum-exp trick)">

The log of a softmax is $\ln p_k = z_k - \ln\sum_j e^{z_j}$. Subtracting the largest logit $m$ from every logit first does not change the answer, $\ln p_k = (z_k - m) - \ln\sum_j e^{z_j - m}$, but now every exponent is at most 0, so no $e^{\dots}$ is bigger than 1.

</Reveal>

**2. A simple, strong gradient.** For both pairings, sigmoid with binary cross-entropy and softmax with cross-entropy, the slope of the loss with respect to each logit is just

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_k} = p_k - y_k
$$

"predicted probability minus target". Wrong and confident means a big push; right means almost no push. (You derived the sigmoid case in [logistic regression](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression); [the network-from-scratch lesson](#/learn/backpropagation/network-from-scratch) derives the softmax case.)

When you **do** want probabilities, for example to show "93% spam", apply `torch.sigmoid` or `torch.softmax` to the logits after the model. To pick the predicted class you do not even need that: softmax never changes which logit is largest, so `logits.argmax(dim=1)` gives the same answer.

The classic way to get this wrong, which you met in [Softmax, cross-entropy and multi-class problems](#/learn/classification/softmax-and-multiclass), is to add your own softmax at the end of the model and then use `nn.CrossEntropyLoss`, which applies another one. Below, "push" means $\partial L / \partial z_\text{true}$, the slope that gradient descent follows: the more negative, the harder the true class's logit is pushed up.

<DoubleSoftmax />

The softmax lesson showed the bug in code: no error, just a larger loss. The visual adds the floor: with the extra softmax the loss can never go below $\ln\big(1 + \frac{K-1}{e}\big)$, about 0.55 for 3 classes and 1.46 for 10, however good the model becomes.

<Mistake title="Softmax (or sigmoid) twice">

Ending the model with `nn.Softmax` and then using `nn.CrossEntropyLoss`, or ending with `nn.Sigmoid` and then using `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss`, squashes the scores twice. Nothing crashes, but training is slow and the loss stalls above zero. With a sigmoid twice, the loss sees probabilities between 0.5 and 0.73, so a "no" example can never score below $\ln 2 \approx 0.69$, the coin-flip value. Keep the model's last layer as a plain `nn.Linear` and let the loss do the squashing.

</Mistake>

<Mistake title="Targets in the wrong shape or type">

- `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` wants **class indices**: one whole number from 0 to $K - 1$ per example.
- `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` wants **float** 0/1 targets with exactly the same shape as the logits.
- For regression, the predictions and targets must have the same shape too.

<Reveal label="PyTorch details">

- The class indices have shape `(N,)` and dtype long. `CrossEntropyLoss` also accepts float probability rows of shape `(N, K)`, so float one-hot rows work, but the integer one-hot tensor from `F.one_hot` does not; indices are simplest.
- Regression predictions of shape `(N, 1)` against targets of shape `(N,)` give a quietly wrong loss with only a warning ([the PyTorch module shows why](#/learn/pytorch/devices-and-common-errors)).

</Reveal>

</Mistake>

<ExamTip>

Remember the two equations `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` = log-softmax + negative log-likelihood, and `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` = sigmoid + binary cross-entropy (in one stable step). Here log-softmax is $\ln$ of the softmax, and negative log-likelihood is $-\ln p_\text{true class}$, which is just our cross-entropy. Questions often ask which layer to remove ("the softmax at the end of the model") or which loss to pick for multi-label data ("BCE with logits, not cross-entropy"). In the Final, a hand-written loss that takes `log` of a softmax or sigmoid is a classic source of `nan` or `inf`.

</ExamTip>

## Why not use MSE for classification?

You saw in [logistic regression](#/learn/classification/logistic-regression) that squared error with a sigmoid gives a push of $2(p-y)p(1-p)$; here is what that means for a network:

<SaturationRace />

The extra factor is the sigmoid's slope $\sigma'(z) = p(1 - p)$, which is almost zero whenever the sigmoid is <Term def="A sigmoid or tanh is saturated when its input is far from 0: the output is pinned near its limit and its slope is nearly 0, so gradients through it are tiny.">saturated</Term>, that is, whenever the model is very sure. At the visual's starting point the model is confidently **wrong**: $y = 1$ but $z = -6$, so $p \approx 0.0025$. Binary cross-entropy pushes with $p - 1 \approx -1.00$; squared error pushes with only $2(-0.9975)(0.0025)(0.9975) \approx -0.005$, about 200 times weaker. The more wrong the model, the less it learns: exactly backwards. And every weight in the network learns through this one push at the output, so the whole network crawls, not just the last layer. Mean absolute error on a sigmoid has the same problem: its push is $\pm p(1 - p)$, which also vanishes when the model is confidently wrong, and it ignores how wrong the prediction is.

You may also meet **hinge loss**, $\max(0, 1 - yz)$ with labels $\pm 1$: the margin loss of support vector machines. It gives no probabilities, so for a sigmoid head binary cross-entropy is the standard choice.

## The starting loss tells you something

Recall the sanity check from [Softmax, cross-entropy and multi-class problems](#/learn/classification/softmax-and-multiclass): before training, the weights are small random numbers, so the logits are all close to 0, softmax gives every class about $1/K$, and the first loss is about $-\ln\frac{1}{K} = \ln K$. For 2 classes that is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ (the coin-flip line in the playground of the previous lesson, [Multi-layer perceptrons](#/learn/neural-networks/multilayer-perceptrons)); for 10 classes it is $\ln 10 \approx 2.303$.

<BaselineLossCheck />

Check it on a real, untrained PyTorch network with 10 classes:

```python
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(784, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 10))

x = torch.rand(256, 784)             # a batch of 256 fake "images"
y = torch.randint(0, 10, (256,))     # labels: class indices 0..9

loss = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()(model(x), y)
print(f"first loss {loss.item():.3f}   ln 10 = {math.log(10):.3f}")
```

```text
first loss 2.316   ln 10 = 2.303
```

<Steps title="Reading the first loss of a 10-class model">
  <Step title="About 2.3">
    Healthy: the model is unsure, exactly as an untrained model should be.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Much higher, say 60">
    The model is confidently wrong before it has learned anything: its logits are huge. Suspect inputs that were never normalised (feed the network above raw pixel values from 0 to 255 instead of 0 to 1 and its first loss is about 62) or an output layer that starts with weights far too big.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Much lower, say 0.01">
    Impossible for an honest untrained model before any training. Suspect the loss itself, for example arguments passed in the wrong order or the loss computed on the wrong tensor. (If instead the loss crashes to almost 0 within a few steps, suspect a leak: the answer is hidden in the inputs.)
  </Step>
  <Step title="Stuck above 1.46">
    It started at 2.3 but never falls below about 1.46: the double-softmax bug. Equal logits stay equal after a second softmax, so it starts at $\ln K$ like a healthy model, but the floor $\ln(1 + 9/e) \approx 1.46$ gives it away.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Takeaways>

- Logits are the last layer's raw outputs. Regression: no activation, MSE or MAE. Binary: 1 logit, sigmoid, binary cross-entropy. Multi-class: K logits, softmax, cross-entropy. Multi-label: K logits, a sigmoid and binary cross-entropy on each.
- `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` and `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` take raw logits and apply softmax or sigmoid inside, for numerical stability and the clean gradient $p - y$. Never add your own softmax or sigmoid before them.
- MSE on a sigmoid output learns very slowly when the model is confidently wrong, because its gradient is multiplied by $\sigma'(z) \approx 0$.
- An untrained $K$-class classifier should start with loss $\approx \ln K$ (2.30 for 10 classes). A very different first loss means a bug.

</Takeaways>
