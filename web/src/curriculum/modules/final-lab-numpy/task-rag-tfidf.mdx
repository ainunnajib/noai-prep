## The plan in six steps

1. **Tokenise** every document and the query.
2. Build the **vocabulary** from the documents.
3. Count words to get **term frequencies** (TF).
4. Weight them by **inverse document frequency** (IDF) to get TF-IDF vectors, and vectorise the query **the same way**.
5. Score every document with **cosine similarity** and keep the **top k**.
6. **Generate** `response` from the retrieved documents.

Steps 1 to 4 turn text into numbers, step 5 is the "retrieve", and step 6 is the "generate".

## Steps 1 to 4: turn the text into vectors

To <Term def="Split a text into tokens, the units a program counts. Here: lower-case words with punctuation removed.">tokenise</Term> a sentence, lower-case it and keep only runs of letters with `re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())`. That way "intelligence." and "intelligence" become the same token. Then drop <Term def="Very common words such as the, is, a, of, how and does. They appear almost everywhere, so they say nothing about what a document is about.">stop words</Term>. The **vocabulary** is the sorted list of every distinct token in the documents. Each word gets a fixed column number, so every text becomes a vector with one entry per vocabulary word.

<TFIDFTableBuilder />

Here is what the table computes, symbol by symbol. For a word (term) $t$ and a document $d$, with $N$ documents in total:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{tf}(t, d) &= \text{number of times } t \text{ occurs in } d \\
\mathrm{idf}(t) &= \ln\frac{1 + N}{1 + \mathrm{df}(t)} + 1 \\
w_{t,d} &= \mathrm{tf}(t, d) \times \mathrm{idf}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

- $\mathrm{tf}(t, d)$, the <Term def="How often a word occurs in one document. We use the raw count.">term frequency</Term>, says how much document $d$ uses word $t$.
- $\mathrm{df}(t)$, the <Term def="The number of documents that contain the word at least once.">document frequency</Term>, counts the **documents** that contain $t$ at least once, not the total number of occurrences.
- $\frac{1+N}{1+\mathrm{df}}$ is big for a rare word and equals 1 for a word found in every document. The $+1$ inside acts as if one extra document contained every word, so we never divide by zero. The $\ln$ squashes the ratio, and the final $+1$ means even the most common word keeps a small weight. This is scikit-learn's default, called the <Term def="An IDF formula with +1 added to N and to df (as if one extra document contained every word) and +1 added at the end, so it never divides by zero and is never 0.">smoothed IDF</Term>.
- $w_{t,d}$ is the TF-IDF weight: **how much this document uses the word × how rare the word is**.

This is not the plain $\mathrm{idf} = \log(N/\mathrm{df})$ from the [TF-IDF lesson](#/learn/text-as-data/bag-of-words-and-tfidf), where documents 2 and 4 of this task scored 0.453 and 0.310 (here they score 0.5685 and 0.4643). The +1s change the numbers but not which two documents win, and they stop a word found in every document from vanishing.

With $N = 5$: "learning" is in 3 documents, so $\mathrm{idf} = \ln\frac{6}{4} + 1 \approx 1.41$. "machine" is in 2, so $\ln\frac{6}{3} + 1 \approx 1.69$. A word found in just one document, such as "data", gets $\ln\frac{6}{2} + 1 \approx 2.10$.

<KeyIdea>

The query must live in the **same vector space** as the documents: same tokeniser, same vocabulary (same column numbers) and the same IDF values. Query words that appear in no document, such as "work", have no column and are simply ignored.

</KeyIdea>

## Step 5: score the documents and keep the top k

Two vectors point the same way when they use the same words in the same proportions. <Term def="The cosine of the angle between two vectors: their dot product divided by the product of their lengths.">Cosine similarity</Term> measures exactly that:

$$
\cos(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{d} \rVert} = \frac{\sum_j q_j d_j}{\sqrt{\sum_j q_j^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_j d_j^2}}
$$

- $\mathbf{q}$ is the query vector and $\mathbf{d}$ a document vector; $q_j$ and $d_j$ are their weights for vocabulary word number $j$.
- The top line, the <Term def="Multiply two vectors entry by entry and add up the products.">dot product</Term>, only collects points from words that **both** vectors contain.
- The bottom line divides by both lengths, so a long document cannot win just by containing many words. Only the **direction**, its mix of words, counts.

TF-IDF weights are never negative, so here the score runs from 0 (no shared words) to 1 (the same mix). The notebook says cosine ranges "from 0 (completely different) to 1". That is true for these vectors, but cosine in general can be as low as $-1$.

Python counts from 0, so index 1 is document 2. The [RAG retriever lesson](#/learn/prompting-and-rag/rag-from-scratch) covers the sort direction and ties in full.

<Mistake title="Five ways to lose marks on this task">

1. **Punctuation stays attached.** With `.split()`, `"learning."` never matches `"learning"`. Use `re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())`.
2. **A separate vocabulary or IDF for the query.** If you build the query's vector from its own word list, column 5 of the query no longer means the same word as column 5 of a document, and the dot product multiplies unrelated words.
3. **Ascending sort.** `np.argsort(scores)[:2]` returns the two **worst** documents. Reverse it or sort `-scores`.
4. **Indices instead of text.** `top_documents` must hold the sentences (`documents[i]`), not the numbers 1 and 3, or the print cell shows `- 1` and `- 3`.
5. **Missing names.** Call the variables exactly `top_documents` and `response`, define them in your cell, and make sure the cell has run before the protected print cell.

</Mistake>
